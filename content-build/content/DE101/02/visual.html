1. The Product Grid

A join is two things you already know, stacked. Pair every row with every row (Tables, Rows and Keys), then keep the pairs whose predicate is TRUE — and only TRUE (NULL Semantics and Three-Valued Logic). Tap any cell to see the comparison it ran. Like a seating chart: every guest against every table, then cross out the pairs that do not work.

Flip order 12's foreign key and watch its whole row of the grid change verdict.

No engine literally builds this grid — it hashes one side, or walks an index. But the meaning of every join is exactly this, and reasoning from it never lies: a left row with no TRUE cell in its row disappears, and a left row with three TRUE cells comes back three times.

2. Four Ways To Keep

Same two tables, same ON condition. The only question each join type answers differently is which unmatched rows do we keep, padded with NULL? Like a class photo: everyone, only the pairs, or one side plus whoever they came with.

RIGHT JOIN is legal and almost never written: a RIGHT JOIN b is b LEFT JOIN a with the tables swapped, and a pipeline reads better when the table you are preserving comes first. FULL JOIN earns its keep in one place — reconciliation. Full-join yesterday's export to today's on the key, and the NULL-on-one-side rows are exactly the adds and the deletes.

3. Where The LEFT Dies

One predicate, o.amount > 100, and three places to put it. Move it and watch user 2 — the user with no orders — live or die. Like a guest list versus a bouncer: one decides who counts as your plus-one, the other throws people out after they are already inside.

For an inner join these three would be interchangeable, and that is exactly why the habit of putting join keys in ON and business filters in WHERE is worth keeping: it is free until the day you write LEFT, and then it is the whole difference between preserved and deleted.

4. Grain Keeper

Two questions that need no columns from the other table — only whether a match exists. Five ways to ask, and they do not agree. Watch the row count and the output grain, not just the ids. Like asking "does this student have any library books?" — you want a yes, not a stack of books.

Pick the form by the grain you want out. Need columns from the other side, or one row per match? Join. Need each left row at most once, filtered by existence? EXISTS / NOT EXISTS. A DISTINCT that "fixed" a count is not a fix — it is a flag that the grain went wrong upstream, and it can just as easily be hiding a second duplication you have not found yet.

5. Fan-out Multiplier

One user, joined to two one-to-many tables in the same query. Drag each side and watch the row count — and the revenue total — go somewhere nobody asked for. Like multiplying two guest lists together instead of adding them.

Read every join in a query with one question: what can this multiply? One-to-one multiplies nothing. One-to-many multiplies the one side. Many-to-many multiplies both, and the query almost certainly needs restructuring. Making that question reflexive is most of what being good at joins means in practice.
