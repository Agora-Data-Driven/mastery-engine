1. Grain Changer

Grouping is the opposite move to a join. Join Fan-out multiplied rows; GROUP BY collapses them. Pick the grouping keys and watch eight rows become the grain you asked for — including the one place NULL Semantics behaves differently here than in a join. Like sorting laundry into piles: you choose the piles, and then each pile answers as one.

Grain is a decision, not a detail. "One row per document" and "one row per document-chunk" differ by one column in GROUP BY, and the second one has not collapsed anything. Say the grain in words before you write the clause.

2. Aggregates Meet NULL

One group, four requests, one of them never measured. Six aggregates walk over the same four rows and disagree about that missing value. Tap each and watch which rows it actually reads. Like a class register versus a test-score average: absent students are counted in one and not the other.

Deciding what a missing value means is a modelling choice, not a formatting one. COALESCE(latency_ms, 0) turns "never measured" into a very fast request and drags the average down. AVG's default — exclude the unknown — is usually the honest answer, and it is the one you get for free.

3. Before Or After

Two predicates, one collapse between them. Put each one on the wrong side and the query either breaks or — much worse — quietly measures a different population. Like weighing ingredients before you bake versus judging the cake afterwards: you cannot swap those two.

status = 'success'
AVG(latency_ms) > 500

A predicate on a grouping key is the one that may legally sit in either clause — and it still belongs in WHERE, because discarding rows before grouping means less shuffle and less aggregate state. Optimisers often push it down for you. Clear SQL should not depend on that.

4. The Set Problem

Three counts over the same four rows give three different numbers — and only one of them changes what the engine has to hold in memory. Then the trap that ships in real dashboards: distinct counts do not add up. Like counting tickets sold versus counting the people who came: the second one needs you to remember every face.

Distinct counts do not add up

Before reaching for DISTINCT, diagnose why the duplicates exist. If a join fanned each judgment out across several logs, COUNT(DISTINCT query_id) will look right while SUM(cost) beside it stays wrong — the DISTINCT hid the row multiplication instead of repairing it. When the duplication is real (a user with many events), distinct is the intended statistic.

5. The Arbitrary Column

A group holds many rows. Ask it for a column that has not been collapsed and you are asking a question with no answer — so the engine either refuses or invents one. Like asking a committee for "their" phone number.

SELECT query_id, document_id, MAX(score) FROM ranked_results GROUP BY query_id;

Four repairs, by intent

The rule that covers every case: every selected column must have a one-value-per-output-group explanation. A key constraint, an explicit aggregate, or membership in the GROUP BY. "It happens to look constant" is not one — and a functional dependency you were relying on can be destroyed by a dimension join that duplicates the supposedly unique key.
