1. Collapse Or Attach

Same partitioning idea, opposite effect on your rows. GROUP BY Mechanics buys a per-group number by destroying the rows; OVER (PARTITION BY …) computes the same number and hands it back to every row. Flip between them and watch the row count. Like a class average written on the board versus written on each student's paper.

Windows are evaluated after GROUP BY and HAVING and before the final ORDER BY, so a window can sit on top of grouped output: SUM(COUNT(*)) OVER () beside COUNT(*) … GROUP BY run_date adds up the grouped rows without collapsing them a second time. That position in the logical order is where every placement rule in this lesson comes from.

2. Three Ways To Rank

Four documents, two of them tied. The three ranking functions agree everywhere except at that tie — and the disagreement is the whole reason there are three. Like a podium: do joint silvers push the next athlete to third or to fourth?

Ranking order and display order are separate things. The ORDER BY inside OVER decides the rank; the query's own final ORDER BY decides what the reader sees. A result can rank by score and display by query id, and neither controls the other.

3. The Frame Window

Adding ORDER BY to an aggregate window makes it order-sensitive, and then a frame decides which ordered rows feed each answer. Pick a frame, then tap any row to see the rows it is actually reading. Like a sliding window on a train: what you see depends on where the frame starts and stops, not on how long the train is.

"Last 100 requests" is inherently a ROWS question. "The previous 24 hours" is not — a fixed row count is wrong the moment the event rate varies, and needs a time RANGE, a join, or a pre-bucketed time table. Frames never reduce cardinality, but a broad one costs state: a bounded trailing frame streams with a small buffer, while unbounded or forward-looking frames make the engine hold on to more.

4. ROWS vs RANGE

Two events share a timestamp. That is all it takes for the same running total to give two different answers — and if you did not write a frame, the engine already chose one for you. Like a queue where two people arrived at the same second: do you serve them one at a time, or treat them as one arrival?

Neither is wrong. ROWS answers "through this physical event"; RANGE answers "through this ordering value." What is wrong is not saying which — because then the answer depends on whether two rows happened to share a timestamp, which is a property of the data rather than of your intent. State the frame explicitly, and add a stable tie-breaker whenever row sequence matters.

5. Defining Previous

LAG does not know about calendars. It hands you the previous row, and if Tuesday never landed, Wednesday's "yesterday" is Monday. Toggle the calendar join and watch the change column stop lying. Like flipping back one page in a diary with a page torn out.

Most wrong LAG results are not function errors — they are failures to define "previous" with enough precision. Partition at the entity whose history is independent, order by the true chronology plus a stable tie-breaker, decide whether gaps are skipped or materialised, and keep the NULL when no predecessor exists.
