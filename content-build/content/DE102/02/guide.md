**The big idea**: **Normalization and Denormalization** asked which entity a fact describes. Dimensional modelling asks the analytics version: which columns are the *measurements*, which are the *context* you slice them by, and — above all — what exactly does one row of the measurement table represent? That last one is **grain**, it must be written down before you choose a single column, and it is the decision every other modelling choice hangs from. The point is not a diagram shape. It is a stable contract about what a row means, which numbers may legitimately be added up, and how someone can ask a question without first reconstructing your operational schema. A good model makes the common analysis obvious and the incorrect analysis hard to express.

**Key concepts**

- **A fact records a measurable event, observation, or state; a dimension describes the context around it.** *Mechanism:* a fact table carries foreign keys to descriptive entities plus numeric **measures**; a dimension row carries the attributes you filter, group and label by. *Example:* `fact_order_line(date_key, customer_key, product_key, order_id, line_number, quantity, gross_amount, discount_amount)` stores a `product_key`, not the product's category, brand and size repeated on every sale. The join to `dim_product` supplies those.
- **Fact and dimension are *roles*, not properties of an entity.** A product is a dimension in sales analysis; a product-price observation is a fact in a price-history process. Start from the business process and the measurement event, then ask what context describes it. Starting from a list of source tables reproduces operational complexity instead of producing an analytical model.
- **Additivity decides what a `SUM` is allowed to mean.** **Additive**: order-line revenue sums across products, customers and dates. **Semi-additive**: a daily account balance sums across accounts *for one day*, but summing one account across days produces a meaningless stock total. **Non-additive**: a conversion rate must be recomputed from components, never summed.
- **Store the components, not the ratio.** *Example:* segment A converts 1 of 10 (10%), segment B converts 50 of 100 (50%). The combined rate is **51/110 ≈ 46.36%**, not the 30% you get by averaging the two rates. Averaging precomputed rates throws away the denominator weight, and no downstream query can put it back.
- **Three fact-table patterns, chosen by the business process.** A **transaction** fact appends one row per event. A **periodic snapshot** records state at a regular interval — inventory at daily close. An **accumulating snapshot** updates milestone columns as one process instance moves through submitted → reviewed → approved → funded. The snapshot makes cycle-time queries easy; an event history preserves every transition and repeat. Some systems keep both.
- **Surrogate keys separate warehouse identity from source identity.** The operational customer id stays as a business-key *attribute*; facts reference a generated warehouse key. That separation is what survives source-system changes, multiple sources with colliding identifiers, and — critically — historical versions: a customer whose region changes gets two dimension rows with two surrogate keys while retaining one business identity.
- **Degenerate and junk dimensions.** An order number analysts filter by, with no useful attributes of its own, lives directly on the fact — a **degenerate dimension**, and it needs no table. A **junk dimension** bundles several low-cardinality flags so the fact is not cluttered with tiny foreign keys. Clarity beats diagram tidiness in both cases.
- **Unknown relationships need explicit members, not broken keys.** A fact can arrive before its customer dimension does. Load an `Unknown` row with a reserved surrogate key and reconcile later — and keep `Not applicable`, `Unknown` and `Error` as *separate* members when the distinction affects quality reporting.
- **Grain is the exact statement of what one row represents, declared before anything else.** "One row per order line as received from the commerce source" is a grain. "Sales data by customer and product" is not: it specifies no time, no event, and does not say whether two lines for the same product are distinct rows.
- **The grain determines the valid key, and the key should be tested at build time.** If an order can repeat a product across lines, `(order_id, product_id)` is not unique and `(order_id, line_number)` may be. If corrections create versions, the grain either includes a revision number or declares that only the latest accepted state exists.
- **Mixed grain is the most damaging warehouse defect there is.** *Example:* order-level shipping cost of 12 repeated on every line of a three-line order, sitting beside line-level revenue. `SUM(shipping_cost)` returns **36**. Nothing errors, and the number is wrong by a factor that differs per order. Put order-level measures in an order fact, allocate them by a documented rule, or store them on exactly one designated line with the semantics written down.
- **Grain also defines which joins are safe** — the fan-out rule, arriving in modelling clothes. Joining an order-line fact to a table with several promotions per order is a many-to-many expansion: ten lines and three promotions give thirty rows and triple the revenue. A **bridge table with allocation weights**, a pre-aggregation, or a separate promotion event fact preserves the totals.
- **Late-arriving and duplicated events test the grain contract.** Pipelines should carry stable source identifiers or idempotency keys so a twice-delivered payment does not double count. When a correction arrives, the model must state whether it replaces the original, appends a compensating event, or creates a new version. Those are semantic decisions, not loading mechanics.
- **The finest practical grain buys the most analytical flexibility.** Atomic order lines roll up to orders, customers, days and categories. A table pre-aggregated to monthly category totals can never answer which customer bought the item — the dimension was deleted at write time. Keep atomic facts where feasible and build aggregates for performance on top.
- **A snapshot grain needs entity *and* time, and missing rows need a meaning.** "One row per account per day at end of day" keeps state from being confused with events. Then decide: does a missing row mean zero balance, entity not observed, or pipeline failure? A completeness test comparing expected active entities against observed snapshots is what makes the answer enforceable.
- **A star schema puts a central fact around relatively denormalised dimensions; a snowflake normalises the hierarchies out.** *Example:* one million products sharing 2,000 brands and 500 categories. The star repeats brand and category labels across product rows — and columnar compression makes that cost modest. The snowflake removes the repetition logically, at the price of traversing product → category on every category analysis.
- **Stars optimise usability, and that is a real engineering property.** One hop from fact to dimension means fewer chances to pick a wrong join path or accidentally multiply rows; BI tools and semantic layers expose the relationships straightforwardly; the optimiser scans only the dimension columns the query names.
- **Snowflake selectively, for governance rather than for tidiness.** A standardised geography dimension reused across customer, supplier and facility genuinely deserves its own structure. Product-to-category membership that changes independently, or supports several simultaneous categories, needs a bridge rather than copied columns.
- **Normalising a dimension does not give you history.** If a product row points at a category table whose label or parent changes **in place**, old sales silently reappear under the new hierarchy. Historical reporting needs versioning at the right dimension grain and time-aware fact keys — star or snowflake, the diagram is irrelevant to this.

**Rules to remember**

- Write the grain statement first, in words, and encode it as a uniqueness test.
- Additive sums anywhere; semi-additive sums across entities but not across time; non-additive must be recomputed from components.
- Never store a ratio without its numerator and denominator.
- One table, one grain. An order-level measure on a line-level row overcounts by the line count.
- Facts and dimensions are roles. Ask what the business process measures, not what the source tables are called.
- Surrogate keys in the fact; the operational id as a business-key attribute in the dimension.
- `Unknown` is a dimension row with a reserved key, never a broken foreign key or a `NULL`.
- Atomic grain first; aggregates are a derived performance layer, not the model.
- Star by default; snowflake where a hierarchy has independent stewardship, genuine reuse, or many-to-many structure.
- The star-versus-snowflake tradeoff is a join-cost tradeoff, and join cost is not only CPU: it includes comprehension, BI configuration, permission enforcement, and the chance of fan-out.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Averaging rates.** It is the same error in every domain — click-through rate across campaigns, precision across query classes, success rate across services. Store the counts.
- **Declaring the grain loosely and discovering it later.** "By customer and product" leaves a mixed-grain table waiting to happen, because nothing in it forbids two rows meaning the same thing.
- **Summing a semi-additive measure across time.** Adding one account's daily balances produces a number with no referent, and it looks entirely plausible on a chart.
- **Pre-aggregating to save storage.** You are not saving storage, you are deleting dimensions. The question you cannot yet imagine is the one it will block.
- **Snowflaking to reach 3NF in an analytical layer.** 3NF is a *transactional* target. In a warehouse, repeated low-cardinality attributes compress well and the extra join buys nothing but complexity — unless governance or reuse is the reason.
- **Denormalising a dimension with no maintenance plan.** Repeated attributes drift the moment two pipelines can update them. Test hierarchy consistency, key coverage, join cardinality, and fact totals before and after joins.
- **Assuming a snowflake preserves history.** In-place updates rewrite the past under either shape. History is versioning, not normalisation.
- **Inferring storage savings from row intuition.** Measure under the actual engine. Uncompressed reasoning about a columnar store is usually wrong by an order of magnitude.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Read every scenario for its grain statement first. If the question does not give one, the answer very often is that the grain is undeclared or mixed.
2. For an additivity question, ask what the measure is a *rate of* or a *stock of*. Stocks are semi-additive across time; rates are non-additive everywhere.
3. When a total is inflated, find the join that expanded rows and count the multiplier — the same fan-out arithmetic you already know, now with `bridge table` as one of the answer options.
4. For star-versus-snowflake, decide on ownership and query pattern rather than on normal forms. "Analysts constantly group by it and it changes with the product" points to the star; "independently stewarded and shared across three contexts" points to the snowflake.
5. If a question describes history changing retroactively, the answer is about versioning, not about schema shape.
6. Watch for the difference between a degenerate dimension (an identifier on the fact, correct) and a missing dimension table (an oversight). The test is whether it has useful independent attributes.
