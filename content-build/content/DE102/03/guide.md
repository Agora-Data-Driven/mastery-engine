**The big idea**: **Dimensional Modeling** ended on the one thing it kept deferring — a dimension row updated in place silently rewrites the past, whether the diagram is a star or a snowflake. This lesson is the fix, and its scope is wider than it first looks. **SCD Type 2** keeps a new row per change with an effective-from and an effective-to, so a February sale still resolves to the region the customer was in *in February*. Document versioning in your corpus is **the same design** with different column names: one stable document identity, an immutable version per change, and a validity interval. The three hard parts are identical in both — make history queryable, prevent overlapping truth, and keep only the history whose future value justifies its cost.

**Key concepts**

- **Type 2 creates a new row when a tracked attribute changes; the old row stays for historical facts.** *Mechanism:* each version carries its own surrogate key, the same **business key**, an `effective_from`, an `effective_to`, and usually a current-row flag. *Example:* customer C42 is region North from Jan 1 to Mar 15, then South. The dimension holds `[Jan 1, Mar 15)` and `[Mar 15, ∞)`. A sale on Feb 10 resolves to North; a sale on Apr 2 resolves to South. Historical revenue by region stays faithful to the classification *at sale time*.
- **Half-open intervals are the convention, and the reason is mechanical.** `effective_from <= event_time < effective_to`. The end is exclusive, so two adjacent versions meet at exactly one boundary without both matching. Make both ends inclusive and an event at exactly Mar 15 joins **twice** — a silent doubling that only appears on boundary days.
- **Three invariants per business key, and they should be tests.** At most one current row; no overlapping validity intervals; deterministic ordering of versions. Gaps may be allowed or forbidden depending on the source. A window function comparing each row's `effective_to` with the next row's `effective_from` checks all of it. Also assert **positive duration** — a zero-length or reversed interval can never match a valid event and always means bad ordering or timestamp normalisation.
- **Applying a change is close-then-insert, in one transaction.** Set the old row's end to T and the new row's start to T. The transaction is what prevents a moment with two current rows or none. Append-oriented warehouses do the equivalent by rebuilding the affected keys in a staged model and atomically replacing a partition.
- **Facts bind to versions early or late, and it is a real architectural choice.** **Early binding** resolves and stores the version surrogate key at load time: simple, fast queries, and revised history requires restating facts. **Late binding** keeps the business key plus the event time and joins on the validity interval: history can be corrected without touching facts, at the cost of a range join on every query. Some systems keep both, for audit and controlled restatement.
- **Late-arriving changes are the hard case, and the design must state its policy.** The warehouse learns on Apr 1 that the region actually changed on Mar 15, after March facts have loaded. Early-bound facts need restatement; late-bound facts resolve correctly the moment the new interval exists. Either is defensible; *not having decided* is not.
- **Not every attribute deserves Type 2.** Classify them: **Type 1 overwrite** for values whose old form should never be reported (a misspelled name), **Type 2 version** for historically meaningful changes (contractual tier, sales territory), and **fixed** for attributes that should not change at all without an incident.
- **Deletes need semantics too.** A source deletion may close the current interval, create an `is_deleted` version, or genuinely remove data under a legal policy. Analytical history, operational state and privacy obligations can conflict — and **a dimension model cannot convert a mandated erasure into indefinite retention by calling it history.**
- **Time zones and precision are part of the model.** A date-only source cannot order two same-day changes. A local timestamp with no offset is ambiguous at a daylight-saving transition. Preserve the source timestamp, normalise the comparison instant, and define a tie-break rule for changes sharing a time.
- **A document has a business identity and versions — the same shape.** `document_id` is the stable identity; `version_id` is the immutable version. A version row stores `effective_from`, `effective_to`, content hash, source modification time, ingestion time, parser version, content location, metadata and deletion status. Each version is *what the system believed the document to be* during an interval.
- **Content hashes prevent false versions — but they are not the whole trigger.** A crawler downloading identical normalised bytes hourly produces a stable hash, so nothing new is created. Metadata or permission changes may still deserve a version even when the body is byte-identical, so **the version trigger must cover every tracked attribute, not content alone.**
- **Chunks are children of a specific document *version*, not of the logical document.** When content changes, offsets and boundaries shift even where most text is unchanged. A chunk id must include or derive from the version and the chunking configuration. **Reusing an old chunk id for new text corrupts embedding caches, citations, and evaluation labels** — all three at once, and silently.
- **Reproducibility is the payoff.** An evaluation run on June 1 used document version V7. Store only current content and, once V8 lands, no later explanation of that run is possible. Versioned corpus snapshots reconstruct candidates, chunks, embeddings and permissions as of the run, and index manifests should record the exact version set or source snapshot.
- **Source-effective time and system-observed time are different axes.** A policy takes effect July 1 and is ingested July 3. *Effective time* answers which policy governed an event; *ingestion time* answers what the retrieval system could have known. **Bitemporal** designs keep both, which is what an audit needs to reconstruct knowledge as observed alongside truth as later corrected.
- **A parser defect is not source history.** If V7 was wrong because of your parser rather than because the document said that, create a new derived artifact under a new parser version and preserve the source version. Conflating content history with processing history destroys the ability to tell which one changed.
- **Version-aware queries must state their intent, and one helper cannot serve all three.** "Show the current policy" selects the open version. "Which policy applied to a claim on May 4?" uses effective time. "What answer did the system have on May 4?" uses ingestion or index-snapshot time. A generic `latest version` helper quietly answers the first for all three.
- **History is worth keeping when it supports a concrete obligation or decision** — financial restatement, compliance audit, model reproducibility, debugging, user-visible revision history, legal discovery, trend analysis. **List the questions first**, then choose granularity and retention.
- **Keeping everything forever is not the safe default.** It increases storage, catalog volume, query complexity, breach exposure and legal-discovery scope, and personal data may carry deletion or purpose-limitation requirements. Keeping only current values is the opposite failure: revenue reports shift silently and model behaviour cannot be investigated.
- **Match granularity to decision risk.** Daily snapshots cannot say which value applied at 10:00 versus 16:00. Event-level change logs can, and cost more. If billing uses status at midnight, daily grain is enough; access-control changes may need seconds *and* observation time. And a rapidly changing telemetry attribute is usually an event fact or a periodic snapshot, not a Type 2 dimension generating millions of tiny intervals.
- **Retention policy must reach the derived copies.** Record data class, owner, legal basis, duration, archive rule, deletion rule and exceptions — then apply it to chunks, embeddings, caches, exports and backups, not only the primary table. **A deleted document that is still reachable through an embedding has not been deleted from the product.**

**Rules to remember**

- Half-open intervals: `from <= t < to`. Inclusive-both makes boundary events match twice.
- Per business key: at most one current row, no overlaps, positive duration, deterministic order. Test all four.
- Close-then-insert in one transaction, or rebuild-and-swap the partition.
- Early binding stores the version key (fast, needs restatement). Late binding stores business key + time (revisable, range join).
- Type 1 overwrite, Type 2 version, or fixed — decide per attribute, not per table.
- Document versioning is SCD2: `document_id` is the business key, `version_id` the version, plus a validity interval.
- Chunk ids derive from the document **version** and the chunking config. Never reuse an id for new text.
- Effective time ≠ ingestion time. Keep both when an audit must reconstruct what was known when.
- Retention applies to every derived copy, or it is not retention.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Inclusive end dates.** The bug appears only on boundary days, doubles those facts, and survives every test that does not sample around a boundary timestamp. Sample there deliberately.
- **Trusting a `current_flag` with no uniqueness test.** Two current rows for one business key is the most common Type 2 defect in production, and every downstream query picks one arbitrarily.
- **Versioning a name typo.** It creates a historical version nobody will ever want to report and, worse, splits the entity's history around an event that never happened in the business.
- **Type-2-ing a fast-changing attribute.** Millions of one-minute intervals is a change log wearing a dimension's clothes. Model it as an event fact.
- **Reusing chunk ids across document versions.** Embedding caches return the old vector, citations point at text that no longer exists at that offset, and evaluation labels attach to the wrong content — three failures with one cause, none of which raises an error.
- **Treating "latest version" as the answer to every temporal question.** It answers "what is true now", never "what was true then" or "what did we know then".
- **Correcting a parser bug by editing the source version.** You have now lost the ability to tell whether the document changed or your pipeline did.
- **Writing a retention policy that stops at the primary table.** Backups, exports and vector indexes are copies, and the obligation follows the data.
- **An unowned Type 2 table.** Source teams define when a change is effective, data teams implement capture, governance defines retention and erasure, consumers define the historical questions. With no named owners it becomes an unbounded archive whose semantics nobody trusts.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For any interval question, test the boundary instant explicitly. Half-open matches once; inclusive-both matches twice; a gap matches nothing.
2. When a scenario says historical numbers changed, ask whether the dimension was overwritten (Type 1 where Type 2 was needed) or whether facts were early-bound and never restated.
3. Distinguish the two time axes before answering anything about "as of". If the question is about what the *system knew*, effective time is the wrong axis.
4. For "how much history to keep", look for the named obligation. No obligation and no decision means the correct answer is usually to overwrite.
5. For corpus questions, check whether chunk identity is tied to the document *version*. If it is not, the answer is almost always about corrupted caches, citations, or labels.
6. Watch for options that solve a governance problem with a schema change, or a schema problem with a policy. They are different layers and the distractors trade on mixing them.
