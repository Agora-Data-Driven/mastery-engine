**The big idea**: **Row vs Columnar Layout** bought you the right to read only the columns you need. Encodings and compression decide how few bytes those columns cost — and since you are billed for bytes scanned, stored and shipped, a 10× size reduction is close to a 10× cost reduction on the I/O-bound part of every query. Two layers do the work, in order: an **encoding** is a type-aware, reversible transform that exploits what the values *are*; a **codec** is a general-purpose byte compressor applied per page afterwards, exploiting whatever redundancy is left. Encodings do the intelligent work; codecs mop up. And the sort order you chose to make **Column Statistics and Predicate Pushdown** work turns out to pay a second time here — one decision, two wins.

**Key concepts**

- **Dictionary encoding targets repetition of whole values.** *Mechanism:* the writer collects the distinct values of a column chunk into a **dictionary page** stored once, and the data pages hold only small integer indexes into it, bit-packed to the minimum width — a column with $k$ distinct values needs $\lceil \log_2 k \rceil$ bits per row. *Example:* a `country` column with 200 distinct names needs 8 bits per value; the raw strings at ~10 bytes cost 80 bits. **10× before the codec touches anything.**
- **Run-length encoding targets *consecutive* repetition.** Store the value once with a count: a million-row `status` chunk that is 600,000 `ok` then 400,000 `error` becomes two (value, run-length) pairs — bytes instead of megabytes.
- **Parquet's workhorse is the RLE/bit-packing hybrid, and it is applied to the *indexes*.** Long runs are run-length encoded; stretches without runs fall back to bit-packed literals. Crucially the hybrid runs over the **dictionary indexes themselves** (and over the definition/repetition levels that track nulls and nesting). So a low-cardinality column that is also **sorted** gets both wins multiplied: tiny indexes, and long runs of the same index collapsing to almost nothing.
- **Worked numbers.** A 1,000,000-row `status` chunk of 8-byte strings is **8 MB** raw. Dictionary-encoded with 4 distinct values, 2-bit indexes give about **250 KB** plus a 32-byte dictionary. Sorted so the values form 4 runs, RLE represents it in **a few dozen bytes**. Same information, five orders of magnitude apart, purely from exploiting structure.
- **Cardinality is the judgment.** Dictionary encoding pays when distinct values are few relative to row count and fails when they are not. Writers cap the dictionary page (~1 MB by default), and when a chunk's dictionary overflows that cap the writer **falls back to plain encoding mid-chunk**. A UUID or free-text column therefore gains nothing and bloats files that expected a win — and *a sudden jump in file size after a schema change is often exactly this fallback triggering*.
- **Sorted numerics get delta encoding.** `DELTA_BINARY_PACKED` stores successive differences rather than absolute values, shrinking magnitudes dramatically on timestamps and auto-incrementing ids. Hold that thought — it is the postings trick, exactly.
- **Honest nulls compress better than sentinels.** Parquet represents nulls through **definition levels**, which are themselves RLE/bit-packed, so a nullable low-cardinality column compresses nearly as well as a required one. Sentinel values like `''` or `0` become ordinary frequent dictionary entries *and* blur "absent" against "zero" in the data itself. The semantics and the compression profile stay clean together.
- **After encoding, each page is optionally compressed by a codec that sees only bytes.** As of early 2026: **Snappy** and **LZ4** — modest ratio, very fast both ways, the long-time default for hot data. **Zstd** — good and tunable, fast at low levels, the modern default; archival at high levels. **Gzip** — good ratio, slow both directions, legacy and cold data. **Uncompressed** — for already-tiny or incompressible data.
- **The asymmetry decides the codec: analytical data is write-once, read-many.** A file compressed once may be decompressed thousands of times, so **decompression speed matters more than compression speed** — which is why Zstd, fast to decompress even at high levels, dominates Gzip, slow in both directions at similar ratios.
- **Whether heavier compression helps depends on where the bottleneck is.** *Example:* a scan moves 10 GB of encoded pages over an effective 200 MB/s per-worker path. Snappy at 2.0× ships 5 GB in 25 s; Zstd level 3 at 2.5× ships 4 GB in 20 s — and if a core decompresses at 800 MB/s or better, decompression overlaps the transfer and the heavier codec is a straight win. The scan is **I/O-bound**, so buying bytes with CPU is cheap. Put the same data on local NVMe at 3 GB/s and the scan becomes **CPU-bound**: now the lighter codec, or a lower Zstd level, wins because decompression is the critical path.
- **Codecs compress *encoded* pages, so measured ratios depend on what the encoding left behind.** A well-dictionary-encoded, RLE-compressed column is already near its entropy floor. If a benchmark shows Zstd level 19 barely beating Snappy on some column, the encoding already did the work — do not pay level-19 CPU for a few percent.
- **Choose per temperature, and Parquet lets you choose per column.** Hot, interactively queried tables want Snappy or low-level Zstd; cold archival partitions tolerate high Zstd levels where a one-time cost amortises over years of storage. And one bulky text column may deserve heavier treatment than the numeric columns beside it.
- **Postings compression is the same playbook.** A term's postings are sorted ascending doc IDs; they are transformed into **gaps** (deltas), which are small positive integers, then stored in few bits by variable-byte or bit-packing schemes. `[1000, 1003, 1007, 1012]` becomes `[1000, 3, 4, 5]` — after the first, each fits in 3 bits instead of 32.
- **One principle underneath both: apply a cheap reversible transform that exposes the data's low entropy, then spend only as many bits as the transformed values need.** `DELTA_BINARY_PACKED` on a sorted timestamp column is literally gap encoding. Dictionary encoding maps unbounded values into a dense integer space and bit-packs. RLE collapses consecutive repetition. **Sortedness is the common fuel:** postings compress because doc IDs are sorted; Parquet columns prune *and* compress because you sorted them at write time.
- **The distribution insight transfers exactly.** A frequent term's postings are *dense* in doc-ID space, so its gaps are small and it costs few bits per document; a rare term's sparse postings have large gaps and cost more per entry. The tabular analogue: a **low-cardinality column** (values recur constantly, like a dense postings list) compresses to almost nothing, while a **high-cardinality column** (every value distinct, like maximally sparse postings) resists every trick in this lesson.
- **Where the two genuinely differ is access pattern, and it explains each side's engineering.** A search engine decodes postings *during scoring*: BM25 traversal walks a list sequentially, intersecting and skipping via skip pointers, so every nanosecond of decode latency is on the query's critical path — postings formats therefore favour schemes that decode at RAM speed and support jumping into the middle of a list. Parquet readers decompress a **whole page at a time** into a vectorized batch; random access within a page is not a goal and per-page cost amortises over thousands of values.
- **And they converge on the same macro-judgment.** Because decoding sits on the read path in both, both ecosystems favour light, fast codecs for anything queried frequently. An index that decompresses slowly is a slow search engine; a lake that decompresses slowly is an expensive warehouse.

**Rules to remember**

- Encoding first (type-aware), codec second (bytes only). Encodings do the intelligent work.
- Dictionary needs $\lceil \log_2 k \rceil$ bits per value. Low cardinality wins; high cardinality falls back to plain mid-chunk.
- RLE runs over the dictionary indexes and over the null/nesting levels, not just raw values.
- Sorted + low cardinality multiplies both wins. Sort order pays twice: pruning *and* compression.
- Delta encoding on sorted numerics = gap encoding on sorted doc IDs. Same idea, different vocabulary.
- Write-once read-many means **decompression speed dominates**.
- Heavier codec wins when I/O-bound; lighter codec wins when CPU-bound. Find the bottleneck first.
- A codec's measured ratio reflects what the encoding left behind, not the raw data.
- Nulls via definition levels compress; sentinel values do not, and they damage the semantics too.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Expecting dictionary encoding to help a UUID column.** It cannot: every value is distinct, the dictionary overflows, the writer silently falls back, and the file gets *bigger* than the estimate.
- **Diagnosing a sudden file-size jump as a data change.** Check whether a schema or cardinality change tripped the dictionary fallback. The bytes moved, the row count did not.
- **Reaching for a higher compression level to fix a slow scan.** If the scan is CPU-bound you have just made it slower. Measure where the time goes before turning the dial.
- **Benchmarking codecs on raw data.** They compress *encoded* pages, so a benchmark that skips the encoding layer measures something that never happens in production.
- **Using Gzip because "it compresses well".** At similar ratios Zstd decompresses far faster, and decompression is the side you pay for on every single read.
- **Encoding "missing" as `0` or `''`.** It inflates the dictionary with a frequent bogus value and destroys the distinction between absent and zero — two costs for one shortcut.
- **Treating compression as separate from clustering.** Unsorted data defeats RLE the same way it defeats min/max pruning, and both failures look like "the format underperformed".
- **Applying one codec to a whole table.** A bulky text column and a two-value flag column have completely different profiles, and Parquet lets you set the codec per column.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Distinguish encoding from codec before anything else. A question about *cardinality* or *sortedness* is an encoding question; a question about CPU or ratio dials is a codec question.
2. For sizing questions, compute the bit width: $\lceil \log_2 k \rceil$ per value, plus the dictionary, and then ask whether the chunk is sorted enough for RLE to collapse runs.
3. When a scenario names the bottleneck — network, object storage, local NVMe — the codec answer follows directly. I/O-bound buys bytes with CPU; CPU-bound does the opposite.
4. Remember the read/write asymmetry. Any option that optimises compression *speed* for analytical data is usually the distractor.
5. For the retrieval bridge, look for the shared transform: sorted values → small deltas → few bits. If a question contrasts postings with Parquet, the honest difference is *access pattern*, not the compression idea.
6. Watch for claims that a codec fixed something an encoding should have. Near the entropy floor, extra levels buy percent, not multiples.
