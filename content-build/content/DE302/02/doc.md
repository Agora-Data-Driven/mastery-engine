BigQuery is where the decision matrix from the last lesson sends every OLAP workload, and it is the gravitational center of the Professional Data Engineer exam: the warehouse your pipelines land in, the substrate the AI layer queries, and the line item that dominates the bill. Understanding its internals is not trivia — partition pruning, slot economics, and the columnar engine are the difference between a query that costs cents and the same answer costing hundreds of dollars. This lesson covers how BigQuery physically executes queries and the two pricing models that monetize that execution.

## Storage-Compute Separation and the Columnar Engine

BigQuery is not a database server; it is two independent planes joined by a network. The **storage plane** keeps table data in Colossus, Google's distributed file system, encoded in a proprietary columnar format (Capacitor). The **compute plane** is Dremel, a multi-tenant pool of execution workers whose unit of capacity is the **slot** — one slot is roughly a virtual CPU's worth of compute with attached memory. When you run a query, BigQuery rents you slots for its duration, workers read the needed data from Colossus over Google's petabit-scale internal network (Jupiter), and when the query finishes the slots return to the pool. Nothing is provisioned per table: storage scales by adding bytes, compute scales by adding slots, and the two never contend.

This separation is the same architectural idea you met in AlloyDB's disaggregated storage, taken to its extreme. The consequences define BigQuery's behavior. Storage is cheap and passive — you can hold petabytes while paying nothing for compute until someone queries. Multiple workloads (ETL, dashboards, ad-hoc analysis) can hit the same tables simultaneously with *separate* compute allocations, so an analyst's runaway query need not starve the nightly pipeline. Loading data is free of compute charges, and there are no indexes to build or vacuum.

The **columnar engine** explains the read path. Capacitor stores each column's values contiguously (with run-length and dictionary encoding compressing repetitive values), rather than storing rows contiguously as OLTP engines do. `SELECT user_id, amount FROM orders` therefore reads exactly two columns' bytes and skips the other thirty — an OLAP scan touches a tiny fraction of the table's bytes, and similar values compress dramatically better side by side. This is also why `SELECT *` is the cardinal sin: it forces every column off disk and, under on-demand pricing, bills for all of them.

Execution itself is a tree: the query decomposes into **stages**, each executed by many parallel workers, with intermediate results passed between stages through a distributed in-memory **shuffle** tier. A `GROUP BY user_id` over 10 billion rows means stage one scans and partially aggregates, the shuffle redistributes partials by key, and stage two merges — recall map-reduce style parallelism, but with slots dynamically reassigned between stages. Failure mode to know: a heavily skewed key (one user with half the events) concentrates one worker's shuffle partition, and the query slows or spills regardless of how many slots you own. Judgment: storage-compute separation makes BigQuery elastically brilliant at scans and joins over huge tables, and structurally wrong for OLTP-style single-row updates — DML exists but each statement is a distributed job, not an indexed write.

## Partitioning and Clustering in BigQuery

Both features exist to let the engine *skip bytes*, which is the entire game in a system that bills and parallelizes by bytes scanned.

**Partitioning** splits a table into physically separate segments by one criterion: a `DATE`/`TIMESTAMP`/`DATETIME` column (hourly, daily, monthly, or yearly granularity), an integer-range column, or ingestion time (the pseudo-column `_PARTITIONTIME` when no natural column exists). A filter on the partitioning column lets the planner eliminate — **prune** — entire partitions before any slot reads a byte. Worked numbers: a 730 GB events table partitioned daily over two years holds ~1 GB per day. `WHERE event_date BETWEEN '2026-07-01' AND '2026-07-07'` scans ~7 GB instead of 730 GB — a 99% reduction in both on-demand cost and slot-time. Pruning requires the filter to be resolvable at planning time: comparing the partition column to a constant or parameter prunes; wrapping it in a function of other columns, or joining it to another table's values, generally does not. Two operational levers ride on partitions: `require_partition_filter` rejects queries that omit the filter (protecting the budget from an accidental full scan), and **partition expiration** auto-deletes partitions older than N days — lifecycle management inside the warehouse, exactly analogous to the GCS lifecycle rules of Lesson 01. Tables cap out around 10,000 partitions, which is why partitioning by a high-cardinality column like `user_id` is wrong by construction.

**Clustering** sorts data *within* the table (or within each partition) by up to four columns, storing it in blocks whose min/max metadata the engine consults to skip blocks that cannot match a filter. It handles what partitioning cannot: high-cardinality columns (`customer_id`), multiple columns, and prefix patterns — filters benefit in cluster-column order, like a phone book sorted by last name then first name: a filter on `customer_id` alone (the first cluster key) prunes well; a filter only on the second key helps far less. BigQuery **re-clusters automatically and free** in the background as streaming inserts and loads dirty the sort order. One billing nuance: because block pruning is decided at run time, the on-demand cost estimate (dry-run bytes) for a clustered table is an *upper bound* — you may be billed less than the estimate, whereas partition pruning is reflected up front.

The standard design stacks them: **partition by date, cluster by the columns your WHERE clauses actually use** (e.g., `PARTITION BY DATE(event_ts) CLUSTER BY customer_id, event_type`). Judgment layer: partition when you need guaranteed, estimable pruning, per-segment lifecycle, or filter enforcement; cluster when cardinality is high or multiple filter columns matter; skip both for small tables (under ~1 GB the metadata overhead outweighs skipping — many tiny partitions can actually slow queries). If your retrieval-corpus table is filtered by `tenant_id` on every query, clustering on `tenant_id` is the difference between scanning the tenant's blocks and scanning every tenant's bytes on every request.

## Editions, Reservations, and Slot Autoscaling

Capacity-based compute is sold through **Editions** — three tiers of the same engine with widening feature sets: **Standard** (basic ad-hoc analytics; no commitments), **Enterprise** (the production tier: materialized-view smart tuning, BigQuery ML, fine-grained security features), and **Enterprise Plus** (adds the compliance ceiling: multi-region disaster recovery, higher assurance controls). The exam expects tier-matching at this level of granularity, not feature-list recall.

The machinery underneath has three parts. A **reservation** is a named pool of slots you create in an admin project and then **assign** to projects, folders, or the whole org — queries from assigned projects draw slots from their reservation instead of on-demand. A **commitment** is optional prepayment (one- or three-year) for some number of slots at a discounted rate. **Autoscaling** makes reservations elastic: you set a **baseline** (slots always allocated, always billed) and a **max**; BigQuery adds slots in increments between baseline and max as queued work demands, billing autoscaled slots per second only while they are provisioned. A baseline of 0 with max 400 is legitimate: you pay nothing at idle, and burst capacity appears when queries arrive.

Why this exists: slots are the scarce resource under capacity pricing, and reservations are how you *isolate workloads from each other* — the concern raised in Lesson 01 about analytics starving production, solved organizationally. Worked example: create `etl-reservation` (baseline 500, for the SLA-bound nightly pipelines), `bi-reservation` (baseline 100, max 300, for dashboards), and `adhoc-reservation` (baseline 0, max 200, for data scientists). The data scientists' runaway Monday queries can exhaust their own 200 slots and queue — the ETL pipelines never notice. Idle slots are not wasted: **idle-slot sharing** lets other reservations in the same admin project borrow unused baseline/committed slots, reclaimed instantly when the owner needs them.

Judgment: a higher baseline buys latency consistency (slots are pre-warmed) at the cost of paying while idle; baseline-0 autoscaling buys economy at the cost of brief scale-up lag and per-second autoscale billing that can exceed a commitment's rate if sustained. Experts set baselines from the P50 of sustained usage and let autoscaling absorb the P95.

## On-Demand vs Capacity Pricing: Estimating Cost

BigQuery bills storage and compute separately; the strategic choice is the compute model.

**On-demand** bills per byte *processed*: roughly $6.25 per TiB scanned (early-2026 list price; the first 1 TiB per month is free), with a 10 MB minimum per table referenced. "Processed" means the bytes of the referenced columns in the partitions/blocks actually read — the columnar engine and pruning from the previous sections are literally your pricing controls. Compute capacity arrives as a transient pool (historically up to ~2,000 slots per project) with no guarantee. **Capacity** bills per **slot-hour** through your Editions reservations: cost is a function of provisioned compute-time, *not* of bytes — a query scanning 50 TB costs nothing marginal beyond the slots it occupies.

Estimating on-demand cost is mechanical and exam-favored. The **dry run** (`bq query --dry_run`, or the console's estimate) returns bytes that would be processed without executing. Worked example: a 2 TB table, 40 equal columns; a query selecting 4 columns over a daily-partitioned table while filtering to 30 of 365 days processes roughly 2 TB × (4/40) × (30/365) ≈ 16.4 GB → 0.016 TiB × $6.25 ≈ **$0.10**. The same query as `SELECT *` without a date filter: 2 TiB → **$12.50**, a 125× ratio produced entirely by column selection and pruning. Remember the traps: `LIMIT 10` does *not* reduce bytes scanned (the scan happens before the limit); billing rounds up per-table minimums; and clustered-table estimates are upper bounds.

Choosing between models is a break-even calculation. At ~$0.04–0.06 per slot-hour (Editions vary), 100 baseline slots ≈ $3,000–4,500/month. If your organization scans, say, 400 TiB/month on-demand ≈ $2,500 — stay on-demand. At 5 PiB/month ≈ $32,000 on-demand, a capacity reservation that runs those workloads for a fraction of that wins decisively. Rules of thumb the exam rewards: **on-demand** for spiky, low-volume, unpredictable usage (you pay only when querying, and pruning discipline directly cuts spend); **capacity** for sustained, heavy, or budget-capped usage (cost becomes flat and predictable, and inefficient queries cost time, not money). Guardrails differ per model: on-demand risk is a runaway scan — mitigate with `maximum_bytes_billed` (the query fails rather than over-bills), `require_partition_filter`, and custom quotas; capacity risk is queueing — mitigate with autoscaling headroom and workload-isolated reservations.

Storage pricing is the quieter half: active storage (~$0.02/GB/month logical) drops to long-term (~half price) automatically for any table or partition untouched by writes for 90 consecutive days — reads do not reset the clock. Large stable corpora (your embedding source archives) get cheap on their own; there is also a physical-bytes billing model that charges on compressed size, which favors highly compressible data. Cost attribution and observability live in `INFORMATION_SCHEMA.JOBS`, where per-job `total_bytes_processed` and `total_slot_ms` tell you exactly which query, user, or pipeline is spending the money.
