1. Two Planes, One Query

The Workload-Driven Storage Decision Matrix sent every OLAP branch here, past Cloud SQL vs AlloyDB vs Spanner and Firestore vs Bigtable vs Memorystore. Those all size one box that holds storage and compute together. BigQuery splits them: Colossus holds the bytes, Dremel rents you slots. Add concurrent workloads and watch the difference. Like a restaurant that owns its kitchen versus one that hires as many chefs as tonight needs.

The separation is AlloyDB's disaggregated storage taken to its extreme, and it cuts both ways. Scans and joins over huge tables are elastic and cheap; single-row updates are structurally wrong here — DML exists, but each statement is a distributed job, not an indexed write. That is the matrix's OLTP branch still pointing somewhere else.

2. Pruning The Partitions

A 730 GB events table, partitioned daily over two years — about 1 GB per day. Each square below is one partition. Drag the window, then change the shape of the filter and watch pruning switch off entirely. Like a filing cabinet with a drawer per day: you open seven, unless you describe the date in a way the clerk can't resolve until he's opened all 730.

Two operational levers ride on partitions, and both are exam answers. require_partition_filter rejects any query that omits the filter — the budget guardrail you just triggered. Partition expiration auto-deletes partitions older than N days: lifecycle management inside the warehouse, the same idea as the GCS Storage Classes and Lifecycle Management rules from Lesson 01, applied to table segments instead of objects.

3. Cluster Key Order

Inside one day's 1 GB partition of PARTITION BY DATE(event_ts) CLUSTER BY customer_id, event_type, the rows are sorted into blocks whose min/max the engine checks before reading. Tick which columns your WHERE clause actually touches. Like a phone book sorted by surname then first name: surname narrows it instantly, first name alone makes you read every page.

When to reach for which: partition when you need guaranteed, estimable pruning, per-segment lifecycle, or filter enforcement; cluster when cardinality is high or several filter columns matter; skip both under roughly 1 GB, where metadata overhead outweighs the skipping and many tiny partitions actually slow queries down. BigQuery re-clusters in the background automatically and free as loads and streaming inserts dirty the sort order — you never schedule it.

4. Baseline Versus Burst

Under capacity pricing, slots are the scarce thing and a reservation is how you hand them out. Pick one of the three from the lesson, then drive demand at it: baseline slots are always billed, autoscaled slots bill per second only while provisioned, and anything past max queues. Like staffing a shop: some staff on permanent contract, some on call, and a queue at the door when both run out.

The tiers sit above all of this: Standard for basic ad-hoc analytics with no commitments, Enterprise as the production tier (materialized-view smart tuning, BigQuery ML, fine-grained security), Enterprise Plus for the compliance ceiling — multi-region disaster recovery and higher assurance controls. A commitment is a separate thing again: optional one- or three-year prepayment at a discount. Editions, reservations and commitments are three different nouns and distractors swap them freely.

5. The Break-Even Line

Two dials on one query against a 2 TB table of 40 equal columns, daily partitioned over 365 days — then the same organisation's whole month, priced both ways. On-demand bills bytes processed at $6.25/TiB; capacity bills slot-hours and ignores bytes entirely. Like a taxi meter versus a leased car: one charges per mile, the other charges whether you drive or not.

Traps worth memorising: LIMIT 10 does not reduce bytes scanned — the scan happens before the limit; billing rounds up a 10 MB minimum per table referenced; the first 1 TiB per month is free; and clustered-table estimates are upper bounds. Storage is the quieter half — about $0.02/GB/month active, dropping to roughly half for anything untouched by writes for 90 days (reads do not reset the clock). Per-job total_bytes_processed and total_slot_ms in INFORMATION_SCHEMA.JOBS tell you who is spending it.
