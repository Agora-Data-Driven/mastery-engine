**The big idea**: **Migration Services: Datastream, DMS, BigQuery Data Transfer, Transfer Appliance** taught you to move a finite dataset into Google Cloud once, and **The Workload-Driven Storage Decision Matrix** taught you to choose from the shape of the work, not of the data. Everything since — **Storage-Compute Separation and the Columnar Engine**, **Partitioning and Clustering in BigQuery**, **Materialized Views and BI Engine** — assumed it had landed. This lesson is about data that never stops arriving. Google Cloud splits that across three services with genuinely different jobs: Pub/Sub transports independently consumed events, Dataflow executes Beam pipelines, and Datastream turns supported source changes into managed replication streams. Choosing among them, or comparing Pub/Sub with Kafka, comes down to five questions and only five — state, ordering, replay, scaling, and operational ownership. Most wrong answers here are a true sentence about the wrong one of the five.

**Key concepts**

- **A topic is the publish point; a subscription is a durable, named delivery path attached to it.** Each subscription receives its own copy of published messages and tracks acknowledgement state independently. Publish 1 message to a topic carrying 3 subscriptions — analytics, alerting, archival — and 3 copies are delivered, each with its own cursor. Share one cursor instead and the slowest consumer defines everybody's backlog.
- **Subscription design must mirror consumer responsibility, and the failure is silent.** Sharing one subscription load-balances among instances of one logical consumer: 4 analytics instances on 1 subscription each handle roughly 25% of the traffic, correctly. Put alerting on that same subscription and it competes for a share instead of receiving all events — no error, just missed alerts.
- **Pull, push and export are architectural choices, not client-library preferences.** Pull lets clients request or stream messages and ack them; push delivers HTTP requests to an endpoint; export writes to supported destinations. The choice changes flow control, error handling and available delivery features.
- **Standard delivery is at least once, so the acknowledgement is the contract.** A message can be redelivered if its acknowledgement deadline expires or the subscriber negatively acknowledges it (nacks). Acknowledge *after* the required durable effect, never before processing begins: ack-then-process loses the message permanently if the worker dies a millisecond later, while process-then-ack costs at most a duplicate. Hence stable message IDs and idempotent sinks.
- **Exactly-once delivery is a bounded option, not a distributed transaction.** Pub/Sub offers it for pull subscriptions within its documented regional scope: it supplies acknowledgement success information and prevents redelivery after a successful acknowledgement for a Pub/Sub message ID. It does *not* merge two duplicate publishes by your application, and does *not* make an external database write atomic with the acknowledgement.
- **Ordering is subscription-specific and driven by ordering keys.** Publishers use the same nonempty key for related messages, publish it consistently under the documented regional requirements, and the subscription enables ordering. Different keys process in parallel; one hot key serializes progress and builds backlog behind itself. Redelivery can affect later messages for the same key, so ordered consumers must tolerate repeats and ack promptly.
- **A dead-letter topic stops a poison message blocking forever — but it is not a perfect ordered transaction log.** Dead-letter forwarding and ordering have documented limitations; it is a release valve, not a guarantee.
- **Retention and replay are finite and configured.** A new subscription generally has no history of messages published before it existed unless topic retention and subscription behaviour cover them. Choose retention from outage-recovery and replay requirements, at a cost.
- **Monitor oldest unacknowledged age, not queue depth.** Watch backlog bytes and messages, acknowledgement latency, redelivery, expired deadlines, dead-letter volume, publish errors and per-ordering-key hot spots. Queue depth hides stale messages behind throughput: a topic looks healthy while one message ages past usefulness.
- **Dataflow is the managed runner for Apache Beam batch and streaming pipelines.** A streaming job continuously consumes unbounded input, maintains windows and state, processes late data and writes sinks. Correctness begins with Beam semantics; Dataflow adds managed execution, autoscaling, metrics and updates.
- **Streaming Engine moves much streaming execution and state management off the worker VMs into the Dataflow service backend.** It cuts worker persistent-disk needs and gives more responsive horizontal autoscaling, at a separate service cost. It removes neither per-key state limits nor an inefficient design.
- **Horizontal autoscaling adds workers only when the work can be split.** It responds to backlog, throughput, CPU and available parallelism within configured bounds. Take 10,000 events per second arriving and one worker sustaining 500: 20 workers clear it, but if the source offers 4 partitions, raising the maximum from 4 to 40 changes nothing — backlog stays high while autoscaling correctly decides extra workers cannot help. A hot key or one serialized sink looks identical.
- **Set worker minima and maxima from steady-state demand, spike recovery, quotas and downstream capacity.** A high maximum is useless when CPU, IP, regional or service quotas prevent allocation, and excessive concurrency can overload BigQuery, other APIs or a database even when Dataflow scales.
- **Low CPU is not evidence of a healthy streaming job.** Observe system lag, data freshness, backlog, watermark progress, per-stage throughput, autoscaling decisions, parallelism, shuffle and failed bundles. A job can idle at low CPU while stuck on I/O, a key bottleneck, a quota, or slow external calls.
- **Design external calls with batching, caching, bounded timeouts, idempotency and rate limiting**, never a client per element. A side effect inside a retried transform can run again, so use transactional sinks or idempotency keys, and keep a dead-letter and replay path.
- **Operational changes in production need a plan.** Compatible updates can preserve state; incompatible topology, coder, window or state changes may require draining or a new job. A drain stops new input and lets in-flight windows progress; cancellation can abandon work. Canary upgrades on a low-risk slice and compare watermark, output counts and late panes before replacing the stable job.
- **Datastream is managed CDC: connect, backfill, then stream changes.** It connects to supported operational sources, performs an initial backfill or snapshot, reads ongoing changes through source-specific mechanisms, and writes supported Google Cloud destinations with low latency — BigQuery for analytics, Cloud Storage as a durable change landing zone. Use it when the requirement is database replication, not arbitrary event processing; Dataflow or SQL consumes the result afterward.
- **Source preparation is the hidden work.** Log-based capture needs database-specific logging, permissions, network connectivity and log retention; private connectivity may use Google Cloud networking plus VPN or Interconnect. Validate source versions and limitations first.
- **"Stream: running" is not "destination is current".** The snapshot and the change stream must meet at a consistent boundary, and operators still monitor backfill progress, source lag, destination errors, schema drift, and failed objects or tables. A stream shown as running can have individual tables falling steadily behind.
- **Recovery is bounded by source log retention.** If an outage exceeds the retained logs, resnapshotting may be necessary: a 12-hour stall against 24 hours of retention is a catch-up, the same stall against 6 hours is a full re-snapshot. Monitor the distance to the oldest required source position, and size infrastructure to catch up without harming the transactional source. Reconcile replicated keys and business totals periodically — status proves neither completeness nor ordering.
- **Schema evolution and delete semantics are destination-aware.** Adding a column may propagate differently from a rename, type change or table recreation, so test the DDL you run and version downstream consumers. A raw change stream may carry operation and ordering metadata while a BigQuery destination presents current state — consumers must know whether they read event history, latest state, or both.
- **Kafka exposes partitioned logs; Pub/Sub exposes topics and subscriptions.** In Kafka, partition count, key mapping, broker storage, replication, retention and consumer groups shape scaling and ordering; records are ordered within a partition and consumers manage offsets, so replay from an offset and long histories are central. Pub/Sub replays within configured retention and scales capacity for you — never assume a partition and offset API underneath.
- **The awareness-level comparison is about ownership and semantics, not speed.** Kafka ecosystems bring stream processing, connectors, schemas, compaction and transactions, and require operating or buying clusters and planning partitions, brokers, upgrades, storage and disaster recovery. Pub/Sub offloads infrastructure, autoscaling and availability while exposing Google Cloud integration and service quotas. Transactions differ too: Kafka has transactional producer and consume-transform-produce patterns; Pub/Sub exactly-once is delivery behaviour. A managed Kafka service changes ownership but not the programming model.

**Rules to remember**

- One subscription per independent application; share a subscription only among instances of the same logical consumer.
- Acknowledge after the durable effect. At least once plus idempotent sinks is the portable design in Pub/Sub and Kafka alike.
- Exactly-once is per message ID and per subscription. It never spans duplicate publishes or an external write.
- Ordering keys give order per key, not globally, and one hot key is a serialization point everywhere it appears.
- Replay exists only inside configured retention; a new subscription is not a time machine.
- More workers help only if the work can be split; parallelism is bounded by partitions, keys, quotas and the sink.
- Streaming Engine changes where state lives and how fast autoscaling reacts, not whether the pipeline is well designed.
- Datastream replicates; it does not transform, deduplicate business events, or give exactly-once side effects.
- Source log retention sets the maximum survivable outage for CDC.
- Neither Kafka nor Pub/Sub is universally superior; it is a cost-of-ownership and guarantees question.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Sharing one subscription between two applications**, so analytics and alerting compete for messages instead of each receiving all.
- **Acknowledging before the work is durable**, converting an at-least-once system into a silently lossy one.
- **Reading exactly-once as "no duplicates anywhere"**, then finding the application published the same event twice, or that the database write and the ack were never atomic.
- **Enabling ordering and then wondering why throughput collapsed** — one hot key serializes, and a redelivery on that key holds up everything behind it.
- **Trusting queue depth as a health signal** while the oldest unacknowledged message ages out of usefulness.
- **Raising max workers to fix backlog** when parallelism, a quota or the sink is the real limit — paying for workers that cannot help.
- **Assuming a pipeline update is compatible** — an incompatible topology, coder, window or state change needs a drain or a new job, and cancellation abandons work.
- **Treating a running Datastream stream as proof of a current destination**, or planning recovery without checking source log retention.
- **Migrating from Kafka by translating APIs** instead of mapping ordering, retention, replay, transactions, compaction, consumer groups, connectors and monitoring.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Classify the requirement first: database replication is Datastream, event transport with independent consumers is Pub/Sub, stateful processing with windows and late data is Dataflow. Many distractors are the right feature on the wrong service.
2. For any delivery-guarantee question, locate the acknowledgement relative to the durable effect. That detail decides loss, duplication or correctness.
3. When a stem says "exactly once", check whether it is really about a *duplicate publish* or an *external write*. If so, the answer is idempotency, not a setting.
4. For scaling questions, ask what limits parallelism — partitions, ordering keys, quotas, sink capacity — before worker counts. Low CPU with high backlog is the tell.
5. For CDC scenarios, compare outage duration against source log retention, and treat "stream running" as a status, never proof.
6. In a Kafka comparison, decide what the organisation must own. Managed operations and elastic capacity point to Pub/Sub; partition control, offsets or portability to Kafka.
