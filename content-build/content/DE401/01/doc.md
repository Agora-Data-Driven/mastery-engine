Every course in this curriculum built one component of the system this capstone operates: the lake from DE 201, the streaming machinery from DE 202, the feature and embedding pipelines from DE 301, the inverted and vector indexes from IR 101 and IR 201, the ranking funnel from IR 202, and the RAG endpoint from IR 301. This lesson assembles them into one backbone, names every stage and its owner, decides which hops run batch and which run streaming, fixes the contracts that make components replaceable, and settles the question that determines whether you can ever recover from failure: where the truth lives.

## Source-to-Serving Data Flow: Ingestion to Lake to Feature Store to Index to RAG Endpoint

The reference flow has six stages. Sources: the application database arriving as change events, user uploads, crawled pages, and SaaS exports. Ingestion: raw events and files landing immutably in cloud storage. The lake: bronze raw landing, silver normalized documents, gold serving artifacts, in an open table format with snapshot publication. Pipelines: parsing, deduplication, chunking, and embedding. Indexes: the inverted index and the vector index. Serving: the hybrid funnel behind a RAG endpoint that answers with citations.

Walk one document through with numbers, because the walk is the architecture. A 40-page PDF lands at 14:00 in the raw zone with an upload event. The parse job normalizes it into a silver document record: `doc_id`, `source_uri`, a monotonic version, the text, MIME type, ACL, and tenant. A chunking pass emits 12 chunks with deterministic IDs derived from document, version, span, and chunker version. The embedding stage encodes all 12 with model v3. Upserts publish to the vector namespace and the inverted index, and by 14:07 the document is visible to the funnel. At 14:32 a support answer cites chunk 7, and the citation resolves back through the chunk to the silver document to the original `source_uri`, so the user can open the exact page that grounded the answer.

Each stage carries four labels: the storage system it lives in, the transform that produces it, the team that owns it, and its freshness SLA. This is not bureaucracy. The observability lesson measures this path, the cost lesson prices it, and the backfill lesson replays it; you cannot monitor, price, or rebuild a stage you cannot name. The medallion discipline from the lake course supplies the crucial property: bronze and silver hold the corpus of record, while gold, the chunks, embeddings, and indexes, is recomputable from silver by definition.

## Batch vs Streaming Paths in the Backbone

The backbone deliberately runs at two speeds. The batch path carries full and scheduled passes: crawls, bulk re-parses, compaction, full re-embedding, and index rebuilds, on Dataflow batch jobs and scheduled queries. Its economics are high throughput and low cost per record at latencies of minutes to hours. The streaming path carries what cannot wait: change-data-capture events from the source database flowing through the log-based capture, queue, and streaming pipeline from DE 202 and DE 302, plus upload events, at seconds-to-minutes freshness and a higher per-record price.

The decision rule per hop is freshness requirement times volume times cost. Ordinary content edits tolerate fifteen-minute micro-batches; most corpora see no user-visible difference. Permission changes and deletions ride the streaming path, because the revocation SLA from the security lesson is a security property, not a freshness preference. Pricing and status facts that answers depend on go streaming. Evaluation datasets and analytics are batch, full stop. A worked split makes the rule concrete. A support product ingests 8,000 document edits a day, 40 ACL or deletion events, and 300 price-sheet updates. The edits flow through a 15-minute micro-batch at essentially batch prices. The 40 security events and 300 price updates ride the streaming path, where their tiny volume makes the per-record premium irrelevant while their freshness requirement is absolute. Running everything streaming would multiply the ingestion bill for zero user-visible gain; running everything batch would leave revoked documents answerable for a quarter of an hour, which is a breach window, not a latency number.

Both paths write the same silver tables and the same indexes, which creates the convergence problem: a slow batch backfill must not overwrite a newer streaming update. The solution is the versioning discipline from the CDC lesson: every upsert carries a monotonic version, and writers apply last-writer-wins on `(doc_id, version)` rather than on arrival order, so a replayed batch event loses to a newer streamed one deterministically.

The final warning is architectural: never let batch and streaming become two codebases. The Beam model from DE 202 exists precisely so the same transforms run in both modes, and teams that fork the logic discover the drift during an incident, when the streaming parse and the batch parse produce different chunks from the same document and nobody can say which one the index should contain.

## Component Boundaries and Interface Contracts

Every arrow in the architecture diagram is a contract with four parts: schema, semantics, SLA, and failure behavior. The components stay replaceable only because the contracts stay stable, and the contract set for this backbone is small enough to memorize.

The raw event contract: `source_uri`, `ingested_at`, immutable payload, content hash, append-only. The silver document contract: `doc_id`, `source_uri`, monotonic version, content hash, text, MIME type, ACL, tenant, `updated_at`, upserted by `(doc_id, version)`. The chunk contract: `chunk_id` as a deterministic hash of document, version, span, and chunker version, plus text, offsets, ACL, and tenant. The embedding contract: `chunk_id`, `model_id`, vector, `embedded_at`. The index contract: postings and vector rows keyed by `chunk_id`, tagged with model and index generation. The query API contract: query plus tenant context plus filters in, candidates with chunk IDs and provenance out. The answer API contract: question in, answer with citations and a trace ID out.

The semantics prevent whole bug classes. Deterministic, version-tagged identity means reprocessing the same input yields the same IDs, which is what makes replay idempotent when a pipeline is rerun after an outage. Every artifact carries the version of the code or model that produced it, chunker version, model ID, index generation, so mixed populations are visible rather than silent. Every record carries tenant and ACL, per the security lesson. Every timestamp exists twice, event time and ingestion time, so watermark logic from the streaming course has something to work with. Each contract also names its failure behavior: a stage whose output fails validation is quarantined, never silently dropped, and consumers read only validated, published snapshots in the sense of the lake course.

The SLAs attach per contract: ingestion lag, parse success rate, chunk coverage, embedding freshness, index visibility lag, query p95, answer p95. A contract without an SLA is a hope, and the observability lesson exists to make each of these a number on a dashboard with an owner.

Contract discipline pays the first time a component is swapped. When this system replaced its embedding model, the only files that changed were the embedding stage and the index generation tag, because the chunk contract upstream and the index contract downstream never learned that a new model existed. Contrast with the incident that taught the discipline: an early version let the serving API read the parser's internal row format directly, and a parser upgrade renamed one field, breaking answers in production at 02:00 even though every schema was technically valid. The serving layer had coupled to an implementation, not a contract, and the fix that weekend was to write the contract down and version it.

## Single Source of Truth vs Derived Copies: Documents, Chunks, Embeddings

The backbone's most important rule fits in one sentence: for each entity, exactly one system holds the truth, and everything else is a derived, recomputable, disposable copy. Here, the lake's silver documents table is the source of truth for documents and their ACLs. Chunks, embeddings, inverted-index postings, vector rows, and every cache are derived copies.

The rule earns its keep on the worst day, not the best one. Derived copies drift: a dropped event here, a partial backfill there, a mixed model generation everywhere. When copies disagree, you must know which one to believe and how to rebuild the rest. The operational test is ruthless: any derived store may be wiped and rebuilt from truth without information loss. If some store cannot survive that test, it has secretly become a source of truth and must be given truth-grade care, backups, schema governance, and change control, whether or not that was the plan.

Synchronization runs on the projection pattern from DE 301: derived stores subscribe to truth's events and versions, and reconciliation jobs compare them continuously, counting documents in silver against live chunks against live vectors per model generation, alerting when the counts diverge beyond a threshold. Deletes and ACL tightenings propagate to every copy within the revocation SLA: tombstones in truth, hard deletes in the copies, verified by reconciliation rather than assumed.

The daily disciplines follow directly. Nobody hand-edits the vector database, because a hand edit is invisible to the next rebuild and will silently revert. Backfills read from truth, never from another copy, or errors photocopy. Migrations build the new copy from truth, validate it, and swap an alias, which is the next lesson's rebuild-and-swap in embryo. Disaster recovery is a backup of the truth plus a rebuild runbook, since the copies need no backups at all. Embeddings get one nuance: they are derived but expensive to recompute, so keeping an old generation around is a cost decision the cost lesson will price, never a truth question.

The failure story that justifies all of it: a team's parser was lossy, dropping tables from PDFs, and nobody noticed for months because the index looked fine. When they fixed the parser and rebuilt, the table text was gone from the index, because it had never existed anywhere else. The index had quietly become the only place some content lived. If you cannot rebuild every index from the lake tonight, you have already lost the truth; you just have not needed it yet.
