The backbone from the previous lesson has a dozen places to degrade silently, and users find every one of them before an uninstrumented team does. Observability is the difference between detecting a freshness lag or a retrieval regression in minutes and discovering it weeks later in a churn report from sales. This lesson builds the monitoring stack in four layers: freshness SLAs with real lag metrics, production retrieval metrics that track what users actually experience, golden sets and canaries that catch regressions before cutover, and a triage framework that assigns any quality drop to its layer in one meeting instead of one week.

## Freshness SLAs and Lag Metrics for Ingestion and Index Builds

Freshness is the elapsed time between a source change and its effect becoming visible to queries. It decomposes along the contract chain: ingestion lag from source commit to raw landing, pipeline lag from landing to parsed, chunked, and embedded, and index visibility lag from embedded to searchable. End-to-end freshness is the sum, and each stage gets its own SLA. A reasonable streaming-path budget: ingestion p95 of 60 seconds, pipeline p95 of five minutes, visibility p95 of two minutes, end-to-end p95 of ten minutes.

The measurement primitive is the watermark. Each stage tracks the maximum source timestamp it has fully processed, and lag is simply now minus that watermark, emitted per stage and per major tenant. Two alert shapes matter: an absolute SLA breach, and lag growth rate, because a lag that doubles every hour means the consumer is falling behind the producer, the queue-backlog failure from the streaming course, visible as rising oldest-unacked-message age or growing backlog latency. A per-stage budget for one production system:

| Stage | Lag metric | p95 SLA |
|---|---|---:|
| Source commit to raw landing | Ingestion lag | 60 s |
| Landing to chunked and embedded | Pipeline lag | 5 min |
| Embedded to searchable | Visibility lag | 2 min |
| End to end | Total freshness | 10 min |

Every cell has an owner and an alert. When two cells breach together, the one upstream is investigated first, because downstream lag is usually upstream lag in disguise.

Full index builds have their own freshness trap: a rebuild that takes six hours publishes an index that is already six hours stale at birth. The fixes are structural: build from a snapshot at a recorded timestamp, keep incremental updates flowing into the live index during the build, and replay the events since the snapshot onto the new index before the alias swaps. Staleness at publish is a metric worth graphing, not an afterthought.

A scenario ties it together. At 09:10 a leaked internal document's ACL is tightened with a five-minute revocation SLA. The dashboard shows visibility lag spiking to 22 minutes because a compaction job had locked the target segment. The document kept answering for seventeen extra minutes. The incident review does two things: compaction windows get coupled to SLA alerts, and the freshness error budget gets a burn-down view, so the third breach in a month freezes non-essential changes until the path is fixed.

## Production Retrieval Metrics: Hit Rate, MRR, Reranker Scores

Offline metrics from the evaluation courses describe judged queries; production metrics describe what users experience right now, and they are not the same instrument.

Hit rate is the fraction of interactions where retrieval produced something the user engaged with: a clicked citation, a copied answer, a thumbs up. Its dark twin is the zero-hit rate, the fraction of queries that returned nothing useful, which is the single best early-warning number a retrieval system has. Production MRR adapts the offline metric to behavior: the mean reciprocal rank of the first clicked or cited result, computed from interaction logs. It measures position-of-use rather than position-of-relevance, drifting downward when the right document slides from rank one to rank four even while offline nDCG holds.

Reranker score distributions are the quiet sensor. Track the p10, p50, and p90 of top-1 and top-50 scores daily. A distribution shift with no code change means the inputs changed: new query types, a new content shape, corpus growth. Falling top-1 score mass means the funnel is feeding the reranker weaker candidates. Funnel attrition metrics complete the picture: candidate counts per source, post-ACL, post-dedupe, and post-rerank, so that a stage which suddenly emits less has an address.

The answer-side signals close the loop: thumbs rate, escalation rate, re-query rate, where a user rephrasing means the first answer failed, and abstention rate, which rises when retrieval weakens or gates tighten.

The scenario: a new chunker deploys Monday. Wednesday, thumbs rate is down eight percent. The reranker top-1 p50 has fallen from 0.81 to 0.74, and attrition shows post-dedupe candidates halved. Diagnosis in one meeting: the new chunker emits near-duplicate chunks that deduplication collapses, starving the reranker. Without per-stage counts, that same meeting is a week of log diving.

## Golden Query Sets and Regression Canaries for Index Changes

The golden set is one to three hundred queries with adjudicated relevant documents and gold answers, versioned, and stratified across query classes: exact-ID, conceptual, negation, multilingual, multi-hop, and each tenant's characteristic shapes. Stratification is what gives the acceptance rule its teeth: a set that is eighty percent conceptual queries will happily pass a change that destroys exact-ID lookup, because the damaged class is too small to move the average. The class proportions should roughly mirror production traffic, and every class needs enough queries, at least twenty or so, for its metric to mean anything. It is the immovable reference from the evaluation lesson, and its first job is gating change.

The canary process applies to every index-affecting change: new embedding model, new chunker, new BM25 parameters, new index version. Build the candidate index, run the golden queries against old and new, and compare per-query metrics paired by query, the paired design from IR 104 that removes query-difficulty variance. The acceptance rule is per class, not per average: no query class may regress beyond its threshold, because averages hide segment regressions, and a two-point average gain that costs the exact-ID class fifteen points is a bad trade shipping under a good headline. Only then comes shadow traffic and the alias swap of the backfill lesson.

Production canaries complement the offline gate. Synthetic canary documents with known retrieval properties, a document that must rank first for `canary refund policy xq9`, are queried every minute: a miss detects live index corruption, an ACL regression, or a serving bug. A freshness canary updates a planted document hourly and measures time-to-visibility, exercising the entire freshness chain end to end. Each canary maps to a layer, which is what makes the page actionable: freshness canary implicates the pipeline, retrieval canary the index, faithfulness canary the generator.

Two failure modes of the practice itself: golden-set rot, where queries go stale as the product and its language evolve, fixed by quarterly review and by a rule that every production incident contributes its queries to the set; and teaching-to-the-test, where parameters are tuned until the golden set passes, fixed by holding out a slice that is never used for tuning, only for final validation.

## Diagnosing Quality Drops: Data Drift vs Index Defect vs Model Change

When quality drops, the cause belongs to one of three families, and each has a signature. Data drift: the world changed. New products, new vocabulary, seasonal query mix, corpus growth. The signature is a gradual decline over days or weeks, concentrated in specific query segments, with reranker score distributions shifting but per-stage counts stable. The response is to refresh evaluation sets, fine-tune on the new distribution, and adapt chunking to new content shapes. Index defect: the machinery broke. A failed build, a mixed-generation index, an ACL filter bug, dropped embeddings. The signature is a step change aligned with a build or deploy timestamp, canaries firing, reconciliation counts diverging, attrition jumping at one stage. The response is rollback or rebuild. Model change: the model moved. A judge update moving your metrics without any system change, a generator swap, an embedding upgrade, a prompt edit. The signature is a shift aligned with a model-version timestamp, uniform across segments rather than concentrated, or metrics moving while user outcomes hold still, which is judge drift specifically. The response is to pin or roll back versions and recalibrate judges.

The diagnostic order exploits these signatures. First, when did it start: align the drop with deploy, build, and corpus-event timelines, because a timestamp match is most of the diagnosis. Second, does the golden set reproduce it offline: if yes, live-only causes like caches, ACL state, and traffic mix are eliminated; if no, look there first. Third, which canaries fired, mapping to layers. Fourth, per-stage attrition: which stage's output changed shape. Fifth, segment analysis: concentrated decline points to drift or a segment-specific defect, uniform decline to a global model change. The order is deliberate: each step is cheaper than the next and eliminates a whole family, so the expensive investigations, segment dives and log archaeology, happen only after the cheap eliminations have narrowed the field.

A full scenario: support quality drops on Thursday. The golden set reproduces the drop offline, eliminating live-only causes. The build timeline shows an index rebuild Wednesday at 23:00. Reconciliation shows live vector count twelve percent below document count for generation v4. Diagnosis: the rebuild silently dropped chunks whose embedding calls timed out, with no quarantine gate, the contract violation from lesson one. Response: roll the alias back to the v3 index, add the gate, rebuild, and add a canary that asserts chunk coverage per generation. The last step is the meta-discipline: every diagnosis ends by adding the metric or canary that would have caught it earlier, because observability compounds.
