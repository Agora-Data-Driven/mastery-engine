**The big idea**: **Source-to-Serving Data Flow: Ingestion to Lake to Feature Store to Index to RAG Endpoint** laid out the path a document takes, **Batch vs Streaming Paths in the Backbone** set the two speeds it runs at, **Component Boundaries and Interface Contracts** drew the seams between its parts, and **Single Source of Truth vs Derived Copies: Documents, Chunks, Embeddings** said which copies are rebuildable. Every one of those seams degrades *silently*, and users find them before an uninstrumented team does. Observability is the difference between catching a freshness lag or a retrieval regression in minutes and reading about it weeks later in a churn report from sales. This lesson builds the monitoring stack in four layers: freshness SLAs with real lag metrics, production retrieval metrics tracking what users actually experience, golden query sets and canaries that catch regressions before cutover, and a triage framework that assigns any quality drop to its layer in one meeting instead of one week.

**Key concepts**

- **Freshness is the elapsed time between a source change and its effect becoming visible to queries, and it decomposes along the contract chain.** Ingestion lag runs from source commit to raw landing; pipeline lag from landing to parsed, chunked and embedded; index visibility lag from embedded to searchable. End-to-end freshness is the sum, and each stage gets its own SLA — one end-to-end number says you are late without saying where. A workable streaming-path budget: ingestion p95 `60 s`, pipeline p95 `5 min`, visibility p95 `2 min`, end-to-end p95 `10 min`. Every cell has an owner and an alert.
- **The measurement primitive is the watermark.** Each stage tracks the maximum source timestamp it has *fully* processed; lag is now minus that watermark, emitted per stage and per major tenant. Without it you are inferring health from throughput, which looks fine while a consumer chews through the wrong end of a backlog.
- **Two alert shapes, not one.** The absolute SLA breach catches "we are late right now". The lag *growth rate* catches "we will be far later soon": a lag doubling every hour means the consumer is falling behind the producer — the queue-backlog failure from the streaming course, visible as rising oldest-unacked-message age. When two cells breach together, investigate upstream first: downstream lag is usually upstream lag in disguise.
- **A full index build is stale at birth.** A rebuild that takes six hours publishes an index describing the world as it was six hours ago. The fixes are structural: build from a snapshot at a recorded timestamp, keep incremental updates flowing into the live index during the build, and replay the events since the snapshot before the alias swaps. Staleness at publish is a metric worth graphing.
- **Freshness has a compliance edge.** At 09:10 a leaked document's ACL is tightened under a five-minute revocation SLA. Visibility lag spikes to 22 minutes because a compaction job has locked the target segment, and the document keeps answering for seventeen extra minutes. The review couples compaction windows to SLA alerts and adds a freshness error-budget burn-down, so the third breach in a month freezes non-essential changes.
- **Production retrieval metrics measure what users experience; offline metrics describe judged queries.** Hit rate is the fraction of interactions where retrieval produced something the user engaged with — a clicked citation, a copied answer, a thumbs up. Its dark twin, the **zero-hit rate**, is the best early-warning number a retrieval system has.
- **Production MRR measures position-of-use, not position-of-relevance.** It is the mean reciprocal rank of the first clicked or cited result, from interaction logs, and it drifts downward when the right document slides from rank 1 to rank 4 even while offline nDCG holds steady.
- **Reranker score distributions are the quiet sensor.** Track p10, p50 and p90 of top-1 and top-50 scores daily. A shift with no code change means the *inputs* changed: new query types, a new content shape, corpus growth. Falling top-1 score mass means the funnel is feeding the reranker weaker candidates.
- **Funnel attrition gives every stage an address.** Count candidates per source, post-ACL, post-dedupe and post-rerank. A new chunker deploys Monday; by Wednesday thumbs rate is down eight percent, reranker top-1 p50 has fallen from 0.81 to 0.74, and post-dedupe candidates have halved. Diagnosis in one meeting: the chunker emits near-duplicate chunks that deduplication collapses, starving the reranker. Without per-stage counts, that meeting is a week of log diving.
- **Answer-side signals close the loop**: thumbs rate, escalation rate, re-query rate — a rephrasing means the first answer failed — and abstention rate, which rises when retrieval weakens or gates tighten.
- **The golden query set is one to three hundred queries with adjudicated relevant documents and gold answers, versioned and stratified.** Classes: exact-ID, conceptual, negation, multilingual, multi-hop, and each tenant's characteristic shapes. Stratification is what gives the acceptance rule its teeth — a set that is eighty percent conceptual will happily pass a change that destroys exact-ID lookup, because the damaged class is too small to move the average. Mirror production proportions, and give every class at least twenty or so queries for its metric to mean anything.
- **The canary process gates every index-affecting change**: new embedding model, new chunker, new BM25 parameters, new index version. Build the candidate index, run the golden queries against old and new, and compare metrics **paired by query** — the paired design from IR 104, which removes query-difficulty variance. Accept **per class, never per average**: a two-point average gain that costs the exact-ID class fifteen points is a bad trade shipping under a good headline. Only then come shadow traffic and the alias swap.
- **Production canaries complement the offline gate, and each one maps to a layer.** A synthetic document that must rank first for `canary refund policy xq9`, queried every minute, detects live index corruption, an ACL regression or a serving bug. A freshness canary updates a planted document hourly and measures time-to-visibility across the whole chain. Freshness canary implicates the pipeline, retrieval canary the index, faithfulness canary the generator — which is what makes the page actionable.
- **The practice has two failure modes of its own.** Golden-set rot: queries go stale as the product and its language evolve, fixed by quarterly review and a rule that every incident contributes its queries. Teaching-to-the-test: parameters get tuned until the set passes, fixed by holding out a slice used only for final validation.
- **Every quality drop belongs to one of three families, and each has a signature.** *Data drift* — the world changed: a gradual decline over days or weeks, concentrated in specific query segments, reranker score distributions shifting while per-stage counts hold stable. *Index defect* — the machinery broke: a step change aligned with a build or deploy timestamp, canaries firing, reconciliation counts diverging, attrition jumping at one stage. *Model change* — the model moved: a shift aligned with a model-version timestamp, uniform across segments rather than concentrated, or metrics moving while user outcomes hold still (judge drift).
- **The responses differ as sharply as the signatures.** Drift: refresh evaluation sets, fine-tune, adapt chunking. Defect: roll back or rebuild. Model change: pin versions and recalibrate judges.
- **The diagnostic order is cheapest-elimination-first.** (1) When did it start — align the drop with deploy, build and corpus-event timelines; a timestamp match is most of the diagnosis. (2) Does the golden set reproduce it offline: yes eliminates live-only causes, no sends you straight to them. (3) Which canaries fired, and to which layer do they map. (4) Per-stage attrition: whose output changed shape. (5) Segments: concentrated points to drift or a segment-specific defect, uniform to a global model change.
- **A worked diagnosis.** Support quality drops on Thursday; the golden set reproduces it offline. The build timeline shows an index rebuild Wednesday at 23:00, and reconciliation shows the live vector count twelve percent below the document count for generation v4: the rebuild silently dropped chunks whose embedding calls timed out, with no quarantine gate — the contract violation from lesson one. Response: roll the alias back to v3, add the gate, rebuild, and add a canary asserting chunk coverage per generation.

**Rules to remember**

- Lag = now − watermark, per stage and per tenant. The watermark is the maximum source timestamp *fully* processed.
- Alert on the absolute breach **and** on the growth rate; when two stages breach, investigate upstream first.
- A six-hour rebuild is six hours stale at publish unless you snapshot, keep updates flowing, and replay.
- Zero-hit rate is the earliest warning; production MRR is position-of-use; reranker distributions move when the inputs move.
- Per-stage candidate counts turn "quality dropped" into "post-dedupe halved".
- Golden set: 100–300 queries, stratified to mirror traffic, ~20+ per class, versioned, with a held-out slice.
- Compare paired by query, and accept per class, never on the average.
- Every canary must map to a layer, or the alert page is not actionable.
- Diagnose in order: timestamp → offline reproduction → canaries → attrition → segments.
- Gradual and concentrated ⇒ drift. Step change and build-aligned ⇒ index defect. Uniform and version-aligned ⇒ model change.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Measuring only end-to-end freshness.** You learn that you are late and nothing about where; per-stage SLAs make the number actionable.
- **Reading throughput as freshness.** A healthy events-per-second rate with a stalled watermark is a pipeline working the wrong end of a backlog.
- **Treating offline metrics as production metrics.** Offline nDCG can hold steady while production MRR falls, because the traffic mix moved and the judged set did not.
- **A golden set that mirrors your interests instead of your traffic.** Eighty percent conceptual queries makes the exact-ID class statistically invisible.
- **Accepting on the average.** Averages hide segment regressions, and the regressing class usually has the crispest user expectation.
- **Unpaired comparison.** Comparing means across different query samples buries a real effect under query-difficulty variance.
- **Tuning against the golden set until it passes.** It stops being a measurement and becomes part of the model.
- **Reading a metric move as a system move.** A judge update shifts your numbers with nothing in the system changed; check whether user outcomes moved too.
- **Ending a diagnosis at the fix.** Observability only compounds if every incident adds the metric or canary that would have caught it earlier.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For a freshness question, find which stage the number belongs to. Ingestion, pipeline and visibility lag have different SLAs and owners, and distractors swap them.
2. If a scenario hands you a *timeline*, use it first. A drop aligned to a build timestamp is a defect; aligned to a model version and uniform across segments, a model change; aligned to nothing and gradual, drift.
3. Ask whether the golden set reproduces the drop offline. Yes eliminates live-only causes such as caches, ACL state and traffic mix; no points straight at them.
4. When candidate counts are quoted per stage, find the stage whose output changed shape. That stage is the answer far more often than the model.
5. Watch for averages hiding classes. If an option offers "average nDCG improved", check what the per-class numbers did.
6. Distinguish "metrics moved" from "users noticed". Metrics moving while thumbs and escalations hold still is judge drift.
7. Prefer the cheaper elimination. The diagnostic order is deliberately cheapest-first, so an answer starting with a segment dive usually describes the wrong process.
