1. Stages Become Sensors

On the left, the backbone you already drew: ingestion → lake → chunks and embeddings → index → RAG endpoint, with its component boundaries and its derived copies. On the right, the sensor each boundary has to carry, because a boundary with no sensor fails quietly. Tap a stage. Like a house with smoke alarms: the wiring did not change, but now the house can tell you which room.

Notice what observability is made of: the interface contracts from lesson one, turned into numbers. "One row per chunk, tagged with its generation" is a contract; reconciliation counting vectors against documents is that same contract read out loud every hour. And because documents are the single source of truth while chunks, embeddings and the index are derived, every defect below the lake is repairable by rebuilding — which is exactly why detecting it fast is the whole game.

2. The Freshness Budget

Freshness is one number the user feels and three numbers you can act on. Drag each stage and watch the end-to-end p95 add up — then flip the trend, because a lag that is growing is a different alert from a lag that is merely high. Like a connecting flight: the total delay is the sum of the legs, and only the leg times tell you which airline to call.

A full rebuild has its own freshness trap that no slider here can fix: a build taking six hours publishes an index that is six hours stale at birth. The fixes are structural — build from a snapshot at a recorded timestamp, keep incremental updates flowing into the live index during the build, and replay the events since the snapshot before the alias swaps. Graph staleness-at-publish like any other lag.

3. Sensors And Diagnosis

A new chunker deployed Monday. By Wednesday something is wrong. Switch instruments on one at a time and watch how far the diagnosis gets — the incident is identical in every case, only your instrumentation changes. Like a doctor with a thermometer versus a doctor with blood work: same patient, very different appointment.

The answer-side signals are the ones users generate for free — thumbs rate, escalation rate, re-query rate (a user rephrasing means the first answer failed) and abstention rate, which rises when retrieval weakens or gates tighten. They are excellent alarms and terrible diagnoses. The zero-hit rate is the earliest of them, and per-stage counts are what turn any of them into an address.

4. Averages Hide Regressions

One candidate index change, measured against one golden set. The per-class results never move — only the mix of the set does. Drag it and watch the headline average flip sign while the damage stays exactly where it was. Like a national average temperature: perfectly true, and useless to the one region that froze.

Two failure modes belong to the practice itself, not to any one change. Golden-set rot: queries go stale as the product and its language evolve — fixed by quarterly review and by a rule that every production incident contributes its queries to the set. Teaching-to-the-test: parameters get tuned until the set passes — fixed by holding out a slice used only for final validation, never for tuning. A golden set you have optimised against has stopped being a measurement.

5. Drift Defect Or Model

Quality dropped. It belongs to one of three families and each has a signature, so triage is reading evidence rather than guessing. Set each row to what your dashboards actually show and watch the three candidates separate. Like a diagnosis by symptom pattern: the fever alone means nothing, the combination names the illness.

The order of those rows is not decoration — it is the diagnostic order, and it is cheapest-elimination-first: timestamp, then offline reproduction on the golden set, then canaries, then per-stage attrition, then segments. Each step eliminates a whole family, so log archaeology and segment dives only happen on a field that is already narrow. And every diagnosis ends the same way: add the metric or canary that would have caught it earlier, because observability compounds.
