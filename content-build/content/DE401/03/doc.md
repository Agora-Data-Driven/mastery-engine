The backbone's operating cost is not a bill that arrives; it is a set of choices, each reversible only at a price. Four choices dominate the ledger: which data sits in expensive hot storage, which values are computed at write time versus read time, how aggressively the indexes are compressed, and how often the corpus is re-embedded. Each has real arithmetic, and this lesson does it.

## Hot vs Cold Storage Tiering for Corpora and Indexes

The access pattern assigns the tier. Hot is the serving path: the inverted and vector indexes in memory or fast SSD, current-generation chunk text for citation display, and ACL metadata, all needing millisecond access. Warm covers recent documents and embeddings still being updated or re-served. Cold holds raw bronze payloads, superseded index generations, and old embedding generations, which can tolerate object storage and, for true archives, nearline or archive classes.

The cost shape is an order of magnitude per step. Memory-class or premium SSD storage costs roughly ten to a hundred times more per gigabyte-month than standard object storage, which itself costs several times archive-class storage. Put the shape on one corpus: two terabytes of embeddings and chunk text held hot might cost as much as a small VM every month, the same two terabytes in standard object storage costs less than a team lunch, and in archive class less than a coffee. The countervailing numbers are retrieval fees and restore latency: archive classes charge for reads and can take hours to restore, so anything you might need quickly, like the previous index generation inside its rollback window, belongs in a tier with minute-scale access, and restore time belongs in the tiering test, not just the price list.

The tiering rules follow from lesson one. The truth, bronze and silver, is never deleted without an explicit retention decision under the governance policies from the platform course. Derived copies follow the rollback window: keep the previous generation hot until the window closes, then cold, then delete. Lifecycle policies automate the transitions so nobody's calendar reminder owns the bill.

The scenario is common: a team keeps six embedding generations hot just in case, 400 GB each in premium storage, and a bill audit shows seventy percent of the storage line serves zero queries. Moving generations older than the rollback window to archive cuts the line item by about two-thirds for a one-time migration. The judgment: tier by measured access, test restore time, and never let hot storage become the default graveyard for derived copies you were afraid to delete.

## Precompute vs Compute-on-Read: The Decision Framework

Every value in the system answers the same question: compute at write time and store, or compute at read time on demand. Embeddings, chunk text, enrichment summaries, and BM25 statistics are precomputed. Conversational rewrites and reranker scores are computed per request, because they depend on the query, which is exactly why the funnel from IR 202 exists. Answer caches precompute the response for known frequent questions.

The framework has four questions. Do the inputs change per request: query-dependent values cannot be precomputed at all. Is the value space sparse: precomputing a join of every user with every document materializes mostly unread rows. Is the value reused: a value read a thousand times between writes amortizes its write cost easily. And how tight is the read latency budget: the serving path's hundred-millisecond stages leave no room for on-read generation.

The break-even arithmetic prices both sides. Precompute cost is write compute plus storage plus invalidation maintenance; compute-on-read cost is reads times per-read compute. Contextual chunk summaries for two million chunks are one batch job plus a few hundred megabytes, versus a generation call per query forever: precompute, obviously. An answer cache for the thousand most frequent questions is precompute; the long tail stays compute-on-read. A marginal case shows the arithmetic working: a per-tenant usage dashboard read 40,000 times a day takes 300 milliseconds of warehouse compute per read, about 3.3 hours of compute daily, while a precomputed table refreshed hourly costs a two-minute build twenty-four times a day, under an hour total, plus trivial storage. Past roughly a few thousand reads a day, precompute wins; below it, the build and its invalidation logic cost more than they save. Materialized views in the warehouse are the database's managed version of this same trade, precomputed joins refreshed on a schedule, as the platform course covered.

The failure modes are symmetric. Precomputing values whose inputs churn produces invalidation storms and stale outputs. Computing on read what most of traffic repeats pays per query for what one batch job buys. And the worst outcome is a precomputed value with no invalidation contract, which is lesson one's contract discipline returning as a cost line.

## Quantization and Index Compression: Recall per Dollar

Vector memory is the dominant ANN cost driver, and quantization is the lever. At 768 dimensions in float32, one vector costs 3 KB, so ten million chunks cost 30 GB of serving memory. Scalar quantization to int8 cuts that fourfold for a typical one to two point recall loss at top-10. Product quantization cuts eight to thirty-twofold for a three to ten point loss unassisted. Binary quantization cuts thirty-twofold and needs overfetch and rescore to stay accurate. The inverted side has the same family of trades from IR 101: variable-byte postings compression, blocked layouts, and the one-byte learned-sparse weights from IR 202.

The pattern that makes compression nearly free is rescore: the compressed index does recall, and full-precision vectors, kept once, rescore only the top few hundred candidates. Recall loss shrinks to noise levels at a few percent extra compute, the same idea as PLAID's residuals from IR 202.

The method is a frontier, not a guess. Measure recall@k against exact search on a sample for each configuration, price the memory of each, and plot recall against cost. A representative frontier: float32 at 100 percent recall and 30 GB; int8 at 99 percent and 7.5 GB; PQ16 at 95 percent and 1.9 GB; PQ16 with rescore at 99.3 percent and 2 GB plus five percent latency. Pick the cheapest configuration above the recall floor. Memory saved is replicas saved: dropping the working set from 30 GB to 8 GB can halve a serving fleet, which is why this is a finance discussion conducted with retrieval metrics.

The scenario: corpus growth from five to twelve million vectors would force a fleet doubling; int8 with rescore keeps the fleet flat at recall@10 within half a point of exact, verified on the golden set before cutover. Judgment: quantization interacts with the embedding model, some of which are trained to quantize well, so any quantization change is a retrieval-path change that goes through the golden-set canary. And the full-precision rescore copy is never itself quantized. One more interaction deserves respect: compression choices compound with index-structure choices from IR 201, so an IVF index with PQ codes has two knobs, probe depth and quantization level, that trade recall against latency together. Sweep them as a pair on the golden queries, because tuning either alone finds a local optimum and reports it as the frontier.

## Re-embedding Cadence: When Quality Gains Justify Rebuilds

A full re-embedding pass is the largest recurring compute decision in the backbone: corpus tokens times the per-token price, plus the index rebuild, plus validation and cutover risk. For an 800-million-token corpus the embedding alone runs to hundreds or thousands of dollars per pass, before the rebuild playbook of the next lesson.

Four triggers justify it. A measurably better model, where measurable means a golden-set gain above threshold on your queries, per class and paired, not the vendor's benchmark chart. Drift: score distributions and quality metrics degrading on current traffic, per the observability lesson's diagnosis. Corpus shape change: a major new content type the current model handles badly, for example a product line that doubles the corpus with tables and scanned documents the old model was never evaluated on. And generation hygiene: too many partial upgrades layered on each other until nobody can reason about which vectors mean what. That last trigger sneaks up on teams: three model generations coexisting across tenants, an urgent patch that re-embedded only one region, and a quarter where new documents embedded on v4 while backfill stayed on v2, until a simple question like `which model produced this vector` takes an afternoon to answer. At that point the rebuild is not buying quality; it is buying back the ability to reason about the system.

The anti-triggers matter as much. Benchmark hype without golden-set confirmation. A single loud incident, which should fix the incident, not the model. And calendar cadence, re-embedding quarterly because it is quarterly: the trigger is evidence, not dates.

The value math decides. Expected gain times traffic times value per better answer, minus rebuild cost, minus risk cost. Three nDCG points on 100,000 daily queries lifting hit rate one and a half percent is 1,500 better answers a day; a two-thousand-dollar rebuild plus a day of engineering pays back within days. A 0.4-point gain never pays back and should be declined politely.

Execution is incremental. New and changed documents embed on the new model immediately so new traffic gains at once. The backfill follows priority: query logs show which ten percent of chunks serve seventy percent of traffic, and those go first. The alias swaps only when the new generation's coverage and golden-set results pass the gates. The scenario: a vendor launch claims eight points on public benchmarks; the golden set shows 1.2 overall but five on the multilingual class, which is eighteen percent of traffic and growing. The decision is a phased re-embed of the multilingual corpus first, class-gated, with the full swap two weeks later, a decision made from segments, not slogans.
