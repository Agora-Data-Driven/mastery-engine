**The big idea**: **Source-to-Serving Data Flow: Ingestion to Lake to Feature Store to Index to RAG Endpoint** named the components, and **Single Source of Truth vs Derived Copies: Documents, Chunks, Embeddings** decided which of them you are allowed to throw away and rebuild. **Freshness SLAs and Lag Metrics for Ingestion and Index Builds**, **Production Retrieval Metrics: Hit Rate, MRR, Reranker Scores** and **Golden Query Sets and Regression Canaries for Index Changes** then gave you instruments that say whether the thing still works, and **Diagnosing Quality Drops: Data Drift vs Index Defect vs Model Change** taught you to read them. This lesson spends money against those instruments. The backbone's operating cost is not a bill that arrives; it is a set of choices, each reversible only at a price, and four of them dominate the ledger: which data sits in expensive hot storage, which values are computed at write time versus read time, how aggressively the indexes are compressed, and how often the corpus is re-embedded. Each has real arithmetic, settled on your golden set rather than a vendor's chart. **Component Boundaries and Interface Contracts** returns here as a cost line, because the most expensive object in the system is a precomputed value that nobody owns.

**Key concepts**

- **The access pattern assigns the tier — hot, warm, cold — and nothing else does.** Hot is the serving path: the inverted and vector indexes in memory or fast SSD, current-generation chunk text for citation display, and ACL metadata, all needing millisecond access. Warm is recent documents and embeddings still being updated or re-served. Cold holds raw bronze payloads, superseded index generations and old embedding generations, which tolerate object storage — nearline or archive classes for true archives. The cost shape is an order of magnitude per step: memory-class or premium SSD runs roughly ten to a hundred times more per gigabyte-month than standard object storage, which itself costs several times archive class. Two terabytes held hot costs about as much as a small VM every month; in standard object storage, less than a team lunch; in archive class, less than a coffee.
- **The countervailing numbers are retrieval fees and restore latency.** Archive classes charge for reads and can take hours to restore, so restore time belongs in the tiering test, not just in the price list. Anything you might need quickly — the previous index generation inside its rollback window above all — belongs in a tier with minute-scale access. A tier that cannot hand back your rollback copy for four hours has priced away the thing you were paying for.
- **Tiering rules for corpora and indexes follow the truth/derived split.** Bronze and silver, the truth, are never deleted without an explicit retention decision under governance policy. Derived copies follow the rollback window: keep the previous generation hot until the window closes, then cold, then delete. Lifecycle policies automate the transitions, so no calendar reminder owns the bill. *Worked case:* a team keeps six embedding generations hot just in case, **400 GB each** in premium storage, and a bill audit shows **seventy percent of the storage line serves zero queries**. Archive the four past the rollback window and the line item falls by **about two-thirds**, for a one-time migration.
- **Every value in the system answers one question: compute at write time and store, or compute at read time on demand.** Embeddings, chunk text, enrichment summaries and BM25 statistics are precomputed. Conversational rewrites and reranker scores are computed per request because they depend on the query — which is exactly why the retrieval funnel exists. Answer caches precompute the response for known frequent questions.
- **The decision framework is four questions.** *Do the inputs change per request?* Query-dependent values cannot be precomputed at all. *Is the value space sparse?* Precomputing a join of every user with every document materialises mostly unread rows. *Is the value reused?* A value read a thousand times between writes amortises its write cost easily. *How tight is the read latency budget?* The serving path's hundred-millisecond stages leave no room for on-read generation.
- **Break-even arithmetic prices both sides.** Precompute costs write compute plus storage plus invalidation maintenance; compute-on-read costs reads times per-read compute. Contextual chunk summaries for two million chunks are one batch job plus a few hundred megabytes, versus a generation call per query forever — precompute, obviously. An answer cache for the thousand most frequent questions is precompute; the long tail stays compute-on-read. *The marginal case is the instructive one:* a per-tenant usage dashboard read **40,000 times a day** at **300 ms** of warehouse compute per read burns about **3.3 hours of compute daily**, while a precomputed table refreshed hourly is a **two-minute build twenty-four times a day — under an hour** — plus trivial storage. Past roughly a few thousand reads a day precompute wins; below that, the build and its invalidation logic cost more than they save. Materialized views are the warehouse's managed version of the same trade.
- **Vector memory is the dominant ANN cost driver, and quantization is the lever.** At 768 dimensions in float32 one vector is **3 KB**, so ten million chunks are **30 GB of serving memory**. Scalar quantization to int8 cuts that fourfold for a typical one-to-two-point recall loss at top-10. Product quantization cuts eight- to thirty-two-fold for a three-to-ten-point loss unassisted. Binary quantization cuts thirty-two-fold and needs overfetch and rescore to stay accurate. The inverted side carries the same family of index compression trades: variable-byte postings compression, blocked layouts, and one-byte learned-sparse weights.
- **Rescore is what makes compression nearly free.** The compressed index does recall; full-precision vectors, kept once, rescore only the top few hundred candidates. Recall loss shrinks to noise for a few percent extra compute. **The full-precision rescore copy is never itself quantized** — it exists to be exact.
- **Recall per dollar is a measured frontier, not a guess.** Measure recall@k against exact search on a sample for every configuration, price each one's memory, then plot recall against cost. A representative frontier at ten million vectors: float32 **100% / 30 GB**; int8 **99% / 7.5 GB**; PQ16 **95% / 1.9 GB**; PQ16 with rescore **99.3% / 2 GB** plus five percent latency. Pick the cheapest configuration above your recall floor. **Memory saved is replicas saved** — dropping a working set from 30 GB to 8 GB can halve a serving fleet, which is why this is a finance discussion conducted in retrieval metrics. *Scenario:* growth from five to twelve million vectors would force a fleet doubling; int8 with rescore keeps the fleet flat, with recall@10 within half a point of exact, verified on the golden set before cutover.
- **A full re-embedding pass is the largest recurring compute decision in the backbone**: corpus tokens times the per-token price, plus the index rebuild, plus validation and cutover risk. An **800-million-token** corpus runs to hundreds or thousands of dollars per pass before any of the rebuild work.
- **Re-embedding cadence is set by four triggers, never by a date.** A **measurably better model** — measurable meaning a golden-set gain above threshold on your queries, per class and paired, not the vendor's benchmark chart. **Drift**: score distributions and quality metrics degrading on current traffic. **Corpus shape change**: a major new content type the current model handles badly, such as a product line that doubles the corpus with tables and scanned documents. And **generation hygiene**: three model generations coexisting across tenants, new documents embedding on v4 while backfill stayed on v2, until `which model produced this vector` takes an afternoon to answer. That last rebuild is not buying quality; it is buying back the ability to reason about the system. The anti-triggers matter as much: benchmark hype without golden-set confirmation, a single loud incident (fix the incident, not the model), and calendar cadence.
- **The value math decides, and it is short.** Expected gain times traffic times value per better answer, minus rebuild cost, minus risk cost. Three nDCG points on **100,000 daily queries**, lifting hit rate **1.5%**, is **1,500 better answers a day**; a **two-thousand-dollar** rebuild plus a day of engineering pays back within days. A **0.4-point** gain never pays back and should be declined politely.
- **Execution is incremental and segment-aware.** New and changed documents embed on the new model immediately, so new traffic gains at once; the backfill follows priority, because query logs show which **ten percent of chunks serve seventy percent of traffic**. The alias swaps only when coverage and golden-set gates pass. *Scenario:* a vendor launch claims eight points on public benchmarks, your golden set shows **1.2 overall but five on the multilingual class, eighteen percent of traffic and growing** — so you re-embed multilingual first, class-gated, with the full swap two weeks later.

**Rules to remember**

- Tier by measured access, and test restore time as well as price.
- The truth is never deleted by a lifecycle rule; derived copies follow the rollback window — hot, then cold, then gone.
- Query-dependent values cannot be precomputed. That is a constraint, not a tradeoff.
- Precompute pays when reads between writes are many, and the crossover moves with your refresh cadence.
- A precomputed value with no invalidation contract is the most expensive object in the system.
- Compression is measured against exact search, per configuration, on your own data.
- Rescore first, then compress harder, and keep one unquantized copy.
- Memory saved is replicas saved. That is where compression turns into money.
- Any quantization change is a retrieval-path change and goes through the golden-set canary.
- The rebuild trigger is evidence, not the calendar and not a benchmark chart.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Letting hot storage become the graveyard for derived copies you were afraid to delete.** Six generations at 400 GB each, and seventy percent of the line answers nothing.
- **Tiering on price alone.** Archive charges per read and restores in hours, so a rollback copy parked there is not a rollback copy.
- **Precomputing values whose inputs churn.** You buy invalidation storms and stale outputs, and the maintenance never appears in the estimate.
- **Computing on read what most of traffic repeats.** You pay per query, forever, for what one batch job buys once.
- **Reading a quantization benchmark instead of measuring one.** Quantization interacts with the embedding model — some are trained to quantize well — so the frontier is yours or it is fiction.
- **Tuning IVF probe depth and quantization level separately.** They trade recall against latency together; sweeping either alone finds a local optimum and reports it as the frontier.
- **Re-embedding on a calendar, on benchmark hype, or after one loud incident.**

**How to approach the questions**

1. Ask what the access pattern is before arguing about a tier. "It might be needed" is not an access pattern; "served on the rollback path within minutes" is.
2. For precompute questions, set reads per day times per-read compute against build cost times build frequency. If the value is query-dependent, stop: there is no arithmetic to do.
3. For a compression question, read off the recall floor first, then take the cheapest configuration that clears it. A configuration with no measured recall is not on the frontier at all.
4. Convert memory into replicas before calling a saving real. Gigabytes are an intermediate; instances are the bill.
5. For rebuild questions, separate the trigger from the payback: an unjustified trigger kills the proposal however good the arithmetic, and a gain reported per class beats one reported overall.
6. Numbers quoted from a vendor benchmark, a calendar, or a single incident are distractors by construction.
