1. Where The Money Sits

Same backbone as lesson 1, same instruments as lesson 2 — one new question laid over the top. Flip the question, then tap a stage to see what changes about it. Like walking your own house twice: once asking is everything working, once asking what is running the meter.

Notice that the two questions never disagree about the shape of the system — the truth stays the truth and derived copies stay rebuildable. What changes is which property you are optimising. Every lever in this lesson is applied to a derived copy, and that is exactly why they are safe to pull: a wrong tier, a wrong quantization level or a wrong precompute is recoverable by a rebuild. Pull the same levers on bronze and you have deleted evidence.

2. The Tiering Ledger

Six embedding generations, 400 GB each, kept "just in case". Tap a generation to move it hot → warm → cold and watch the line item move with it — then watch what you broke. Like a fridge, a cupboard and a deep freezer: the freezer is nearly free, and it is the wrong place for tonight's dinner.

The judgment: tier by measured access, and test restore time, not just the price list. The bill audit in the scenario found seventy percent of the storage line serving zero queries — but the fix is not "archive everything old", it is a lifecycle policy that follows the rollback window, so that no calendar reminder owns the bill. And note which rule does not apply here: bronze and silver are the truth, and no lifecycle rule may delete them without an explicit retention decision.

3. Precompute Break-Even

A per-tenant usage dashboard costs 300 ms of warehouse compute per read. Drag how often it is read and how often you would rebuild it — then flip the four framework questions and watch them overrule the arithmetic. Like cooking to order versus batch-cooking on Sunday: the answer is just how many people are eating, until somebody wants their steak done differently.

The failure modes are symmetric, and the second one is worse than it looks. Precomputing values whose inputs churn buys invalidation storms and stale outputs; computing on read what most of traffic repeats pays per query forever for what one batch job buys once. The worst outcome is neither: it is a precomputed value with no invalidation contract — lesson 1's contract discipline returning as a cost line, and as a correctness one.

4. Recall Per Dollar

Every configuration is a point on a frontier of recall against memory — and memory is really replica count. Set your recall floor, grow the corpus, and read off the cheapest configuration that still clears it. Like buying tyres: you do not want the cheapest, you want the cheapest that passes the wet-braking test.

Two expert habits. Quantization interacts with the embedding model — some are trained to quantize well — so any change here is a retrieval-path change and goes through the golden-set canary before cutover. And compression compounds with index structure: an IVF index with PQ codes has two knobs, probe depth and quantization level, that trade recall against latency together. Sweep them as a pair on the golden queries; tuning either alone finds a local optimum and reports it as the frontier.

5. Rebuild Payback Math

A vendor announces eight points. Your golden set says something else. Pick the evidence, then drag the gain and the slice of traffic it applies to, and read the payback. Like a kitchen refit: the question is never "is the new one better", it is "better by how much, for whom, and paid back in how long".

The triggers are evidence: a measurably better model on your queries, drift in the live score distributions, a corpus shape change the current model was never evaluated on, and generation hygiene — the quarter where three model generations coexist and "which model produced this vector" takes an afternoon to answer. That last rebuild is not buying quality; it is buying back the ability to reason about the system. The anti-triggers are benchmark hype, one loud incident (fix the incident, not the model), and the calendar.
