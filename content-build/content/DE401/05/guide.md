The curriculum ends where operations begin: with a symptom, a number that moved, or a reviewer asking why the system is built this way. This lesson practises three capstone skills. Triage maps any symptom to the layer that caused it, using cheap checks in the right order. Latency work decomposes the budget and spends effort only on the binding stage. Design defense justifies every architectural choice with constraints, measurements, and a stated condition that would change the decision. Nothing here is new material — everything here is the curriculum under pressure.

**Key concepts**

- **Symptom-to-layer triage: mapping failures to pipeline stages is a lookup, not an investigation.** Symptoms appear at the top of the system — bad answers, stale answers, missing documents, slow answers — but they are *caused* at a specific layer, and the cost of triage done wrong is fixing the wrong one. The table is the curriculum compressed: wrong-but-fluent answer → grounding, retrieval or generation, first check claim verification and context recall on that query; document missing → ingestion, parse, chunk, rank, filters, rerank, first check the chain of custody; stale answers → ingestion, pipeline, index visibility, first check per-stage watermarks; slow answers → retrieval, rerank, generation, first check latency decomposition; cross-tenant leak → gateway, filter, cache, response, first check which layer saw the foreign tenant context; quality drop after a change → drift, defect or model, first check timestamp alignment and canaries.
- **Each candidate layer gets one cheap check, and checks run in cost order.** Dashboards first, direct index queries second, log archaeology last. `Answer is wrong but fluent` splits three ways: grounding (verify claims against the context), retrieval (context recall on that exact query), generation (does the response address the question asked). Without the ordering you pay log-archaeology prices for a dashboard-priced answer.
- **The chain-of-custody walk, with real numbers.** Users report the new refund policy never appears in answers. The trace ID from the answer contract follows one failing query end to end. Raw landing shows the PDF arrived Monday 09:00. Silver shows the parsed document intact. A chunk-text search finds the policy sentence — parsing and chunking cleared **in two minutes**. A direct index query for `refund policy extension` ranks the chunk at **34**. Funnel attrition shows it entering the rerank set, since BM25 at **34** and dense at **180** fuse to roughly **46**, inside the depth of **50**. The reranker orders it **11th**, and the context budget admits **8**. Layer found: context budget and rerank position — not retrieval, not ingestion.
- **Mis-triage is measured in weeks.** The correct fix above is an afternoon of depth and context-k tuning. The wrong fix — re-embedding the corpus — costs a week and changes nothing, because the evidence was reaching the system all along. The habit that prevents it: before touching any layer, name the evidence that would convict it.
- **A shorter scenario, same discipline.** Spanish-language queries suddenly return English documents. The index is untouched, canaries are green, and the deployment log shows one change: a new rewriter prompt that dropped the language-preservation instruction. Diffing rewrite outputs before and after confirms it in one check. The defect lived in the query-presentation layer; every hour spent suspecting the index would have been wasted.
- **Latency budget breakdown: retrieval, rerank, generation is an invariant method.** Decompose into stages, measure p50 and p95 per stage, identify the one *binding* stage, apply that stage's levers, re-verify — because the bottleneck moves after every fix.
- **Scenario A, a retrieval-bound system.** p95 climbs from **3.8 s to 6.1 s** over two weeks. Decomposition shows retrieval p95 up from **0.45 s to 1.9 s** while rerank and time-to-first-token are flat. The corpus grew **forty percent**, the ANN probe count was never revisited, and candidates now fail ACL filters more often, forcing deeper fetches. Retrieval levers are probe depth, pruning, tenant sharding to shrink each graph, and hot-query caching. Sharding plus a probe increase restores retrieval to **0.6 s**. Three of the four plausible fixes targeted stages that were not binding.
- **Scenario B, a prefill problem wearing a quality costume.** Time-to-first-token doubles after a quality initiative raised context from **8 chunks to 16**; input tokens rose from **4,000 to 9,000**, and prefill cost tracks input size. Generation levers are context size, prompt compression and model routing. The resolution keeps the quality: chunk count returns to **10** with a reranker score threshold doing the filtering that raw volume was doing, chunk wrappers are stripped of redundant metadata, and simple query classes route to a smaller model — and every change passes the faithfulness and relevance gates. A latency fix that spends quality is a trade, not a fix.
- **Scenario C, decode-bound.** p95 sits at **12 s** because the prompt requests detailed explanations and answers average **900 tokens**. The lever is the output budget: concise-by-default generation with user-expandable detail, a **400-token** cap, and streaming that puts first token at **1.5 s**. Perceived latency collapses while total compute barely changes.
- **Each stage has its own lever set and its own signature when binding.** Retrieval: probe depth, pruning, sharding, caching — its p95 grows with corpus size. Rerank: depth, batch size, accelerator pool — its p95 grows with depth and queueing. Generation: tokens in, tokens out, model size, routing — TTFT tracks input size and decode tracks output size.
- **Queueing belongs in every latency review.** A stage's p95 includes time spent waiting for a worker, not just working. A stage whose p50 is flat while its p95 climbs is usually *saturated*, not slow, and the fix is capacity or admission control, not optimisation. Plenty of real incidents are a reranker pool one replica short at peak.
- **Design review defense: justifying choices under constraints has a fixed format.** State the constraint set, name the forces in tension, show where the chosen design sits on the tradeoff frontier, and give each rejected alternative its rejection reason. `Why not drop the index and use long context?` — two million chunks cannot ride the prompt, per-query input cost multiplies by **twenty or more**, prefill takes seconds, freshness becomes snapshot-bound, and needle recall degrades with context length; long context is admitted where it wins, nightly whole-corpus synthesis. `Why RRF instead of learned fusion?` — no labels exist to train on, ranks do not drift when models are swapped, the reranker already owns final ordering, and the fusion stage is a contract boundary, so the decision is reversible. `Why per-tenant namespaces instead of per-tenant indexes?` — **three hundred tenants** make per-index operations unsupportable, and gateway-injected tenant context plus response-layer re-verification close the isolation gap, with a documented graduation path for regulated tenants. `Why is quarterly re-embedding not the policy?` — because the triggers are evidence, not dates: a golden-set gain, measured drift, a corpus shape change.
- **Every defense ends with its reversal condition.** If multilingual traffic crosses **thirty percent**, re-embed on the newer model. If retrieval p95 crosses **800 ms**, shard. One transform codebase serves batch and streaming because two parse logics produce two chunk sets from the same document and the divergence surfaces mid-incident — and it gets re-proposed if streaming compute cost ever exceeds the incident budget it protects, with a conformance test suite as the price of admission.

**Rules to remember**

- Symptoms surface at the top; causes live at a layer. Name the layer before naming the fix.
- Every candidate layer has one cheap check. Run checks in cost order: dashboards, then direct index queries, then logs.
- Before touching a layer, state the evidence that would convict it. Refuse work on layers already cleared.
- Decompose before optimising. Work on a non-binding stage buys zero user-visible gain.
- Budget at p95, because users live in the tail — and re-run the decomposition after every change.
- Flat p50 with a climbing p95 means saturation. The lever is capacity or admission control.
- TTFT tracks tokens in; decode tracks tokens out. Two different problems with two different fixes.
- A defense is constraints, measurements, rejected alternatives with reasons, and a stated reversal condition. Anything less is preference.
- The reviewer's checklist: constraints listed explicitly, alternatives considered with rejection reasons, blast radius bounded, rollback rehearsed, observability named, unit cost computed.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Re-embedding as a reflex.** It is the most expensive move in the playbook and it fixes nothing when the evidence was already reaching the reranker — a week spent instead of an afternoon.
- **Suspecting the index because the index is interesting.** Green canaries plus an untouched index plus one deployment-log entry points at the query-presentation layer, and the Spanish-query incident was one diff away.
- **Optimising a non-binding stage.** In Scenario A, rerank and TTFT were flat; work on them shows up as exactly zero seconds.
- **Reading a mean instead of a tail.** A healthy p50 hides the p95 users live in, and hides queueing entirely.
- **Buying latency with quality.** Cutting context without a reranker score threshold drops faithfulness; the gates exist to catch precisely that.
- **Confusing perceived latency with compute.** Streaming at 1.5 s and a 400-token cap transform the experience while total compute barely moves.
- **Fixing once and declaring victory.** The bottleneck moves to wherever you just made room; the decomposition has to be re-run.
- **Defending a design with preference.** "We prefer RRF" loses the review; "no labels, ranks stable under model swaps, reranker owns final ordering, and the stage is a reversible contract boundary" wins it.
- **Omitting the reversal condition.** A decision with no stated condition that would change it cannot be audited later, and reads as dogma.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Read the symptom first and map it to candidate layers before reading the proposed fixes. The distractors are almost always plausible fixes at the wrong layer.
2. Track what the scenario has already *cleared*. If chunk text is found and the direct index query returns the chunk, ingestion, parsing and chunking are out — and any answer that touches them is wrong however reasonable it sounds.
3. For latency questions, find the stage that moved. Flat rerank and flat TTFT with retrieval at 1.9 s means the answer is a retrieval lever, not a generation one.
4. Distinguish tokens in from tokens out. TTFT doubling after a context increase is prefill; a 12-second p95 with 900-token answers is decode.
5. When p50 is flat and p95 climbs, answer capacity or admission control, not algorithmic optimisation.
6. For design-review questions, pick the option that cites a constraint, a measurement and a rejection reason — and prefer the one that also names what would reverse it.
7. Watch for calendar-driven policies. "Quarterly" is a distractor whenever an evidence trigger (golden-set gain, measured drift, corpus shape change) is available.
