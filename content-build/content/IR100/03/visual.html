1. How Much Order

Hash Map vs Sorted Structure: When Order Matters gave you two extremes — O(1) lookup and no order, or complete order at a price. The heap is the middle: enough order to name the weakest winner, and nothing else. Tap an operation and watch which promise pays. Like a to-do list: a pile you can search, a list sorted end to end, or just knowing which job is least urgent.

Read the last two rows together and the whole lesson is there. The sorted structure answers "the best 100" instantly — but it paid ~20 million comparison units to get sorted, and it holds all 1,000,000 records. The heap answers the same question in one pass while retaining 100. A promise you never make is a promise you never pay for.

Heap validity is cheap to assert after any custom implementation: for every index i > 0 the parent key must not exceed the child key. That O(n) sweep catches wrong parent arithmetic and incomplete sifting — including the comparator-direction bug that silently retains the bottom k instead of the top k, which raises no error and simply returns wrong results.

3. The Admission Threshold

Cap the heap at k and its root becomes the cutoff: the weakest current winner, one lookup away. Set k, then walk the stream [8, 2, 5, 10, 7, 1] and watch each candidate get admitted or refused. Like a club with a fixed capacity where the doorman only has to know who inside is the least impressive.

Two things this pattern needs in production. Ties need an explicit deterministic rule — compare tuples such as (score, -doc_id) in the heap and in the output, or traversal order and shard timing decide which equal-score document survives and pagination stops being reproducible. And shards repeat the pattern: each keeps its local top k, a coordinator merges them. That is provably sufficient for pure score ordering — a document outside its own shard's top k has at least k documents in that shard alone above it — but not once a later stage applies diversity, quotas or a different reranker. Then shards must overfetch.

4. Heap Versus Full Sort

Full sort is O(n log n) and retains all n. The bounded heap is O(n log k) and retains k. Drag both dials and watch the gap open — and close again as k approaches n. Like ranking every runner in a marathon versus just holding the podium.

The heap does not hand back a ranked list — its root is the weakest winner and parent-child relations hold, but arbitrary positions are unordered. Sorting the winners costs O(k log k), so the honest combined bound is O(n log k + k log k), dominated by the first term whenever n is much larger than k. Quote the second term when the question asks for the output.

5. Choosing The Algorithm

Three tools, and the deciding question is never "which is fastest?" — it is how much order must the consumer actually receive, and how does the data arrive. Pick a scenario. Like choosing between reading the whole leaderboard, finding the cut line, or standing at the door with a clipboard.

The bounded heap's real distinction is that it is online: it never needs to see a future candidate, so it works on a stream larger than RAM. Quickselect and full sort both need the whole input resident and mutable. Read a question for the words "stream", "iterator" or "larger than memory" — they settle the answer before any asymptotics are compared.
