**The big idea**: **The Inverted Index: Dictionary and Postings** gave you a dictionary of terms each pointing at a postings list, **Boolean Query Processing by Intersecting Postings** walked two of those lists in step, **Query Optimization: Processing Rarest Terms First** made that walk cheap, and **Term-Document Incidence and Why Matrices Do Not Scale** said why the list had to exist. Then **Tokenization Choices and Their Retrieval Consequences**, **When Stemming Helps Recall and Hurts Precision**, **Normalization: Case, Accents, and Equivalence Classes** and especially **Stop Words: Space Savings vs Phrase-Query Damage** fixed which keys those lists are filed under — while insisting that token positions were an output you would need later. This is later. Document-level postings answer whether terms occur; they cannot say whether `cloud run` appears as a phrase, whether two concepts occur near each other, or where to highlight. Positional indexes keep within-document order and answer all three, at substantial storage cost. Skip pointers solve a different problem — comparisons while intersecting long document-ID lists. Both are one bargain: extra bytes at construction, against less or richer work at query time.

**Key concepts**

- **A positional posting stores *where*, not just *whether*.** A non-positional posting needs a document ID and perhaps a term frequency. A positional posting adds the ordered token positions of the term in that document: `retrieval -> [(doc=4, tf=2, positions=[7,31]), (doc=9, tf=1, positions=[12])]`. Positions come from the analyzer, a direct output of the pipeline you just studied: if document 4 tokenizes as `hybrid retrieval improves retrieval quality`, `retrieval` sits at 2 and 4 under one-based numbering. Character offsets are separate metadata for exact highlighting; token positions express order and distance. Term frequency is implied by the position count, though formats often store it anyway so a decoder knows how many values to read.
- **Positional indexes and their size cost, derived.** Let $T$ be total indexed token occurrences and $P$ total distinct term-document pairs. Document-only postings hold about one entry per pair, so $O(P)$; positions hold one value per occurrence, so $O(T)$. A term can repeat inside a document and never occurs fewer than once, so $T \ge P$, often by a wide margin. Worked scale: one million documents averaging 500 indexed tokens gives $T = 500$ million positions; 300 distinct terms per document gives $P = 300$ million pairs. Raw 32-bit positions alone are $4 \times 5 \times 10^{8} = 2$ GB on top of the document-level entries. Gap encoding shrinks it (`[7,31,35]` becomes `[7,24,4]`) but never makes it free.
- **The layout is nested, which is what lets a reader opt out.** Document-ID gaps run across the postings list; position gaps reset inside each document. Blocks and offsets let a reader skip the positional payload when scoring does not need it, so positions do not tax every bag-of-words query on the field.
- **Analyzer behaviour determines positional truth.** Remove stop words from `state of the art` and the retained tokens must keep `state:1`, `art:4`; renumber them 1 and 2 and the index has manufactured the phrase `state art`. Synonyms at one position represent alternatives honestly, but multiword expansion needs position lengths or graph-aware analysis, or it invents phrase paths.
- **Index options are a per-field decision.** A title can afford positions — phrases matter there and the text is short. A tag field with one unordered value per tag needs document presence only. A large body field might keep frequencies but omit positions if the product will never do phrases, proximity or highlighting. Measure positional bytes *by field* against the query classes they buy, never as a whole-index percentage.
- **Positions enable proximity, not meaning.** Terms within five tokens and passage-aware scoring come free, but two terms adjacent across a sentence boundary may be less related than the distance suggests, and token distance is not comparable across languages. Conventions must also survive segment boundaries: store the index option and analyzer version in segment metadata and reject incompatible merges, or identical source phrases behave differently by ingestion date — a failure index-size monitoring never surfaces.
- **Phrase query evaluation with positions has two stages.** For `"vector database"`: intersect the document-ID postings — the intersection you already know; then, inside each candidate document, intersect the positional lists with the required offsets. Stage one prunes, stage two verifies.
- **The positional merge is the document merge one level down.** In doc 7, `vector` is at `[2, 10, 20]` and `database` at `[3, 12, 21]`. Seek pairs where `database_position = vector_position + 1`: matches at `(2,3)` and `(20,21)`, while `(10,12)` has a one-token gap and fails at slop zero. Hold a cursor in each sorted list and compare $p_v + 1$ with $p_d$ — equal, record and advance; $p_v + 1 < p_d$, advance `vector`; otherwise advance `database`. Work is linear in the two list lengths.
- **Longer phrases anchor and offset.** For `"neural vector database"`, if `neural` is at $p$, require `vector` at $p+1$ and `database` at $p+2$; implementations merge matches progressively or hold several cursors, keeping phrase start positions for scoring. Worked document `neural vector database and vector database` gives `neural:[1]`, `vector:[2,5]`, `database:[3,6]`: `"vector database"` matches at starts 2 and 5, `"neural vector database"` at start 1 only. Boolean AND reports all three terms present and cannot tell those arrangements apart — exactly why positions exist.
- **Slop and proximity relax adjacency, with engine-specific semantics.** `vector NEAR/5 database` examines distance rather than an exact offset. Some engines preserve order under slop, some allow transposition; because definitions differ, product code must test its own engine.
- **Repeated and removed terms break naive matchers.** `"to be or not to be"` holds `to` and `be` twice, so the algorithm must match complete positional sequences without reusing an occurrence. And if stop-word removal deleted `to` and `be`, exact verification is impossible however faithfully gaps preserved distance.
- **Phrase cost depends on document frequency and within-document frequency together.** Common terms give many candidate documents *and* many positional combinations inside each, so a rare anchor is what collapses the positional work — though every phrase term's positions are still needed to verify. Boundaries need explicit gaps: index fields separately, or insert a position gap no phrase or slop window can span, so a match never runs from title into body.
- **Skip pointers: faster intersection for extra storage.** Basic intersection advances one document ID at a time, so when one cursor is far behind, many comparisons merely confirm that intermediate IDs are too small. A skip pointer is a shortcut from one posting to a later posting, labelled with its destination document ID. With `A = [2,4,8,16,32,64,128]` and `B = [2,128]`: after matching 2, B sits at 128 and A at 4, so a plain merge compares 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64 on the way. Give A a skip from 4 to 32 and another from 32 to 128 and it arrives in two moves.
- **The correctness rule is one inequality.** Take a skip only when its destination cannot leap past a possible match: during AND, if A's skip destination is $\le B[j]$, advancing discards only IDs no greater than the target and equality is still testable at the destination. If it exceeds $B[j]$, refuse — A may contain $B[j]$ before it. Everything else about skips is performance; this part produces wrong answers.
- **Spacing is a heuristic: about $\sqrt{L}$ skips in a list of length $L$.** With $k$ evenly spaced skips a traversal costs roughly $L/k$ sequential steps plus about $k$ skip tests, and $L/k + k$ is minimised at $k = \sqrt{L}$ — so a million-entry list wants about 1,000 skips spanning about 1,000 postings each. More skips mean shorter jumps but more bytes and branch checks. Modern formats often store block-level maximum IDs instead: the same principle at compressed-block granularity.
- **The benefit comes from asymmetry, not length.** A million-entry common-term list against a thousand-entry rare list is the good case: skips, galloping search or binary search seek toward each rare candidate instead of scanning large regions. Two similarly dense lists with frequent matches offer few jumps, and sequential decoding may beat testing skips; evenly spaced skips also disappoint when IDs cluster. Skips improve traversal, not asymptotics: two identical million-entry lists must emit a million results, so the work stays $\Omega(1{,}000{,}000)$, and adversarial layouts leave it $O(m+n)$.
- **Updates are why skips live in immutable segments.** Inserting document IDs into a mutable list invalidates spacing and destinations; immutable segments build skips once after postings are finalised, and merges rebuild them.

**Rules to remember**

- Positions are $O(T)$ over occurrences, document postings $O(P)$ over term-document pairs, and $T \ge P$ always.
- Positions answer order, distance and highlighting. Nothing else in the index can.
- Gap-encode positions within a document and document IDs across a list; the two streams are separate.
- Never renumber positions after removing tokens — preserved gaps are what keep invented phrases out.
- Phrase evaluation is intersect-then-verify: document IDs first, positions inside each survivor.
- Two-term adjacency is $p_2 = p_1 + 1$; an n-term phrase is offsets $p, p+1, \dots, p+n-1$ from an anchor.
- Slop, NEAR semantics and transposition rules are engine-specific. Verify, do not assume.
- Take a skip only if its destination is at or below the other list's current ID.
- Roughly $\sqrt{L}$ skips for a list of length $L$; they pay on unequal lists and nothing on equal dense ones, and they reduce comparisons, never the size of the output.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Indexing positions on every field "just in case".** The bill — storage, build time, merge I/O, cache pressure — is permanent, while the query classes justifying it may not exist in the product.
- **Compressing positions after stop-word removal.** `state:1, art:2` invents `"state art"`. Correct gaps do not restore removed terms, but they stop the index lying.
- **Reading a Boolean AND result as a phrase result.** All three terms occur in `neural vector database and vector database` under every arrangement; only positions separate those.
- **Assuming slop means the same everywhere.** Order preservation and transposition differ by engine, so a phrase feature ported between stacks changes behaviour silently.
- **Letting a phrase cross a field or array boundary.** Flattened positions make title-then-body look adjacent; separate fields or a large gap is the fix.
- **Taking a skip whose destination overshoots the other cursor.** That is not slow, it is wrong: the skipped region can hold the match.
- **Fine-grained skips on short or mutable lists.** The metadata can cost more than the comparisons it saves, random reads can lose to a sequential decoder, and insertions invalidate spacing.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Ask what the query needs: presence, order, distance, or an exact highlight. That alone decides whether positions are required, optional, or waste.
2. For size questions, separate occurrences from pairs. Given tokens per document and distinct terms per document, you are comparing $T$ with $P$.
3. For phrase questions, write the position lists out and check offsets by hand. Most distractors share a document but not an adjacency.
4. Treat any mention of stop words, stemming or synonyms in a phrase scenario as the defect under test — the analyzer decided the answer before the query ran.
5. For skip questions apply the destination rule literally. A skip landing beyond the other list's current ID must be refused, and any option claiming a speed-up from taking it is wrong.
6. Ask whether the two lists are lopsided, and distrust absolutes — "positions are too expensive", "skips make intersection sublinear". Both are false as stated; honest answers are per-field and per-workload.
