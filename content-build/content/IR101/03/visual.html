1. Terms Or Places

The Inverted Index: Dictionary and Postings and Boolean Query Processing by Intersecting Postings can tell you a document holds every term. They cannot tell you the terms are next to each other. Flip the index and ask the same five questions. Like a guest list versus a seating plan: one says who came, the other says who sat together.

Note what the analyzer already decided. Stop Words: Space Savings vs Phrase-Query Damage warned that dropping and is only safe if the retained tokens keep their positions — vector:[2,5], database:[3,6]. Renumber them and the index invents phrases nobody wrote. Positions are an output of the analyzer, not a separate feature.

2. The Price Of Positions

Document postings are counted over term-document pairs; positions are counted over token occurrences. Drag the collection and watch the two counts separate. Like the difference between a guest list and a photo of every time each guest spoke.

The judgment is per field, not per index. A title can afford positions; a tag field with one unordered value per tag needs presence only; a huge body field may keep frequencies and drop positions if the product will never run a phrase, a proximity query or a highlight. Measure positional bytes by field against the query classes they buy — one verbose field can dominate the whole number.

3. Marching The Positions

Inside one candidate document, phrase verification is the postings intersection you already know — one level down, over positions instead of document IDs. Step the cursors and move the slop. Like two people walking separate rows of seats, calling out whenever they are side by side.

Two traps this walk hides. Repeated terms: a phrase like "to be or not to be" uses to and be twice, so the matcher must consume complete sequences instead of reusing one occurrence for two slots. And removed terms cannot be verified at all — if a stop list deleted to and be, no gap arithmetic brings the postings back. Cost follows both document frequency and within-document frequency, which is why a rare anchor term is what actually collapses the work.

4. Taking A Skip

A skip pointer is a shortcut from one posting to a later one, labelled with its destination document ID. It is bought with bytes and it comes with one rule you cannot break. Change the opposing list and watch the same skip become legal or illegal. Like an express train: brilliant, unless it runs past your stop.

Everything else about skips is performance; this is the part that can return the wrong answer. Note too that the skip destinations are baked into the list: inserting a document ID would invalidate the spacing and the targets, which is why skips are built once over finalised postings inside immutable segments and rebuilt when segments merge.

5. Sizing The Skips

How many skips should a million-entry list carry? Too few and the jumps are useless; too many and you pay bytes and branch checks to avoid work that was cheap. Drag the count, then switch the workload and watch the whole argument evaporate. Like motorway exits: one per mile is clutter, one per county is no help.

Evenly spaced skips are a heuristic, not a law: if document IDs cluster, uniform spacing jumps over the sparse regions and crawls the dense ones. Hierarchical skips serve both long and short jumps at the cost of more metadata, and modern formats often skip a level of indirection entirely by storing a maximum document ID per compressed block — the same principle at block granularity, where decoding one small block can be cheaper than navigating fine-grained pointers.
