Notice which number barely moves. Sort work is O(T log M) — the logarithm is over the block, not the corpus — so shrinking M by 50× changes comparisons by about a fifth while changing peak memory by 50×. That asymmetry is the whole reason blocked construction exists: you buy a hard memory bound almost for free, and pay for it in runs to merge and temporary storage.

2. Build Runs, Merge Runs

Three sorted runs go in, one dictionary comes out. Step the merge and watch equal terms from different runs meet, their already-sorted postings joining into one list. Like three alphabetised card boxes being combined into one: you only ever look at the front card of each.

The final writer records dictionary offsets and encodes document and position gaps — the next lesson. What it cannot do is recognise semantic duplicates: if the same document was ingested twice under two IDs, the merge dutifully produces two postings. That is a job for stable source IDs and build policy, and it is why the manifest carries each run's input scope, checksum, record counts, term range and analyzer version.

3. The Binary Counter

Logarithmic merge keeps immutable indexes whose capacities double: B, 2B, 4B… Add a batch and the carries cascade exactly like adding one to a binary number. B = 1,000 documents. Like an odometer: most clicks move one wheel, and occasionally the whole row rolls over.

Write amplification here is the price of not rebuilding: a document is rewritten at most once per level as merges carry it upward, which is O(log N) — against O(N) if every batch rebuilt the corpus. The knobs are the size ratio, level versus tier strategy, merge concurrency and throttling; the things to monitor are segment count, tombstone ratio, merge backlog, bytes written per source byte, and query latency.

4. Segments And Tombstones

Merging buys read speed with write I/O, and a delete is not gone until a merge sees both the tombstone and the data it suppresses. Move the policy, then switch off generation ordering and watch a deleted document come back. Like crossing a name off a list in pencil: it is still on the page until somebody retypes the list.

Concurrency rests on generation IDs too: a merge snapshots its inputs, documents arriving meanwhile land in another segment and are not lost, and at commit you verify the inputs are still live before publishing the new manifest. Old segments are garbage-collected only once no reader references them — which is exactly why nothing is ever edited in place.

5. Rebuild Or Merge

Corpus size S, daily changed documents Δ, merge write amplification W. Incremental costs about WΔ a day; a rebuild costs about S. Then tick a trigger and watch the arithmetic stop mattering. Like repainting one wall versus the whole house — until you change the colour, and then it is the whole house whatever the paint costs.

The professional default is hybrid, and the closing question is the useful one: what failure should the system heal automatically? A rebuild heals historical index state from the source. A merge preserves existing state efficiently — including any undetected mistake in it. Either way, stable IDs, deterministic analysis, manifests, counts, golden queries and rollback versions are what make this a reversible engineering choice rather than a leap of faith.
