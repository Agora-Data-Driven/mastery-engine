**The big idea**: IR 101 built the machine that answers a query — **The Inverted Index: Dictionary and Postings**, **Boolean Query Processing by Intersecting Postings**, **Index Construction at Scale** — and every one of those lessons took the document collection as given. This course is about where that collection comes from: a retrieval system can only rank what acquisition brings home, and your BM25 statistics, embedding space and reranker training signal are all downstream of the crawler. Crawler architecture decides *which* URLs get fetched, *when*, and *at what cost to whom*. Get it wrong and you get one of two classic failures: a corpus of junk aliases and trap pages, or a crawler banned from the sources that matter most. Three components do the work — the frontier, the politeness layer, and the URL hygiene that keeps both honest.

**Key concepts**

- **The crawl frontier holds every URL discovered but not yet fetched — the to-do list and the scheduler at once.** The loop is: pop a URL, fetch it, parse the HTML, extract outlinks, canonicalize each one, run it through a **URL-seen test** (has this canonical URL ever been enqueued?), push the survivors back. Everything else in this lesson modifies that loop.
- **A naive FIFO frontier gives breadth-first search, and it breaks on growth and on locality.** Growth: a typical page yields **50–100 outlinks**, so you pop one and push tens — a serious crawl holds hundreds of millions of frontier URLs, forcing a disk-backed design with batched reads and writes. At 2,000,000 fetches/day with 60 links a page, the seen test alone runs 120 million lookups a day. Locality: most links point within the same host, so consecutive pops cluster onto a handful of hosts — and under politeness delays your workers then either hammer one server or sit idle.
- **The Mercator design splits the frontier into two tiers, and the split *is* the idea.** **Front queues** implement prioritization: a URL is routed into one of F queues by a priority score (link indegree, source importance, and later in this course change rates and quality priors). **Back queues** implement politeness: one per host, with a min-heap on each host's *earliest next allowed fetch time* deciding which one a worker draws from next; an empty back queue refills from the front queues, biased toward higher priority. **What is worth fetching** (front) and **what may be fetched right now** (back) are different questions: politeness rules short-horizon scheduling, priority rules long-horizon allocation.
- **The per-host ceiling is arithmetic, and no amount of parallelism moves it.** At one fetch per 5 seconds, $86{,}400 / 5 = 17{,}280$ fetches per day per host, so a 500,000-page site takes at least $500{,}000 / 17{,}280 \approx 29$ days however many workers you add. With 50,000 active hosts the heap does trivial work; the hot path is the URL-seen test — hundreds of millions of canonical-URL hashes a day, typically an in-memory hash of recent URLs in front of a disk-backed store.
- **Strict max-priority popping causes starvation**, so production frontiers *sample* across the priority queues weighted by priority — a mid-tier site discovered early otherwise waits forever behind higher-scored URLs. Know when not to build the full machine, too: for a scoped research corpus of thousands of vetted hosts, per-host FIFO queues under a simple host scheduler are usually right — most of Mercator's complexity exists to survive the open web. Breadth-first is also a strong early baseline, because high-indegree pages tend to be shallow.
- **robots.txt is the declarative half of politeness and lives at exactly one place per origin**, `scheme://host:port/robots.txt`, standardized as **RFC 9309 (2022)**. The file is groups of `User-agent:` lines followed by `Allow:` and `Disallow:` rules; you pick the group with the most specific matching user-agent (falling back to `*`), then apply the **longest-match rule** — among all rules whose path prefix matches, the longest matched path wins, and on a tie `Allow` wins. Patterns support `*` wildcards and `$` end-anchors. **Worked example:** against `Disallow: /search` plus `Allow: /search/about`, the path `/search/results?q=x` is disallowed (it matches `/search`, 7 characters) while `/search/about` is **allowed**, because the `Allow` rule matches 13 characters and 13 beats 7 — one file, opposite verdicts, decided by length.
- **Fetch-status semantics are asymmetric.** A **4xx** response (including 404) means "no restrictions — crawl freely". A **5xx** or unreachable robots.txt is treated conservatively as **complete disallow, temporarily** — the site is in trouble and you cannot know its policy. Cache the file (24 hours is conventional) rather than refetching per URL. **`Crawl-delay` is *not* part of RFC 9309**: a de-facto directive honoured by some crawlers (Bing historically), ignored by Google, giving seconds between requests. Honour it — it is the operator stating their capacity in plain text.
- **Rate limiting is the behavioural half — what you do when robots.txt is silent.** Keep **one open connection per host** and wait an adaptive delay proportional to observed response time, `delay ≈ k × t_response`, with k around 3–10 and a floor of about 1 second. A server answering in 200 ms with k = 10 gets one request every 2 s, so $86{,}400 / 2 = 43{,}200$ fetches/day at most; a server answering in 3 s automatically drops to one request per 30 s. On **429 or 503, honour `Retry-After`** exactly if present, else back off exponentially, and identify yourself with a descriptive `User-Agent` and a contact address.
- **Rate limits must also be clustered per IP, not only per hostname.** Shared hosting puts many hostnames on one physical server, so 12 hostnames each politely fetched at 43,200/day is 518,400 requests a day on one machine — polite per host, and still a melted server. And a ban is permanent: you lose the source *for good*, which for a research corpus can be worse than never crawling it. Politeness is mechanically voluntary and professionally binding.
- **"Slow" and "no" are different problems.** `Crawl-delay: 60` over 2,000,000 pages is 120,000,000 seconds ≈ **3.8 years**. The answer is not rotating IPs but a sanctioned bulk channel — an official API, a published dump, a sitemap-guided partial crawl, or an email to the operator.
- **A crawler trap is a region of URL space that generates unbounded unique URLs carrying worthless or duplicate content.** The classics: an events **calendar** whose "next month" link exists forever; **session IDs** minting a fresh URL universe per visit; **faceted navigation**; relative-link bugs building `/a/b/a/b/a/b/`; and **soft-404s**, returning HTTP 200 with a "not found" body so error space looks like content space. Usually accidental, occasionally adversarial, and an undefended crawler sinks a host's whole budget into one. Faceted navigation is the arithmetic case: 12 colours × 8 sizes × 20 brands × 6 sort orders × 25 pages is 288,000 URLs over a few thousand products, and widening each facet reaches millions.
- **Defenses are budget- and shape-based, and the diagnostic is a ratio.** Per-host page budgets cap a trap's cost at one host's allocation; add caps on URL **length**, path **depth** and **query-parameter count**; and monitor **unique content per URL**. When URLs fetched keeps climbing while distinct content flattens, you are in a trap or a duplicate farm, and that host's rules should tighten automatically.
- **URL canonicalization gives the seen test, the frontier and the corpus one notion of identity.** RFC 3986's transformations are *always safe* — they cannot change what the server returns: lowercase the **scheme** and **host**, remove **default ports** (`:443`, `:80`), resolve **dot-segments** (`/a/../docs/` → `/docs/`), normalize **percent-encoding** (decode unreserved characters, `%7E` → `~`; uppercase remaining hex escapes), and drop the **fragment**, which never reaches the server. Worked example: `HTTPS://Example.COM:443/a/../docs/?utm_source=news#top`, `https://example.com/docs/?utm_source=partner` and `https://example.com/docs/` all collapse to `https://example.com/docs/`.
- **Heuristic normalizations usually help and sometimes lie**: stripping tracking parameters (`utm_*`, `gclid`, `fbclid`), sorting query parameters, removing trailing slashes, collapsing duplicate slashes, dropping session IDs. Two you must **not** do blindly: lowercasing the **path**, case-sensitive on most servers (`/Docs` and `/docs` can be different resources), and stripping parameters that select content — `?page=2` and `?id=42` are content, not tracking, and stripping them collapses a forum's every thread page onto page 1, a coverage hole you find months later. Production crawlers keep **per-site parameter rules**, learned by fetching variants and comparing content. A site's own `rel="canonical"` (a `<link>` tag or HTTP `Link` header) is a **hint, not a directive** — frequently wrong or abused — so weigh it against your own content-equality evidence.
- **Fail open versus fail closed is the asymmetry to carry out of this lesson.** Under-canonicalize and the seen test fails *open*: the frontier fills with aliases and budget burns on duplicate fetches. Over-canonicalize and it fails *closed*: distinct content is declared "seen" and never fetched. The first wastes money; the second silently costs coverage, the more dangerous defect for a research corpus.

**Rules to remember**

- The frontier answers two questions: what is worth fetching (priority) and what may be fetched now (politeness). Mercator separates them physically.
- Per-host throughput is $86{,}400 / \text{delay}$ per day. Workers never raise it; only more hosts do.
- Sample across the priority queues instead of popping the max, or low-priority work starves.
- robots.txt: most specific user-agent group, then longest matching path prefix, `Allow` wins ties. 4xx means crawl freely; 5xx or unreachable means stop, temporarily. Cache ~24 hours.
- `Crawl-delay` is de facto, not RFC 9309 — honour it anyway. Otherwise the delay is `k × t_response`, k ≈ 3–10, floor ~1 s, and `Retry-After` is honoured exactly. Rate-limit per IP as well as per hostname.
- Traps are bounded by budgets and shape caps, and detected by unique content per URL.
- Safe canonicalization is RFC 3986's list. Never lowercase the path; never strip content-selecting parameters.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Treating the frontier as a queue, not a scheduler.** FIFO plus politeness produces idle workers — and **more workers never beat a crawl delay**, because the per-host ceiling is fixed.
- **Popping strictly by priority.** It looks optimal and starves mid-tier sites indefinitely. The opposite error: **building Mercator for a vetted 3,000-host corpus**, insurance against an open web you are not crawling.
- **Reading robots.txt rules first-match or shortest-match.** It is longest matched prefix, `Allow` winning ties — and a 404 robots.txt permits everything while a 503 forbids everything.
- **A fixed sleep between requests**, or **politeness measured per hostname only**. A delay proportional to response time protects a struggling server automatically; a constant does not — and shared hosting multiplies per-host politeness onto one machine. A crawler that is technically polite and still causing damage is usually this.
- **Over-aggressive normalization.** Stripping `?page=` or lowercasing the path fails the seen test *closed* and costs coverage silently — as does **trusting `rel="canonical"`** and letting a site collapse its whole archive onto one URL.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For any throughput scenario, compute the per-host ceiling first: seconds in a day over the delay. Adding workers rarely changes the answer.
2. For robots.txt, count characters — matched prefix length per rule, longest wins, ties to `Allow` — and check the status code first, because 4xx and 5xx give opposite verdicts and distractors swap them.
3. For trap questions, ask what *bounds* the damage — a per-host budget, a depth or parameter cap — not which trap it is. Detection is distinct content over URLs fetched.
4. For canonicalization, sort each transform into *always safe* (RFC 3986), *heuristic*, or *never blindly*, then ask which way the failure runs: fail open wastes fetches, fail closed loses documents.
5. Watch for "slow" versus "forbidden". If the operator stated a policy, the answer is a sanctioned channel, never a workaround.
