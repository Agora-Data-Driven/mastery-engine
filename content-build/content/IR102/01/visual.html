Note which step is the hot path. The URL-seen test runs once per extracted link, so hundreds of millions of canonical-URL hashes a day — typically an in-memory hash of recent URLs in front of a disk-backed store. And note the order: canonicalization happens before the seen test, because two spellings of one URL are only "seen" if they were normalized to the same string first.

2. Front And Back Queues

One FIFO list answers "what is next?" with whatever arrived first — and politeness then idles your workers. Mercator splits the question in two: front queues decide what is worth fetching, back queues decide what may be fetched right now. Switch the design and add workers. Like a bank with one queue for one teller versus a ticket system feeding eight windows.

Two judgment calls live on top of this machine. Never pop strictly by max priority — a mid-tier site discovered early would wait behind an endless supply of higher-scored URLs, so production frontiers sample across the priority queues weighted by priority. And know when not to build it at all: for a scoped research corpus over thousands of vetted hosts, per-host FIFO queues under a simple host scheduler are usually right. Most of this complexity exists to survive the open web.

3. The robots.txt Rulebook

The declarative half of politeness, at exactly one address per origin and standardized as RFC 9309 (2022). Tap a path to see which rules matched, how long each match was, and who wins — then change the file's HTTP status and watch every verdict flip. Like a house rule pinned to the door: the most specific sign wins, and a missing sign is not a locked door.

Cache the file — 24 hours is the conventional ceiling — rather than refetching it per URL. And note the one directive here that is not in RFC 9309: Crawl-delay is de facto only (Bing honoured it historically, Google ignores it). Honour it anyway; it is the operator stating their capacity in plain text, and visual 4 is what you do when it is absent.

4. The Politeness Dial

When robots.txt says nothing about rate, you set it — and the professional default is not a constant but a delay proportional to how slowly the server is answering you. Drag the server's response time and the multiplier k, then hand the site a Crawl-delay and watch the calendar move. Like backing off in conversation when someone is struggling to keep up.

Three rules travel with this dial. One open connection per host. On 429 or 503, honour Retry-After exactly if it is present and back off exponentially if it is not. And identify yourself with a descriptive User-Agent and a contact address — because the cost of getting this wrong is not a slow crawl, it is an IP ban, and a source lost for good can be worse for a research corpus than one never crawled.

5. Traps And Canonical Form

Two halves of one problem: URL space is bigger than content space. A trap mints unbounded URLs over almost no content; aliasing mints several URLs for one page. Widen the facets, then switch tabs and toggle normalization rules on eight real URLs. Like a hotel where every door opens on the same room — and a corridor that never ends.

The last word belongs to a site's own rel="canonical" — a <link> tag or HTTP Link header naming the URL a page considers its true self. Treat it as a hint, not a directive: it is frequently wrong or abused, so weigh it against your own content-equality evidence, and keep per-site parameter rules learned by fetching variants and comparing what comes back.
