**The big idea**: **The Crawl Frontier: Prioritization and Queue Management** gave you a queue and a rule for what to fetch next; **Politeness: Rate Limits, robots.txt, and Crawl Delay** decided how fast you may fetch it; **Crawler Traps and URL Canonicalization** kept a URL's identity stable so the frontier could not eat itself. All three begin with a URL already in hand. This lesson is about where the URLs come from — because a crawler cannot retrieve a document it never discovers, and link traversal alone is biased toward well-linked, already-known pages, leaving orphan pages, brand-new pages and unlinked sources invisible. Sitemaps, feeds, change notifications, search APIs and chosen seeds are *complementary* paths, each blind where another sees. The idea that survives all of them: their outputs are **leads, not truth**, and every lead needs identity, scope enforcement, validation and provenance before its content enters a research or RAG corpus.

**Key concepts**

- **A sitemap is a publisher-provided inventory, and it is the cure for orphan pages.** An XML sitemap lists canonical-looking URLs, optionally with `lastmod`; a sitemap index points at several files so large sites can publish big inventories. You find them via `robots.txt`, conventional paths, or configuration. Mechanically: fetch, parse the entries, normalize, apply scope policy, enqueue what is eligible. Without one, a page with no inbound links is unreachable by traversal however long you crawl. What it does **not** give you is completeness, quality, accessibility or permission — entries may redirect, error, duplicate parameter variants or fall out of scope, and robots policy still governs each one.
- **`lastmod` is a publisher claim, not a content timestamp.** Some sites stamp it on every deployment, so everything looks changed; others never touch it. Prioritize recrawls with it, but measure its reliability *per source*.
- **Inventory diffing, with real numbers.** Yesterday's sitemap held **100,000** URLs; today's holds **100,500**. A set difference identifies **500** new candidates — the discovery event. Now the asymmetry: if **200** old URLs have disappeared, that does *not* prove deletion — the publisher may have split files, hit a cap, or changed policy. Confirm with an HTTP status and *repeated* absence before removing indexed content.
- **Feeds are incremental by construction and windowed by design.** RSS or Atom publish recent items in time order with stable entry IDs, links, publication and update times. Poll conditionally, deduplicate by entry ID or canonical URL, enqueue anything unseen or updated. Because a feed retains only the latest window it serves **freshness, not historical completeness**: miss a month of polls and only a sitemap, an archive or an API can fill the gap.
- **Change notifications push instead of poll — and push is at-least-once.** A webhook, queue message, storage event or publisher protocol says an object was created, updated or deleted, collapsing detection lag and removing wasted polling. The price: events can be duplicated, reordered or lost, so you need stable event IDs or object versions, idempotent writes, and a periodic reconciliation scan.
- **Worked ordering hazard.** Version **7** of document `d42` arrives *after* version **8**, because queues reorder. A sink applying arrival order regresses the content silently, with no error. Store the source version and accept an update only if it is newer than what you hold — that one monotonic rule buys reordering tolerance *and* idempotence: a repeated version 8 is now harmless.
- **Conditional HTTP is the lever that makes polling cheap.** Persist the sitemap or feed's `ETag` and `Last-Modified`, then send `If-None-Match` or `If-Modified-Since`; a `304 Not Modified` avoids downloading *and* parsing the body — on a 100,500-entry sitemap, roughly 12 MB you never pay for. But validators describe **the fetched resource only**: a changed sitemap still needs entry-level diffing, and an unchanged one does not prove the listed pages are unchanged.
- **The robust architecture is all three channels plus provenance.** Notifications for latency, feed polls for recent reconciliation, a periodic inventory diff for completeness. Record `discovered_via`, discovery time, publisher timestamps and source version, so channel disagreement is *observable* rather than resolved in favour of whatever arrived last.
- **A search API is acquisition from someone else's index.** It returns documents or URLs matching a query, reaching sources your links cannot, with domain and date filters. It is **not** an enumeration mechanism unless the provider guarantees one. Mechanically it is query planning plus pagination: define acquisition queries, call within quota, record results with rank and query provenance, normalize URLs or stable result IDs, deduplicate, then fetch permitted content. Prefer cursor-based pagination — offset pages shift underneath you when the result set changes mid-crawl.
- **Overlap is the norm.** Three queries — `site:cloud.google.com vertex vector search`, `site:cloud.google.com bigquery vector_search`, `site:research.google retrieval embeddings` — returning 100 results each do **not** yield 300 unique pages: high-authority pages rank for many queries. Deduplicate by canonical URL, but keep *every* discovery-query record so later analysis knows why a page entered scope.
- **Search results are ranked samples, so coverage is empirical.** Providers cap retrievable depth, personalize, suppress near-duplicates and change ranking without notice, so repeating one broad query never approaches exhaustive coverage — the same head pages fill the whole window. Use a query matrix across subtopics, entities, time ranges, languages, file types and known domains. Snippets are query-biased fragments: fine for triage, never authoritative text when the source can be fetched.
- **The economics, derived.** 1,000 planned queries at five pages each is 5,000 calls; at $0.005 per request that is **$25** before a single document is fetched. If overlap leaves only 2,000 unique URLs, that is **1.25 cents per unique discovery**. In general, with $q$ queries, $p$ pages, price $c$ and $u$ unique discoveries, the figure is $qpc/u$ — and the trap is the denominator: $u$ saturates with depth while $qpc$ grows linearly, so each extra page costs the same and returns less. Track unique eligible discoveries per request and retire a query family when that number collapses. A `429` means slow down, never rotate credentials.
- **Result URLs are untrusted input.** Scheme and host allowlists, no private IP ranges, sandboxed processing. Search rank is not credibility — spam ranks, and robots still decides whether you may fetch.
- **A seed is where crawling starts, and it decides what is reachable.** The homepage is the obvious seed and often a poor one, dominated by navigation and marketing links; documentation indexes, repository roots, sitemap URLs and trusted topic hubs usually reach more of what you want, sooner.
- **Seed selection is a set-cover judgment, tested by marginal coverage.** Each candidate seed reaches some subset of the desired regions under your link and depth rules. Choose a few *diverse* seeds whose union covers product docs, API references, release notes, research and support — not many seeds funnelling into the same central pages. Test it: add one seed, crawl to a fixed budget, measure the new canonical in-scope documents beyond the current set. A seed whose marginal yield is duplicates is one to remove.
- **Scope policy is enforced before the frontier, and again after every redirect.** A worked policy for a documentation corpus: allowed hosts `cloud.google.com` and `research.google`; path prefixes `/vertex-ai/`, `/bigquery/` and selected `/pubs/`; scheme `https` only; denied patterns `/search`, `/login`, calendar parameters, session IDs; max depth 8; tracking parameters dropped, documented semantic parameters retained; content types HTML and PDF. Every discovered URL is normalized and evaluated against this **before** entering the frontier, and redirect targets are re-checked — otherwise an allowed URL redirects to an out-of-scope or internal host. Resolved DNS and IPs are validated to prevent server-side request forgery.
- **Scope has many dimensions, and "same domain" is not one of them.** Hosts and subdomains, path prefixes, schemes, ports, query-parameter policy, content type, language, date, response size, depth. `example.com` may link to user-controlled subdomains or a shared CDN, so name exact hosts. And the depth cap is the bluntest dimension: priority plus per-host and per-path budgets crawl documentation deeply while limiting low-yield archives. Report discovered, rejected by each rule, fetched, redirected, duplicated and failed — a surge of rejections is how a bad seed or a redesign announces itself.
- **Canonicalization and scope interact, and both extremes are wrong.** Strip *all* query parameters and you merge genuinely different resources such as `?version=v1` and `?version=v2`; keep every tracking parameter and you manufacture infinite duplicates. Define parameter semantics per source. Canonical tags are hints — a canonical target outside scope does not authorize ingestion.
- **Coverage needs a denominator, and scope is a legal boundary.** "The crawler fetched 100,000 pages" proves volume, not completeness: compare crawled canonical URLs against publisher inventories, known navigation, API counts or curated gold pages, and stratify missingness by host, path family, language and date so a blocked subsite cannot vanish inside a healthy aggregate. And permission, terms, credentials, tenant isolation and data residency are stricter than reachability — "the crawler can fetch it" is not authorization.

**Rules to remember**

- Discovery channels emit leads, not truth. Identity, scope, validation and provenance apply to all of them.
- Absence from an inventory is not deletion. Confirm with HTTP status and repeated absence.
- Notifications are at-least-once: idempotent writes, a version guard, a reconciliation scan. Accept an update only if its source version is newer than the one you hold.
- Feeds serve freshness; sitemaps and archives serve completeness. Never substitute one for the other.
- Judge a query family by unique eligible discoveries per request; judge a seed by marginal coverage.
- Evaluate scope before enqueue, and re-evaluate after every redirect.
- Volume is not coverage: coverage needs a denominator.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Treating a sitemap as ground truth.** Its entries still face robots and scope rules, and a URL vanishing from the file is not a deletion.
- **Trusting `lastmod` without ever checking it.** A site stamping it every deploy makes your crawl look stale; one that never touches it makes it look frozen.
- **A notification sink that applies arrival order.** Version 7 landing after version 8 regresses the document, and logs nothing.
- **Polling without conditional requests, or reading a `304` as "the pages are unchanged".** One wastes a multi-megabyte transfer every cycle; the other over-reads a validator that describes the sitemap and nothing else.
- **Counting search results as coverage.** Three queries × 100 results is nowhere near 300 unique pages; total result count is the wrong metric.
- **Paginating deeper because the budget allows.** Marginal cost per unique discovery rises sharply with depth; that is where a family should be retired.
- **Treating search rank as credibility, or result URLs as safe.** Spam ranks; result URLs need allowlists and private-IP blocking.
- **Crawling broadly and deleting later.** Fetching itself can violate limits, and untrusted content is already inside.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Ask which channel *could* have discovered the page at all. Orphan pages point at sitemaps, brand-new items at feeds or notifications, unlinked third-party sources at a search API.
2. When a document is stale or regressed, suspect arrival-order application, a missed poll window, or an unreliable `lastmod` — in that order. For disappearance, the answer is "confirm before acting".
3. On search-API questions, separate *result count* from *unique eligible in-scope discoveries*. Distractors quote the former.
4. For cost, compute calls first (queries × pages), then cost, then divide by unique yield — and check whether the average or the marginal page is being asked about.
5. For scope questions, check *where* the rule is enforced: before enqueue, after redirect, at fetch, at storage, at indexing. Wrong answers enforce it in one place.
6. Watch for "the crawler can fetch it" reasoning. Permission and terms are separate from reachability, and stricter.
