1. From Frontier To Leads

The Crawl Frontier, Politeness and URL Canonicalization all begin with a URL already in hand. This panel is the step before them: switch discovery channels on and watch which of ten real pages become reachable at all, and how long each takes to notice. Like a shop that only restocks what customers ask for — the things nobody mentions never arrive.

Nothing here skips what you already know: every URL a channel produces is still normalized, canonicalized, checked against scope and robots, then queued and rate-limited exactly as before. Discovery only decides what gets the chance to be crawled — and its output is a lead, not truth.

2. Poll, Diff, Confirm

One sitemap poll, three separate decisions: was it worth downloading, what is new, and what has vanished. Only the first two are cheap. Like a shop inventory sheet: a missing line means "look into it", never "the item was destroyed".

lastmod is a publisher claim, not a content timestamp — some sites stamp it on every deployment, others never touch it, so prioritise recrawls with it only after measuring its reliability per source. And an ETag validator describes the sitemap file: a 304 proves nothing about the pages it lists.

3. Events Out Of Order

Change notifications are delivered at least once, in no guaranteed order. Deliver the five events for document d42 and watch what your sink ends up storing. Like post arriving out of sequence: apply every letter as it lands and last week's news overwrites today's.

A version guard fixes duplication and reordering. It cannot fix loss — a webhook dropped by an integration defect never arrives to be compared against anything. That is why the design pairs notifications with a periodic reconciliation scan over the feed or sitemap: push for latency, poll for truth.

4. Cost Per Unique Page

A search API bills per request and pays out in unique pages, and those two numbers grow at very different rates. Drag the plan and watch the last page of results become the expensive one. Like panning a river: the first pan holds most of the gold, the tenth is mostly gravel you already own.

Three real queries — site:cloud.google.com vertex vector search, site:cloud.google.com bigquery vector_search, site:research.google retrieval embeddings — returning 100 results each do not give 300 unique pages: high-authority pages rank for many queries. Deduplicate by canonical URL, and keep every discovery-query record, so later you can still say why a page entered scope.

5. Seeds And Scope Gate

Seeds decide what is reachable; scope policy decides what is allowed. Pick seeds and watch the union of corpus regions they cover, then set the parameter and redirect rules and watch eight candidate URLs pass or fail. Like planning a trip: which stops you can reach, and which ones you are actually permitted to enter.

Seeds — select a set that covers the corpus

Enforce scope at discovery, at fetch, at storage and at downstream indexing — defence in depth, because "crawl broadly and delete later" is unsafe: the fetch itself may breach terms, and untrusted content is already inside. Permission, robots, credentials and tenant isolation are stricter than reachability: "the crawler can fetch it" is not authorization.
