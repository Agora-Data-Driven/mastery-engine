**The big idea**: **The Crawl Frontier: Prioritization and Queue Management** gave you a queue that has to be ordered, and **Politeness: Rate Limits, robots.txt, and Crawl Delay** gave you the hard budget that ordering spends. **Sitemaps, Feeds, and Change Notifications** gave you a cheap channel for hearing that something moved, and **Main-Content Extraction and Boilerplate Removal** gave you a clean text so a page can be compared against its own past. This lesson supplies the number all of that was missing: *how likely is this page to have changed since we last verified it?* Corpus freshness is an allocation problem — every page **can** change, but fetch capacity and rate limits are finite. Recrawl everything equally and you burn bandwidth on static archives while release notes go stale. So: estimate a change rate, schedule by expected value per unit of cost inside the politeness constraint, and let ranking use recency **only** where the query benefits. That third step is a genuinely separate decision, and the system must explain both why a URL was fetched now and why a document's age moved it up a result list.

**Key concepts**

- **Changes modelled as a Poisson process.** Let meaningful changes arrive at rate $\lambda$ per day. Zero arrivals in an interval $t$ has probability $e^{-\lambda t}$, so at least one change has probability $P(\text{changed by }t)=1-e^{-\lambda t}$. That expression converts "it has been a while" into a number you can multiply by a cost. A release-notes page changing on average once every two days has $\lambda=0.5$: after one day the change probability is $1-e^{-0.5}\approx0.393$, after four days $1-e^{-2}\approx0.865$. A policy PDF revised once a year has $\lambda\approx1/365$, so a week gives only $1-e^{-7/365}\approx1.9\%$. Recrawl both weekly and you are wrong in both directions at once.
- **The Poisson assumption is a tractable baseline, not a belief.** It asserts independent changes at a stable average rate. Real pages violate both halves: news bursts, annual reports move seasonally, documentation changes after a release, a deleted product page stops changing. Carry page features and recent observations rather than pretend one rate is timeless.
- **Estimating the rate from fetch history.** Store fetch time, status, validators, both raw and normalised content hashes, and the extractor version. Count **meaningful content changes**, not byte differences from ads, rotating timestamps or template churn. If a page changed in $c$ of $n$ observed intervals over exposure time $T$, the estimate is $\hat\lambda=c/T$: three changes over 30 observed days gives 0.1 per day.
- **Sparse history needs smoothing, and the Gamma prior is the standard move.** One unchanged fetch on a new page must not classify it permanent. A Gamma prior on a Poisson rate is conjugate, so the posterior mean is $(\alpha+c)/(\beta+T)$, with $\alpha/\beta$ the prior mean rate. Take $\alpha=1$, $\beta=20$ days — prior mean 0.05/day — and observe $c=3$, $T=30$: the posterior mean is $4/50=0.08$ rather than the raw 0.1. The prior comes from the page class, so release notes start higher than archived legal PDFs.
- **HTTP validators are evidence, not content truth.** A changed `ETag` may only reflect recompression or a redeploy; a stable validator from a broken server hides a real update. Fetch conditionally to save bytes, but audit validator reliability against content hashes, and calibrate `Last-Modified` per source.
- **Magnitude matters as much as frequency.** A page rotating an hourly timestamp has a huge byte-change rate and zero research value. Normalise and extract before hashing, or classify which regions changed. Keep both hashes: raw changes diagnose transport, semantic changes drive reindexing.
- **Page classes carry different initial policies.** Release notes burst near launches — daily, faster after a detected release. An API reference changes in weekly clusters — every few days. A historical paper — monthly or quarterly. A homepage churns on template edits — hash the content region, moderate interval.
- **The observation process is censored by your own schedule.** Check a page weekly and three changes between checks are seen as one difference, so counting detected changes undercounts fast pages. Publisher versions, feeds or changelogs improve the estimate; otherwise model the probability of *at least one* change per interval rather than treating a changed fetch as one event. That uncertainty should widen audit coverage, not license a falsely precise interval.
- **Recrawl scheduling turns change probability into frontier priority.** The naive rule makes the interval inversely proportional to the rate, but useful scheduling also weighs importance, staleness cost, fetch cost and source constraints. One benefit score is $\text{priority}(u)=w_u(1-e^{-\lambda_u a_u})/c_u$: $w_u$ is importance or the cost of serving stale content, $a_u$ is age since last successful verification, $c_u$ is expected fetch cost. The numerator is the expected value of discovering a change; dividing by cost favours productive fetches.
- **Worked comparison.** Page A: importance 5, $\lambda=0.2$/day, age 3 days, cost 1, so priority $=5(1-e^{-0.6})\approx2.26$. Page B: importance 1, $\lambda=0.05$/day, age 10 days, cost 1, so $1(1-e^{-0.5})\approx0.39$. A wins despite B being older, because A matters more and moves faster. Raise A's cost to 10 units for JavaScript rendering and its priority falls to 0.226 — now B wins. Cost is a first-class term: an `If-None-Match` answered with 304 transfers almost nothing, so a *reliable* validator lowers $c_u$ and justifies checking more often, while rendering JavaScript or pulling a 500 MB PDF raises it — and expected extraction and reindex work belongs in there too.
- **Politeness still owns the queue.** A single global queue lets one high-rate site monopolise workers and cause synchronised bursts. A two-level scheduler picks among *eligible hosts* first — each carrying a next-allowed time from its crawl rate, robots policy, errors and server feedback — then that host's best URL. Failure backoff is separate again: repeated 429 or 503 responses back off exponentially with jitter, honour `Retry-After`, and cut host concurrency; a confirmed 404 or 410 marks absence; an authentication failure needs an alert, not hot retries.
- **Guard against starvation with a maximum interval.** A low predicted rate drives priority toward zero forever, so impose an audit deadline: every eligible page is eventually revalidated. New pages get exploratory fetches to learn their rate; no-change outcomes lengthen the interval, a detected change shortens it. Change notifications override normal timing but remain hints — version checks prevent out-of-order regression, and scheduled checks remain as reconciliation.
- **A freshness SLA is measured in lag, and scheduling owns one stage.** The chain is publisher change → detection → extraction → indexing → serving; recrawl scheduling controls detection lag alone. A page fetched within a minute but parked in a six-hour embedding queue is not fresh. Track stage timestamps separately.
- **Scheduling must be idempotent under worker failure.** Lease a frontier item for a bounded time and key fetch output by URL plus observed content hash, so a retry after a lost acknowledgement recognises the same hash instead of writing a duplicate version. An expired lease returns work to the queue; it does not prove the earlier fetch failed.
- **Recency as a ranking signal is query-dependent.** Recrawling decides what version exists in the corpus; ranking decides whether age should affect *this* query. `latest Vertex AI quotas` wants current documents; `PageRank original paper` does not improve because a blog repost is newer.
- **Time decay and its half-life.** A simple recency signal is $r(a)=e^{-\gamma a}$ for age $a$. Setting $r(h)=1/2$ gives $\gamma=\ln2/h$ — the half-life $h$ is the interpretable dial. With $h=30$ days a new page scores 1, a 30-day page 0.5, a 90-day page $0.5^3=0.125$.
- **Fuse, never replace.** Score as $S(d,q)=S_{\text{rel}}(d,q)+\alpha(q)r(a_d)$ once the signals are on compatible scales, or rerank with recency as a feature. $\alpha(q)$ is large for freshness-intent queries and near zero for timeless ones. Raw BM25 and cosine scores are not comparable with a 0-to-1 decay, so normalise, learn the combination, or use rank-based fusion.
- **Worked ranking.** *Old* has relevance 0.95 and recency 0.1; *New* has relevance 0.80 and recency 0.9. With $\alpha=0.1$ the scores are 0.96 and 0.89 — Old stays first. With explicit "latest" intent and $\alpha=0.4$ they become 0.99 and 1.16 — New leads. Recency flips the result only when the policy values it enough to close a 0.15 relevance gap.
- **Which date decays matters.** Publication date, last meaningful content update, event effective date and crawl time are four different things. Crawl time records when *you* looked, so decaying on it rewards freshly fetched ancient documents. Prefer trusted content timestamps, retain provenance, cap implausible future dates, and never let a template edit reset semantic freshness.
- **Validity is not decay.** A 2024 policy is *correct* for a question explicitly about 2024. Metadata filters or temporal query understanding should select the requested effective period before recency ranking runs. Newest is not correct.

**Rules to remember**

- $P(\text{changed by }t)=1-e^{-\lambda t}$; $\hat\lambda=c/T$ raw, $(\alpha+c)/(\beta+T)$ smoothed.
- Count meaningful changes on normalised, extracted text — never raw bytes.
- Slow polling censors the observation: detected changes undercount the true rate on fast pages.
- $\text{priority}(u)=w_u(1-e^{-\lambda_u a_u})/c_u$. Age alone is not staleness risk; cost is a real divisor.
- Host eligibility gates the global queue: two-level scheduling, always.
- Every page needs a maximum audit interval, however static it looks.
- Detection lag is only the first stage of freshness lag.
- Half-life converts to decay as $\gamma=\ln2/h$; recency is added to relevance, weighted by query intent.
- Decay a content timestamp, never crawl time.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Freezing a page after two unchanged fetches.** A rate estimate is uncertain; record sample count and confidence, and keep the audit deadline. Overconfidence creates invisible staleness.
- **Treating a changed `ETag` as a changed document.** It is transport evidence, and it misleads in both directions.
- **Counting byte diffs as changes.** A rotating timestamp gives a high rate and zero research value.
- **Estimating the rate from detected changes on a slowly polled page.** You measure your schedule, not the publisher.
- **Ordering the frontier by age alone.** The oldest-first policy this lesson replaces; it spends the budget on archives. Ignoring fetch cost is the same error one term over.
- **Optimising crawl volume.** The objective is expected stale-content harm avoided per constrained fetch, with popularity feedback capped so head pages do not starve long-tail coverage.
- **Letting recency override relevance globally.** It amplifies low-quality churn and SEO junk: a site publishing superficial updates dominates unless quality and relevance stay primary.
- **Reporting one global freshness metric.** Evaluate on query slices — current events, mutable documentation, evergreen reference, historical lookup — a global average hides damage to evergreen queries.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Given a rate and an interval, reach for $1-e^{-\lambda t}$ first; check whether the rate is per day or per year.
2. If the question mentions a prior, a new page, or "only one observation", it is testing smoothing: apply $(\alpha+c)/(\beta+T)$ and expect an answer between prior and raw estimate.
3. For scheduling comparisons, compute all four terms — importance, change probability, age, cost. Distractors change exactly one and expect you to keep the old ordering.
4. Separate the two decisions. "Why was it fetched now?" is scheduling. "Why did its age move the ranking?" is fusion. Questions blur them deliberately.
5. For recency questions, ask what the query's own weight should be, then check whether the recency gap closes the relevance gap.
6. Any answer resting on crawl time as the document date is wrong unless the question is about detection lag.
7. Watch for absolutes: "always recrawl daily", "newest first", "static pages need no audit". Each is a policy the lesson bounds rather than endorses.

**Where this leads**: freshness decides *which version* of a page you hold and how much its age counts. It says nothing about whether the page was worth having.
