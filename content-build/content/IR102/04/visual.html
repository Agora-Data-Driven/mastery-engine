1. Frontier Meets Freshness

The Crawl Frontier gave you a queue that must be ordered and Politeness gave you the budget it spends. What was missing is the number that orders it. Choose a policy and a budget, and watch where the fetches go. Like a night nurse with time for three rooms: check the quietest patients, or the ones most likely to have changed?

Neither policy escapes politeness: a two-level scheduler still picks an eligible host first — each host carrying a next-allowed time from its crawl rate, robots policy and error history — and only then that host’s best URL. And whichever policy wins, every page needs a maximum audit interval, or the policy PDF at the bottom of the value ranking is never fetched again.

2. The Change Curve

Model meaningful changes as a Poisson process at rate λ per day. Zero changes in t days has probability e^(−λt), so P(changed) = 1 − e^(−λt). Pick a page class, drag the days, read the probability. Like a kettle you cannot see: the longer you wait, the more sure you are it boiled — but never certain.

The Poisson assumption is a tractable baseline, not a belief: it claims independent changes at a stable average rate, and real pages break both halves — news bursts, annual reports move seasonally, documentation changes right after a release, a deleted product page stops changing altogether. And the event being counted must be a meaningful content change: normalise and extract before hashing, or an hourly rotating timestamp gives you a huge rate and zero research value.

3. Counting From History

You never observe λ. You observe c meaningful changes over T days of watching, and then you have to decide how much to believe it. Step the counts, switch the prior, and slow the polling down. Like judging a restaurant from three visits: the average is real, the confidence is not.

A rate estimate is uncertain, so record the sample count and the confidence beside it. Freezing a page after two unchanged fetches creates invisible staleness — nothing in the system reports it, because nothing is looking. Segment models by source and type, detect regime shifts, and keep a maximum audit interval even for pages you believe are static.

4. Priority Per Fetch

priority(u) = w·(1 − e^(−λa)) / c. Importance times the chance of finding a change, divided by what the fetch costs. Tune page A against a fixed page B and watch the ordering flip. Like deciding which errand to run: how much it matters, how likely it has changed, and how far away it is.

Optimise expected stale-content harm avoided per constrained fetch, not crawl volume — and cap popularity feedback so head pages do not starve long-tail coverage. Remember too that this score only moves detection lag. Freshness is publisher change → detection → extraction → indexing → serving; a page fetched in a minute and parked in a six-hour embedding queue is not fresh.

5. Recency Meets Relevance

Recrawling decides which version exists; ranking decides whether age should matter for this query. Fuse it: S = S_rel + α(q)·r(a), with r(a) = e^(−γa) and γ = ln2/h. Pick an intent, move the half-life, then switch which date you decay. Like milk in a fridge: the date on the carton is the point, not the day you happened to buy it.

Recency also amplifies low-quality churn: a site publishing frequent superficial updates dominates unless source quality and relevance stay primary. And validity is not decay — a 2024 policy is correct for a question about 2024, so temporal query understanding should select the effective period before ranking. Evaluate on slices (current events, mutable docs, evergreen reference, historical lookup); a single global metric hides the damage done to evergreen queries.
