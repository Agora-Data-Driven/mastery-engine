**The big idea**: **The Inverted Index: Dictionary and Postings** gave you a structure that answers *which* documents contain a term, **Boolean Query Processing by Intersecting Postings** turned that into a set, and **Query Optimization: Processing Rarest Terms First** already leaned on rarity — but only as a cost heuristic, a way to keep the intersection small. This lesson turns both of those instincts into numbers. An inverted index identifies documents containing query terms; ranking must decide **how much each match means**. Two observations drive all classical lexical weighting: repeated occurrences provide diminishing evidence that a document is about a term, and a match on a rare term is more informative than a match on a ubiquitous one. Term frequency and inverse document frequency turn those observations into numeric weights, and their product becomes the vector-space score of the next lesson.

**Key concepts**

- **Raw term frequency is the naive rule, and it is naive in one specific way.** For term $t$ and document $d$, $tf_{t,d}$ is the number of times $t$ occurs in $d$, and the first scoring rule anyone writes is $S(q,d)=\sum_{t\in q} tf_{t,d}$. For the query `vector database`, a document containing `vector` 5 times and `database` once scores 6; one containing each twice scores 4. That rewards repeated evidence — and it also asserts that the tenth occurrence is worth exactly what the first was. It rarely is. The first `database` changes a document from nonmatching to matching; the second supports topicality; the fiftieth may be boilerplate or keyword stuffing. Evidence should grow **sublinearly**, or saturate.
- **Sublinear tf is the standard repair, and the arithmetic is the whole argument.** The common transform is $w_{tf}(t,d)=1+\log tf_{t,d}$ for $tf_{t,d}>0$, and $0$ when $tf_{t,d}=0$. Using natural logs, frequencies 1, 2, 10 and 100 become weights 1, 1.693, 3.303 and 5.605. Increasing frequency tenfold from 10 to 100 adds only $\log 10\approx 2.303$ — not 90 units. Any logarithm base yields the same ranking when applied consistently, up to a scale factor, so the base is a convention rather than a decision.
- **The saturation problem, priced against an adversary.** A relevant document R contains `retrieval` 8 times in 1,000 words; a spam document S repeats it 80 times in 300 words. Raw tf hands S ten times R's term evidence, 80 against 8. Log tf gives $1+\ln 8\approx 3.08$ against $1+\ln 80\approx 5.38$ — a ratio of only 1.75. Sublinearity does not solve spam or length bias, but it stops repetition from dominating proportionally.
- **The zero-frequency branch has to be written down.** Extending $1+\log tf$ to $tf=0$ is undefined, and a generic fallback would assign a weight where there should be none. Sparse implementations simply store weights for terms that are present. Unit tests should cover zero, one, threshold values and very large counts: a transform that behaves on ordinary prose can overflow, lose precision, or dominate scores on generated junk.
- **Other tf transforms encode other beliefs about repetition.** Binary frequency is 1 if present and 0 otherwise — it saturates immediately and suits fields where repetition carries little meaning, such as a short tag set. Square root $tf^{1/2}$ is sublinear but less aggressive than the logarithm at some scales. **Maximum-frequency normalization** divides by the largest term frequency in the document, often as $0.5+0.5(tf/\max tf)$; it removes document-specific scale but is unstable, because adding one highly repeated unrelated term changes every other term's normalized weight. Document-length normalization at scoring time, taught next lesson, is usually cleaner.
- **Frequency is field-dependent.** Three mentions in a six-word title are suspicious and redundant; three in a long body are ordinary. Field-specific weights or normalized variants exist to reflect that. Tokenization and stemming move tf too: merging morphological variants raises counts, stop-word removal deletes them.
- **Inverse document frequency is derived from information content, not invented.** Let $N$ be the corpus document count and $df_t$ the number of documents containing $t$. The empirical probability that a uniformly sampled document contains $t$ is $p_t = df_t/N$. The information content of an event of probability $p$ is $-\log p$, so $idf_t=-\log(df_t/N)=\log(N/df_t)$. That is the core of the idea that **rare terms carry signal**: a rare event is informative, a certain one is not. If every document contains `the`, then $df=N$, $idf=\log 1 = 0$, and matching it distinguishes nothing.
- **Worked idf numbers.** With $N=10{,}000$: `database` appears in 1,000 documents, so $idf=\ln 10\approx 2.303$; `vector` appears in 100, so $idf=\ln 100\approx 4.605$. One `vector` occurrence carries twice the idf of one `database` occurrence, because it narrows the candidate population by another factor of ten. At corpus scale the effect is larger still: `reciprocal` in 100 of one million documents gives $\ln 10{,}000\approx 9.21$.
- **idf is collection-specific, so scores are not portable.** Adding documents changes $N$ and the document frequencies. A specialist corpus where `vector` appears in half the documents gives it low idf; a general corpus makes it rare. Scores computed from separately built indexes are not inherently comparable.
- **The unsmoothed formula has edge cases, and the smoothed ones are conventions.** A query term absent from the corpus has $df=0$ and divides by zero — it has no postings and contributes nothing, so absence is handled before scoring. Very small corpora produce unstable values. Smoothed variants such as $idf_t=\log\frac{N+1}{df_t+1}+1$, or $\log(N/(1+df_t))$, keep values finite and positive. They are engineering conventions and not interchangeable numbers, so persist the formula alongside the model configuration.
- **High idf is not automatically trustworthy.** A random UUID, a typo or an extraction corruption is maximally rare and may be useless. Vocabulary filtering, spelling correction, field rules and quality controls keep noise from taking extreme influence; capping idf or ignoring terms below a minimum collection frequency helps, at the cost of also suppressing genuine rare identifiers.
- **Document frequency counts documents, not occurrences.** Repeating a term 1,000 times in one document raises collection frequency but adds exactly 1 to $df$. That is precisely what makes idf complementary to tf: tf measures within-document evidence, idf measures across-collection discrimination.
- **Segment-local idf is a real trap.** If each immutable index segment computes rarity independently, a term that is rare in a tiny new segment gets a score incomparable with the same term in the main segment. Systems use collection-level statistics, approximate global statistics, or merge-normalized scoring — and evaluation must include newly ingested documents, because inconsistent statistics manufacture freshness-shaped rank boosts unrelated to content.
- **The tf-idf weight is the product, and the log-tf form is the prose default.** $w_{t,d}=tf_{t,d}\times idf_t$, or with sublinear tf, $w_{t,d}=(1+\log tf_{t,d})\log(N/df_t)$ for present terms and 0 otherwise. Worked, with $N=10{,}000$: a document with `vector` 3 times and `database` once scores $3(4.605)=13.815$ and $1(2.303)=2.303$ under raw tf-idf, versus $(1+\ln 3)(4.605)\approx 9.665$ and $2.303$ under log tf-idf. The rarer term still dominates; repetition is moderated.
- **The tf-idf variants form a family of weighting schemes, and the choice is a modelling statement.** Binary tf × idf for short sets; raw tf × idf, simple but vulnerable to verbosity; log tf × idf, the common prose baseline; augmented tf × idf, which reduces scale but couples every term in a document to one term's maximum; and Boolean weighting without idf, for exact set matching where rarity should not affect eligibility. **SMART notation** compresses a combination into a three-letter code for term-frequency, document-frequency and normalization choices — `ltc` commonly means logarithmic tf, idf, cosine normalization; `nnn` means raw tf, no idf, no normalization. Document the convention you use rather than memorising codes without an implementation in front of you.
- **Query weights need not match document weights.** Short user queries usually contain each term once, so binary query tf is adequate while document tf stays logarithmic. Deliberate query repetition could boost a term, but accidental repetition should not multiply importance unchecked.
- **Weighting applies after analysis, and fields must be kept apart.** If stemming maps `databases` onto `database`, their tf and df combine. If title and body are separate fields, compute field-specific statistics or weights: adding title repetitions into a 5,000-word body treats incomparable evidence as one count. A fielded scheme such as `3 × title_tfidf + 1 × body_tfidf` makes the judgment explicit — that title evidence is worth more — and should be tuned on judged queries rather than chosen because a formula is traditional.
- **Numerical scale matters, and explainability is the payoff.** Changing the log base rescales idf consistently, but smoothing, caps, field boosts and normalization can change rankings; store enough precision and break ties deterministically. Sparse vectors omit zero-weight terms. For any returned document you can report matched terms, their document frequencies, transformed term frequencies, field boosts and contribution to the total — which reveals whether a result won on one rare identifier, on repeated generic prose, or on a boosted title, and makes analyzer regressions visible as a missing contribution.

**Rules to remember**

- $S(q,d)=\sum_t tf_{t,d}$ is the baseline everything else corrects; raw tf says occurrence ten equals occurrence one.
- $1+\log tf$ for $tf>0$, and exactly 0 at $tf=0$ — the branch is explicit, never a fallback.
- $idf=\log(N/df)$ comes from $-\log p$ with $p=df/N$. $df=N \Rightarrow idf=0$.
- df counts documents; collection frequency counts occurrences. They are different statistics.
- $df=0$ divides by zero. Handle absence before scoring, or smooth.
- idf is a property of the collection, so scores from different indexes do not compare.
- Rare does not mean useful. UUIDs and typos are maximally rare.
- Query weighting and document weighting are separate choices.
- Any weighting scheme is part of the index model: changing it invalidates stored scores and comparisons.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Treating raw tf as neutral.** It is a strong claim about linear evidence, and it is the claim spam exploits.
- **Applying $1+\log tf$ without the zero branch,** then wondering why absent terms carry weight.
- **Reading idf as "importance".** It measures discrimination within one collection, nothing more.
- **Comparing scores across corpora or across segments** whose statistics were computed independently.
- **Assuming maximum-frequency normalization is harmless.** One repeated unrelated term rewrites every other weight in that document.
- **Confusing df with collection frequency** — the classic trap being a term repeated 1,000 times in a single document.
- **Letting body repetition outvote a title match** because the fields were summed into one count.
- **Expecting tf-idf to model proximity, word order, synonymy, or interaction between query terms.** It models none of them.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Identify which half of the weight a scenario is about: within-document evidence (tf) or across-collection discrimination (idf). Most distractors swap them.
2. When a question gives counts, actually compute. $1+\ln 8 = 3.08$ against $1+\ln 80 = 5.38$ settles arguments that intuition does not.
3. For any idf claim, check whether $N$ or $df$ moved. Adding documents changes both, and it changes every score.
4. Watch for edge cases planted in one clause: $tf=0$, $df=0$, $df=N$, a single document holding 1,000 occurrences.
5. On scheme questions, ask what belief the scheme encodes — binary says repetition is meaningless, raw says it is linear, log says it is diminishing.
6. Treat "rare therefore relevant" as a flag. The correct answer usually names filtering, capping, or analyzer quality.
