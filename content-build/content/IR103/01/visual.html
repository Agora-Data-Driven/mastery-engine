1. From Sets To Scores

The inverted index told you which documents contain a term, and Boolean query processing by intersecting postings turned that into a set — a set with no order inside it. Four documents, query vector database, corpus of N = 10,000. Change the scoring rule and watch the winner change. Like a shortlist versus a ranked shortlist: same names, but now someone has said who is first.

Both halves of the weight are visible here and they answer different questions. tf is within-document evidence; idf is across-collection discrimination. D4 has the most occurrences of anything in the set and the least rare vocabulary — which rule you pick decides whether that reads as the strongest match or the weakest.

2. The Saturation Curve

Raw term frequency claims the tenth occurrence is worth exactly what the first was. Drag the count, or jump to a landmark value, and compare what each transform pays for it — then read the spam row, where a document repeating the term against a relevant document’s 8 occurrences is scored by each rule. Like a tip jar: the first coin tells you something, the fiftieth tells you almost nothing new.

The transform is a claim about the field, not a universal law. Binary for categories and exact attributes, where repetition means nothing; sublinear for prose; raw counts mainly when the counts themselves are the phenomenon. And note the branch at zero: extending 1 + log tf to tf = 0 is undefined, so the rule carries an explicit 0 case rather than a generic fallback — sparse implementations simply store nothing for absent terms.

3. Rarity Is Information

idf is not a heuristic — it is the information content of an observation. Drag a term’s document frequency through a corpus of N = 10,000 and watch the derivation recompute: p = df/N, information = −log p, and therefore idf = log(N/df). Like being told someone’s birthday month versus their exact date of birth — the rarer answer narrows the crowd more.

Two things that follow, and both bite in production. df counts documents, not occurrences — repeating a term 1,000 times in one document raises collection frequency and adds exactly 1 to df, which is what makes idf complementary to tf rather than a second copy of it. And idf is a property of this collection: add documents and both N and df move, so scores from separately computed indexes — or from independently-scored segments — are not inherently comparable.

4. Choosing A Scheme

tf-idf is not one formula but a family of weighting schemes, and every member is a claim about what repetition and rarity mean in your field. Set a term’s tf and df and compare the five variants side by side on the same evidence. Like choosing a scoring system for a competition before anyone plays: the same performances, a different champion.

Query weights need not match document weights. A short user query usually contains each term once, so binary query tf is adequate while document tf stays logarithmic — deliberate repetition in a query could boost a term, but accidental repetition should not multiply importance unchecked. SMART notation compresses a combination into three letters for the tf, df and normalization choices: ltc commonly means logarithmic tf, idf, cosine normalization; nnn means raw tf, no idf, no normalization. Document the convention rather than memorising codes without an implementation in front of you.

5. Title Boost Crossover

Document X has vector once in its title and ten times in its body. Document Y has it in the body only. A flat tf-idf lets body repetition dominate, so a fielded scheme scores w × title_tfidf + 1 × body_tfidf. Move the title weight and Y’s body count, and read off how much repetition it takes to overturn one title match. Like a headline versus a footnote: the same sentence, and everyone knows they are not worth the same.

This decomposition is the explainability story. For any returned document you can report the matched terms, their document frequencies, transformed term frequencies, field boosts and each one’s contribution to the total — which reveals whether a result won on one rare identifier, on repeated generic prose, or on a boosted title, and makes an analyzer regression visible as a missing expected contribution rather than as a mysterious ranking. Tie those explanations to the exact index generation, or later corpus statistics reconstruct a plausible but historically false score.
