**The big idea**: **Term Frequency and Its Saturation Problem** gave you within-document evidence, **Inverse Document Frequency: Rare Terms Carry Signal** gave you across-collection discrimination, and **tf-idf Variants and Weighting Schemes** combined them into a single number $w(t,d)$ per term per document. What none of them supplied is a rule for turning a bag of those numbers into a rank. This lesson supplies it by turning retrieval into geometry: give every indexed term a coordinate, place a document's weight for that term in it, and documents and queries become vectors in one shared space. Ranking becomes a comparison of weighted directions rather than a brittle test for string equality. Three ideas carry the lesson — **documents as bag-of-words vectors** define the axes, **cosine similarity as a ranking function** measures alignment along them, and **document length normalization** decides how much a long document's extra opportunities to match are allowed to count.

**Key concepts**

- **A bag-of-words vector is one coordinate per vocabulary term, with word order discarded.** Take the vocabulary `[database, index, query, vector]`. The document "database index index query" has the raw-count vector `[1, 2, 1, 0]`; the query "vector query" becomes `[0, 0, 1, 1]`. Both now inhabit the same four-dimensional space and can be compared arithmetically. Without a shared coordinate system there is nothing to compare and a rank is impossible.
- **Sparsity is what makes the geometry practical.** Real vocabularies run to millions of terms while each document uses a tiny fraction, so nothing allocates a million-element array: it stores nonzero entries as `(term_id, weight)` pairs, and that store is the inverted index you already have. The posting list for `query` is exactly the set of documents whose `query` coordinate is nonzero. Drop sparsity and the model is a thought experiment; keep it and it is an index scan.
- **Representation and weighting are separate decisions.** Bag-of-words fixes the axes; the weighting scheme decides how much evidence an active axis supplies. A coordinate can hold binary presence, a raw count, saturated tf, or the previous lesson's tf-idf form $w(t,d) = (1 + \ln tf(t,d)) \cdot idf(t)$: a term occurring four times with idf 2.0 receives $(1 + 1.386) \times 2 = 4.772$, not the raw 4. Every weighting scheme you learned applies unchanged — it now chooses coordinate values rather than summands.
- **Discarding order buys invariance and costs structure.** "vector search ranks documents" and "documents rank vector search" activate identical coordinates — usually right for topical retrieval, and exactly what lets an inverted index aggregate evidence term by term, independently. The price: "dog bites person" and "person bites dog" have identical bags, and "not reliable" overlaps "reliable" almost completely despite the opposite implication. Phrase indexes, positional features or a reranker restore some of that.
- **Vocabulary construction is modelling, not clerical preprocessing.** Tokenization decides whether `PostgreSQL`, `postgres` and `postgresql` share an axis; stemming merges `connect`/`connected`/`connection`, improving recall and erasing distinctions; stop-word removal saves coordinates but damages "flights to Singapore"; character n-grams survive misspellings at the cost of index size. Each choice changes the geometry in which similarity is later measured.
- **A worked overlap.** Document A has `timeout` three times and `database` once; B has `timeout` once and `browser` twice. For the query `database timeout` both share `timeout`, but only A shares `database` — and if `database` carries the higher idf, A gains a strong second contribution, regardless of either text's length.
- **No shared axis means no score, and that is a boundary of the representation.** A document containing no query term has a dot product of zero with the query and cannot receive positive lexical similarity. A query for `car` will not match a document that only says `automobile` unless expansion, normalization, or another retrieval channel connects them. That is a property of the axes, not a defect in cosine similarity.
- **The dot product measures aligned weight.** $q \cdot d = \sum_t q_t d_t$, and only coordinates nonzero in *both* vectors contribute — which maps exactly onto traversing the query terms' postings. With $q = [1,1,0]$, $A = [2,1,0]$, $B = [1,4,0]$ the dot products are $3$ and $5$: on this alone, B ranks first.
- **Raw dot product rewards magnitude, so cosine compares direction instead.** A long document has more opportunities to mention query terms and accumulates larger weights even when the query is a minor part of it. Cosine corrects for magnitude: $\cos(q,d) = \dfrac{q \cdot d}{\lVert q \rVert \lVert d \rVert}$, with $\lVert d \rVert = \sqrt{\sum_t d_t^2}$. Here $\lVert q \rVert = \sqrt2$, $\lVert A \rVert = \sqrt5$, $\lVert B \rVert = \sqrt{17}$, so A scores $3/\sqrt{10} \approx 0.949$ and B scores $5/\sqrt{34} \approx 0.857$. **The ranking reverses**: A's proportions point at the query, while B spends disproportionate weight on its second coordinate.
- **Range and scale invariance fall out of the definition.** With the nonnegative weights lexical retrieval uses, cosine lies in $[0,1]$: zero means no shared positive coordinate, one means identical direction. `[1,2]` and `[3,6]` have cosine exactly 1 — repeating a whole document does not change its topical direction.
- **The runtime form is not the textbook form.** For one query $\lVert q \rVert$ is constant across every candidate, so removing it preserves the ordering exactly. Document norms are computed at index time and stored; the scorer accumulates the numerator from query-term postings then multiplies by a stored inverse norm, with no vocabulary-wide scan. A ranking function can be neat and operationally poor; this is what makes cosine neither.
- **Cosine does not rescue a bad weighting scheme.** Weights shape the angle before cosine runs: a common term at raw count 20 beside a discriminative term at count 1 steers both directions. The pipeline is tokenize, weight, accumulate overlap, normalize — never cosine over unexamined counts.
- **Cosine decomposes into per-term evidence, and that is the debugging payoff.** A matched term contributes $q_t d_t / (\lVert q \rVert \lVert d \rVert)$: for `vector database`, an explanation might show 0.42 from `vector` and 0.31 from `database`, producing 0.73. Such traces expose a typo carrying nearly the whole score, or a title boost inflating one coordinate.
- **Cosine is not always the right rule.** Very short queries make the query direction coarse; exact identifiers want filters or boosts rather than smooth angular similarity; field structure is awkward when all text collapses into one vector; BM25 often calibrates length better. The judgment is empirical.
- **Document length normalization asks how much confidence a match in a large document deserves.** Without it a 5,000-word manual beats a focused 200-word answer by repeating query terms more often; over-normalize and the concise answer wins even when the manual holds the more useful discussion. Length is a confounder that also carries information.
- **Cosine normalizes by the norm, not by token count, and the distinction has teeth.** The norm depends on the weights and their distribution, so ten repetitions of one term give a different norm from ten distinct terms. $A = [3,0,0]$ has norm 3; $B = [1,1,1]$ has norm $\sqrt3 \approx 1.732$. Both carry three weighted units; normalization treats them very differently.
- **$\lVert d \rVert^{\alpha}$ turns the penalty into a dial.** $\alpha = 0$ is no normalization, $\alpha = 1$ is full cosine, $\alpha = 0.5$ the usual compromise. Two documents with numerator 6 and norms 2 and 8 tie at 6 unnormalized; full cosine gives 3 and 0.75; $\alpha = 0.5$ gives $6/\sqrt2 \approx 4.24$ and $6/\sqrt8 \approx 2.12$. The parameter controls the penalty's strength instead of assuming one universal strength.
- **Pivoted normalization compares each document with the collection average.** A schematic denominator is $(1-s) + s \cdot \dfrac{\text{length}(d)}{\text{avg length}}$, where $s$ is the slope of a line pivoting on the average. With $s = 0.2$ and average 500, a 1,000-word document gets $0.8 + 0.4 = 1.2$ and is penalized; a 250-word one gets $0.8 + 0.1 = 0.9$ and is modestly amplified. At $s = 0$ every denominator is 1; at $s = 1$ it is pure length ratio.
- **Length must be defined per field.** Body token count, unique-term count and weighted norm encode different assumptions. A 12-token product title is not unusually long because a body field averages 800, and normalizing them together suppresses useful title matches. Fielded systems keep a separate length statistic and average per channel.
- **Document type changes the right judgment, and norms are versioned state.** Long legal opinions may be relevant precisely because they are comprehensive; a support-answer collection rewards focus; source files inflate length with repeated syntax tokens. Segmenting long documents into passages usually beats hunting for one exponent. And stored norms computed before a tokenizer change produce incoherent scores with new weights — so index metadata must record tokenizer, vocabulary statistics, weighting formula, field configuration and norm calculation.

**Rules to remember**

- Axes come from the vocabulary; values from the weighting scheme. Two independent decisions.
- Only coordinates nonzero in both vectors contribute — which is why postings traversal is the implementation.
- No shared term means a dot product of zero and no lexical score. That is the representation, not a bug.
- Dot product ranks by magnitude; cosine by direction. The reversal above is the standard example.
- $\lVert q \rVert$ is constant within one query, so dropping it never changes an ordering.
- Cosine is exactly 1 for any positive scalar multiple: `[1,2]` and `[3,6]` point the same way.
- Cosine normalizes the norm, not the token count.
- $\alpha \in [0,1]$ tunes normalization strength; pivoted normalization tilts a line around average length with slope $s$.
- Changing the tokenizer or the weighting formula invalidates every stored norm.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Treating a bag-of-words vector as dense.** The geometry is million-dimensional; the storage is a handful of postings. Confusing them makes every cost estimate wrong.
- **Expecting semantic matching from lexical axes.** `car` and `automobile` are different coordinates.
- **Ranking by dot product and calling it cosine.** The orderings genuinely differ, and length separates them.
- **Thinking that dropping $\lVert q \rVert$ changes results.** It rescales every score in one query by the same constant, so the order is identical.
- **Confusing token count with vector norm.** `[3,0,0]` and `[1,1,1]` carry the same total weight and different norms.
- **Treating full normalization as the safe default.** $\alpha = 1$ is one point on a dial, and it is the point most favourable to short documents.
- **Normalizing every field against one average length.** A 12-token title against an 800-token body average is penalized for existing.
- **Tuning length handling by taste.** Slice evaluation by length bucket and query class — a parameter that improves the mean can wreck identifier queries.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Write the vectors down. Most bag-of-words questions collapse once the coordinates are on the page.
2. Given two candidate documents, compute *both* the dot product and the cosine. The interesting answers live where they disagree.
3. Ask whether a change alters direction or only magnitude. Scaling a whole vector leaves cosine untouched; changing one coordinate's share does not.
4. For any normalization question, identify which length is meant: token count, unique terms, or the weighted norm.
5. On $\alpha$ and pivot questions, evaluate the endpoints first ($\alpha = 0$ and $1$; $s = 0$ and $1$) — distractors usually get an endpoint wrong.
6. Treat "cosine is the best ranking function" as a flag; the right answer usually names BM25, an exact-identifier filter, or fielded scoring.
7. Separate the representation (which axes exist) from the comparison (what angle they make). Distractors swap them.
