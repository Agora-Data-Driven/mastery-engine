1. From Weights To Axes

Term Frequency and Its Saturation Problem and Inverse Document Frequency: Rare Terms Carry Signal handed you one number per term per document. They never said what to do with a bag of them. This lesson answers that: each term gets an axis, and the weight is the coordinate. Drag the count of index and watch the number from the last lesson become a position. Like scoring a dish for salt, acid and heat — and then plotting those three numbers so two dishes can actually be compared.

Representation and weighting are separate decisions. Bag-of-words fixes the axes; the weighting scheme decides how much evidence an active axis supplies — binary presence, a raw count, saturated tf, or the full tf-idf product. Everything you learned about weighting schemes still applies here unchanged. It is now choosing coordinate values instead of summands.

2. Building The Vector

A bag-of-words vector is one coordinate per vocabulary term, with word order thrown away. Build a document with the steppers and watch it meet the fixed query vector query = [0, 0, 1, 1]. Only axes nonzero in both contribute anything at all. Like two shopping lists compared item by item: what neither list mentions can never match.

Order is discarded on purpose, and it buys real invariance: "vector search ranks documents" and "documents rank vector search" activate identical coordinates, which is what lets an inverted index aggregate evidence term by term, independently. The price is structure — "dog bites person" and "person bites dog" have the same bag, and "not reliable" overlaps "reliable" almost perfectly. Phrase indexes, positional features or a reranker put some of that back.

3. Dot Product Versus Cosine

Two ranking rules over the same three vectors. The dot product rewards magnitude; cosine divides it out and ranks by direction. Drag B's second coordinate and watch the two rules hand you opposite winners. Like judging archers: one rule counts total arrows fired, the other asks how close to the bullseye they were pointing.

Cosine does not rescue a bad weighting scheme. The weights shape the angle before cosine ever runs: leave a common term at raw count 20 next to a discriminative term at count 1 and that common coordinate steers both directions. The pipeline is tokenize → weight → accumulate overlap → normalize, never cosine over unexamined counts. And cosine is not always the right rule at all — very short queries make the query direction coarse, exact identifiers want filters or large boosts, and BM25 often calibrates length better.

4. The Alpha Dial

Full cosine divides by ‖d‖. That is one point on a dial, not a law: dividing by ‖d‖α lets you choose how hard length is punished. Two documents with the identical numerator 6 and norms 2 and 8 — drag α from "ignore length" to "full cosine". Like grading an essay per page instead of per essay: the right divisor is a choice, and both extremes are defensible for someone.

Cosine normalizes by the vector norm, not by token count — and that distinction has teeth. The norm depends on the weights and their distribution, so ten repetitions of one term produce a different norm from ten distinct terms, especially after tf saturation and idf. Two documents carrying "three weighted units" can sit at norm 3 or norm 1.732 depending purely on how those units are spread.

5. Pivoted Length Penalty

Pivoted normalization takes a different view: compare each document with the collection average and tilt a line around that pivot. Denominator (1 − s) + s × length(d) / avg. Drag the slope and watch a 5,000-word manual trade places with a 200-word answer. Like a handicap in golf: the pivot is the average player, and the slope decides how much the strong get penalised.

Whatever the dial says, the definition of length must match the field and the tokenizer. A 12-token product title is not unusually long because a body field averages 800, and normalizing them together suppresses useful title matches — fielded systems keep a separate length statistic and average per channel. Norms are also versioned state: stored norms computed before a tokenizer change, combined with new term weights, make incoherent scores, so index metadata must record tokenizer, vocabulary statistics, weighting formula, field configuration and norm calculation. For very long documents, segmenting into passages usually beats hunting for one exponent that fixes everything.
