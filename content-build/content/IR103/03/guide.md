**The big idea**: **Term Frequency and Its Saturation Problem**, **Inverse Document Frequency: Rare Terms Carry Signal** and **tf-idf Variants and Weighting Schemes** gave you a weight for one term in one document; **Documents as Bag-of-Words Vectors**, **Cosine Similarity as a Ranking Function** and **Document Length Normalization** turned those weights into a single comparable score per document. All of them answered *what is this document's score*, and this lesson does not change that answer by a decimal place. It asks the other question: the user reads the first few results, so how much scoring can you refuse to do and still be certain your top k is the exhaustive scorer's top k? Efficient top-k retrieval preserves the scoring model while avoiding work that cannot change the final heap — three moves: organise the traversal, maintain a competitive threshold, and use safe upper bounds to prove when candidates or postings can be skipped.

**Key concepts**

- **The inverted index gives one posting list per query term, and a ranker must combine them.** For `vector database` take `vector: (2,1.5), (5,0.7), (9,1.0)` and `database: (2,0.8), (7,1.2), (9,0.6)` — each pair a document identifier and that term's score contribution. Two classic traversals organise the same arithmetic differently: **term-at-a-time vs document-at-a-time scoring**.
- **Term-at-a-time (TAAT) consumes one entire term's postings before moving to the next.** It adds the `vector` contributions into an accumulator — document 2 gets 1.5, 5 gets 0.7, 9 gets 1.0 — then processes `database`, raising 2 to 2.3, creating 7 at 1.2, raising 9 to 1.6. Only at the end does it select the largest scores.
- **TAAT's advantage is sequential access; its cost is accumulator state.** One list at a time is cache-friendly, parallelises by term, and permits blockwise vectorized accumulation and high-impact terms first. But a broad query can touch millions of distinct documents to return ten, and clearing a dense score array is itself expensive, so implementations track the touched slots.
- **Document-at-a-time (DAAT) advances all lists together in increasing document-identifier order** and finishes one candidate before the next. Both iterators start at document 2, so the scorer sums 1.5 and 0.8, finalizes 2.3, and advances both. The heads become 5 and 7: document 5 is finalized from `vector` alone at 0.7, then 7 from `database` alone at 1.2, and finally both meet at 9 for 1.6.
- **Complete scores in document order are what make a heap possible.** Each finished score feeds a min-heap of size k. Before the heap is full every candidate enters; once full, the root is the current **threshold** and a new candidate must exceed it. Memory is bounded by the iterators, the heap and small scoring state, not by touched documents — and coordinated iterator movement is the foundation for the rest of this lesson.
- **Neither traversal wins universally.** DAAT repeatedly switches among lists and naïve implementations pay branch and pointer costs; TAAT pays in accumulator size. Query length, list sizes, compression layout, cache behaviour, scoring complexity and available pruning rules decide the winner; a planner should read collection statistics, not fix one traversal for all queries. With lists of length 50, 50 and 5,000,000, a conjunctive query should drive traversal with a short list and seek into the long one; a disjunctive query over the frequent term creates many weak candidates, which is where high-idf terms first, or DAAT bounds, pay.
- **Semantics change the traversal.** Conjunctive retrieval requires every term, so DAAT can leap lagging iterators toward the current maximum identifier; disjunctive retrieval must consider the union, though top-k bounds may still avoid full scoring.
- **Index elimination reduces the postings considered before or during scoring.** The simplest form drops query terms of negligible discriminative value: with several content terms plus one extremely common term, ignoring the common one saves a long traversal and barely moves the ranking. It is a heuristic — the eliminated term may matter for a phrase, entity name or navigational query — so the policy must read query structure, not idf alone.
- **Champion lists keep only the documents likely to score highly for each term.** A champion list for term `t` holds the r documents with the largest term-specific weights for `t`, chosen at indexing time. If the full posting has 1,000,000 documents and its champion list has 1,000, the initial ranker scores a pool three orders of magnitude smaller — for `vector database`, the union of the two lists. Work moves from request time to index construction.
- **r is the dial, trading recall against cost.** At r = 100 retrieval is fast but competitive documents can be missed; at r = 100,000 recall improves and most of the saving disappears. Use a fixed r, a score cutoff, or a term-specific size — rare terms may need their whole short posting, frequent terms benefit from aggressive restriction. Measure recall of the exhaustive top k, not overlap of candidate sets.
- **They are least reliable where the final scorer is not a sum of independent term orderings** — freshness, personalization, proximity, field combinations. A document mediocre for each term can be excellent because it matches all of them. Static quality extends the idea into global champion lists and a **two-tier index**: high-quality postings in tier one, the rest in fallback.
- **The fallback condition is where heuristics and proofs separate.** Suppose k = 10 and the first tier returns 10 documents, but the heap threshold is only 0.15 while omitted postings could contribute as much as 0.9. Stopping is unjustified. If a safe upper bound proves every omitted candidate scores at most 0.12, stopping is correct. Heuristic index elimination accepts recall risk; bound-based elimination proves the skipped work could not change the answer.
- **WAND and MaxScore: skipping non-contenders turns that proof into a traversal.** For each query term the index supplies an upper bound on its possible contribution. If `vector` can contribute at most 1.4 and `database` at most 0.9, any document matching both scores at most 1.4 + 0.9 = 2.3 under an additive scorer. Anything a bound cannot lift above the current threshold is a non-contender.
- **WAND (Weak AND) finds a pivot.** Sort the document-ordered iterators by their current document identifiers, then accumulate term upper bounds from the front until the running sum exceeds the threshold; the identifier there is the pivot. With threshold 1.5 and iterators at documents 10, 14 and 14 carrying bounds 0.4, 0.7 and 0.8: 0.4 is insufficient; 0.4 + 0.7 = 1.1 is still insufficient; 1.1 + 0.8 = 1.9 > 1.5, so 14 is the pivot. If every iterator contributing to that bound already sits at the pivot, it is fully scored; otherwise the iterator at 10 advances toward 14, and if it jumps past, the iterators are reordered and the test repeats. Everything in between is skipped for want of possible evidence.
- **The threshold is dynamic, which is why early strong candidates accelerate everything after them.** Before the heap fills it is effectively negative infinity (or zero for nonnegative scores), so pruning is weak; as competitive documents arrive it rises and more candidates become provably irrelevant — without changing the scoring formula.
- **MaxScore splits terms instead of pivoting.** Terms whose cumulative maximum contribution cannot lift a candidate over the threshold become **nonessential**; essential postings drive candidate generation, and nonessential terms are consulted only when their remaining possible contribution might rescue a candidate. As the threshold rises, more terms cross over. That split — required traversal versus optional score refinement — is why MaxScore is worth knowing alongside the pivot.
- **Block-Max WAND tightens the bound where it matters.** Store an upper bound per block of a posting list rather than one loose maximum per term: if a term's global maximum is 4.0 but the next block's maximum is 0.3, the local bound supports a large skip the global value would forbid. Smaller blocks tighten bounds but cost metadata and decoding — block size is a space, CPU and pruning tradeoff.

**Rules to remember**

- TAAT finishes a term then moves on; DAAT finishes a document then moves on. TAAT's state is the accumulator, DAAT's is the heap.
- Only DAAT gives complete scores in document order, so only DAAT supports a live threshold — which is why WAND-style skipping is built on it.
- The threshold is the k-th best score so far. It only rises, and pruning strength rises with it.
- Bounds must cover **every** positive component of first-stage scoring, including field boosts. If a later feature can add 0.5, include its maximum or apply it only after a safely generated candidate set.
- An underestimated maximum can skip a true winner: a correctness bug, not a performance regression.
- Champion lists and term dropping are heuristics with recall risk; WAND and MaxScore are exact given valid bounds. Label which you shipped.
- Scores must not depend on traversal order: floating-point aside, TAAT and DAAT rank the same fully evaluated candidates identically.
- Decode positions for phrase and proximity features only *after* a document is a plausible candidate.
- Measure postings decoded, candidates fully scored, block skips and heap updates — not latency alone.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Believing a traversal strategy changes the ranking.** It changes which work is done, never the score definition.
- **Forgetting TAAT's accumulator cost.** "Only ten results" does not bound the documents touched, and clearing a dense array is real work.
- **Stopping because you have k results.** Ten first-tier hits at threshold 0.15 with omitted postings worth up to 0.9 is a guess, not an answer.
- **Loose bounds, or bounds missing a component.** One global maximum per term forbids skips a per-block maximum would permit; a boost applied after the bound was computed silently makes an exact algorithm approximate.
- **Ignoring filters.** If nine of ten champions fail an access-control filter you must expand, not return one result. Filters also starve the heap, so the threshold rises slowly and pruning stays weak.
- **Stale champion lists.** Weights, quality and deletions drift; store the construction version and rebuild with the scoring model. Per-segment champions with inconsistent statistics let a small new segment dominate or vanish.
- **Merging shard-local top-k with too few per-shard candidates** drops a globally competitive document before the coordinator sees it.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Decide first whether the question is about *which documents get scored* or *what a score is*. This lesson only ever changes the first.
2. For TAAT/DAAT questions, ask what the state is: an accumulator over touched documents, or a heap of size k. Memory and threshold claims follow.
3. For any skipping claim, ask whether it is a **proof** or a **guess**. Bound-based skipping is exact; dropping a term or trimming to r champions is not.
4. Trace pivots literally: sort by current document identifier, add upper bounds from the front, stop the moment the sum exceeds the threshold, read off the identifier there.
5. Watch the threshold's state — before the heap is full, pruning does almost nothing, and many "why was this slow" scenarios are exactly that. When a bound and a skip disagree, suspect an incomplete bound, not a broken algorithm.
6. Validate every efficiency claim against an exhaustive reference scorer on sampled queries — ties, missing terms, deleted documents, segment boundaries. Performance counts only after equivalence is demonstrated.

**Where this leads**: this is the last lesson of IR 103, and it closes the loop the course opened — tf-idf defined the score, the vector space model made scores comparable, top-k traversal makes them affordable. What it leaves open is the quality of the score itself: WAND and MaxScore are indifferent to whether the ranking function is any good, and both assume an additive scorer with per-term upper bounds. Probabilistic ranking and learned rankers come next, and each must be re-examined against this bound-and-threshold machinery — negative contributions and non-additive interactions require careful derivations, not a copied additive WAND implementation.
