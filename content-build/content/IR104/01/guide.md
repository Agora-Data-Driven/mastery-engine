**The big idea**: core ML gave you **precision**, **recall** and **F1**, computed over a *fixed predicted set* that a score threshold handed you. IR 103 then gave you the machinery that produces rankings — **tf-idf term weighting**, **cosine similarity as a ranking function** — and that machinery emits no set at all. Your production retriever fuses BM25 with embeddings under reciprocal rank fusion and then reranks; it emits an *ordered list*, and every tuning decision you make (fusion weights, rerank depth, how many chunks to stuff into a prompt) changes that order. A set metric is blind to most of what your system does. This lesson rebuilds evaluation for rankings in three steps: cutoff metrics (P@k, R@k), a whole-ranking summary that rewards putting relevant documents early (AP and MAP), and a first-hit metric for single-answer tasks (RR and MRR).

**Key concepts**

- **A classifier hands you a set; a retriever refuses to.** Precision is TP/(TP+FP) and recall is TP/(TP+FN) over a fixed predicted set, which a classifier produces by thresholding a score. BM25 or your fused hybrid scores essentially every document in the corpus, producing a total ordering, so "the retrieved set" is undefined until you choose a cutoff `k`. That cutoff is a modelling decision encoding how the output is consumed, not a nuisance parameter.
- **P@k and R@k are the old formulas with the cutoff supplying the set.** Cut at position `k` and treat the top-`k` as retrieved: $P@k = |\text{relevant} \cap \text{top-}k| / k$ and $R@k = |\text{relevant} \cap \text{top-}k| / R$, where $R$ is the total number of relevant documents for the query. Worked example: query `cloud run cold start latency`, $R = 4$, System A ranking them at positions 1, 3, 5 and 10. Then P@5 = 3/5 = 0.60 and R@5 = 3/4 = 0.75; P@10 = 4/10 = 0.40 and R@10 = 4/4 = 1.00.
- **The monotonicity is mechanical, and it reproduces a curve you already know.** As `k` grows the numerator is non-decreasing while $R$ is fixed, so recall can only rise or stay flat. Precision tends to fall because every increment adds 1 to the denominator and only sometimes 1 to the numerator. Sweeping `k` from 1 to the list depth traces exactly the precision–recall tradeoff curve you know from classifiers, with the rank cutoff playing the role of the score threshold.
- **What ranking changes, first: there is no single precision and recall per system** — there is a *pair per cutoff*, and you must pick cutoffs matching how the output is consumed.
- **What ranking changes, second — the subtle part: a cutoff metric is blind inside itself.** P@k and R@k are sensitive to rank only *across* the cutoff boundary, never *within* it. System B, ranking the same four relevant documents at positions 6, 7, 8, 9, ties System A at P@10 = 0.40 and R@10 = 1.00 — even though A answered at rank 1 and B made the user scan five junk results first. Swapping the documents at ranks 1 and 10 changes nothing at P@10. Any metric built from a single cutoff treats the top-`k` as a bag.
- **Two denominator gotchas bite in practice.** (1) If a query has fewer than `k` relevant documents, P@k cannot reach 1: with $R = 3$ the ceiling on P@10 is 0.3, so averaging P@10 across queries with wildly different $R$ punishes queries for having few relevant answers. **R-precision** — precision at cutoff $R$, i.e. P@R — adapts the cutoff per query and fixes this. (2) R@k needs $R$, the count of *all* relevant documents in the corpus, which nobody knows; in practice it is the count in the judged set, so recall is always relative to those judgments.
- **Choose `k` by consumption point.** A search results page: P@10. A RAG pipeline stuffing 5 chunks into the prompt: P@5 and R@5, because a relevant chunk at rank 6 does not exist as far as the LLM is concerned. A first-stage candidate generator feeding your reranker: R@100 or R@1000, and precision there barely matters — the reranker can reorder the top 200 but can never recover a document the first stage failed to surface. Missed recall at the first stage is unfixable downstream; poor precision is merely work for the next stage.
- **Average precision walks the ranking and averages precision at the relevant positions.** Mechanism: at each position where a relevant document appears, record the precision at that position, then average those values over $R$: $AP = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{d \in \text{relevant}} P@\text{rank}(d)$, with any relevant document never retrieved contributing exactly 0. Same ranking as above: P@1 = 1.0, P@3 = 2/3 ≈ 0.667, P@5 = 3/5 = 0.600, P@10 = 4/10 = 0.400, so AP = 2.667/4 ≈ 0.667. Move only the last relevant document from rank 10 up to rank 6: P@6 = 4/6 ≈ 0.667 and AP = 2.933/4 ≈ 0.733. P@10 and R@10 did not move; AP did. That is the whole point — AP sees the position of *every* relevant document.
- **AP is (approximately) the area under the uninterpolated precision–recall curve.** Derivation: each relevant hit raises recall by exactly $1/R$, so the $R$ recorded precisions sit at equally spaced recall increments; averaging them is a Riemann sum of width $1/R$ approximating $\int_0^1 P \, dR$.
- **Two further readings make AP less arbitrary.** It is the *expected precision experienced by a user who stops reading at a relevant document chosen uniformly at random* — crude but honest. And it is intrinsically top-heavy with no explicit discount function: rank 1 contributes 1.0, while rank 100 contributes a tiny P@100, precision at depth being diluted by everything above it.
- **MAP is the arithmetic mean of AP over the query set — a macro-average.** Each query carries equal weight whether it has 1 relevant document or 50. Queries with zero relevant documents in the judgments are excluded, because AP is undefined there ($R = 0$).
- **Reciprocal rank is the metric for tasks needing exactly one hit** — known-item search, navigational queries, factoid lookup, or a RAG setup where a single gold passage holds the answer. $RR = 1/r_1$ with $r_1$ the rank of the *first* relevant result, and $RR = 0$ if none appears (or none above the cutoff — MRR@10 is a common variant). MRR is the mean of RR over the query set. Worked example: three queries with first hits at ranks 1, 2 and 4 give 1.0, 0.5 and 0.25, so MRR = 1.75/3 ≈ 0.583. Add a fourth query with no relevant document in the top 10, scored under MRR@10: it contributes 0, and MRR = 1.75/4 ≈ 0.438.
- **Why the reciprocal rather than the rank itself.** Top-heaviness that matches user abandonment: dropping from rank 1 to rank 2 costs 0.5 of the score, while dropping from rank 9 to rank 10 costs about 0.011. And boundedness: RR lives in (0, 1], so one catastrophic query — first hit at rank 500 — cannot dominate the mean the way it would dominate *mean rank*, which is unbounded and outlier-hostile. By Jensen's inequality $E[1/r] \ge 1/E[r]$, so never convert an MRR back into "average position" — report the median first-relevant rank instead.
- **AP equals RR whenever $R = 1$ — derive it once and you never confuse the two again.** With exactly one relevant document at rank $r$, the AP sum has one term: $AP = \frac{1}{1} \cdot P@r = \frac{1}{r} = RR$, since precision at rank $r$ is one relevant document out of $r$ seen. So MRR is exactly MAP restricted to single-answer queries: with one judged passage per query, MAP and MRR are the same number and computing both tells you nothing.
- **All three families assume binary relevance.** Graded judgments must be squashed to binary first, and the squashing threshold can change which of two systems wins.

**Rules to remember**

- No cutoff, no set, no precision. `k` is part of the metric's definition.
- Recall is non-decreasing in `k`; precision generally falls. Sweeping `k` is the classifier's threshold sweep.
- A single-cutoff metric sees membership of the top-`k`, never order inside it.
- P@k has a ceiling of $\min(R, k)/k$. Use R-precision (P@R) when $R$ varies wildly across queries.
- AP averages precision at each relevant position over $R$; a missed relevant document contributes 0, not "nothing".
- MAP is a macro-average over queries; zero-relevant queries are excluded, not scored 0.
- RR = 1/first relevant rank, 0 if none. MRR is its mean, and $E[1/r] \ne 1/E[r]$.
- $R = 1 \Rightarrow AP = RR$, so MRR is MAP on single-answer queries.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Reporting "precision and recall" for a retriever with no `k` stated.** The numbers are meaningless without the cutoff, and distractors exploit exactly this.
- **Believing P@10 rewards putting the answer at rank 1.** It does not. Systems A and B tie at 0.40 with radically different user experiences.
- **Averaging P@10 over queries with very different $R$.** Queries with $R = 3$ are capped at 0.3 and drag the mean down for a reason unrelated to system quality.
- **Optimising precision at the first stage.** Recall lost before the reranker is unrecoverable; precision lost there is merely work.
- **Treating an unretrieved relevant document as "excluded" from AP.** It contributes a hard 0, which is what makes AP recall-aware.
- **Using MRR for a recall-oriented task.** It is blind past the first hit: one relevant document followed by nine junk results ties ten relevant documents. A RAG prompt built from five chunks cares about all five slots.
- **Converting MRR into an average position.** Jensen's inequality says that number is wrong, and wrong in a flattering direction.
- **Forgetting the MRR@10 cliff.** A first hit moving from rank 10 (0.1) to rank 11 (0.0) reads as total failure — an artifact of the cutoff, not a user-visible catastrophe.
- **Trusting a small MRR delta.** Per-query RR takes only the values 1, 0.5, 0.333, 0.25 …, so the distribution is coarse and high-variance. Per-query AP is likewise noisy and heavy-tailed — report the distribution, not just the mean.

**How to approach the questions**

1. First ask what the metric can *see*. P@k and R@k see membership of the top-`k`; AP sees every relevant document's position; RR sees the first hit and nothing after. Most distractors attribute one family's sensitivity to another.
2. When two systems tie on a cutoff metric but differ in description, the intended answer is almost always "the cutoff metric is order-blind inside `k`" — reach for AP or MRR.
3. For "which metric should we report" questions, identify the consumer: fixed-size prompt or results page → P@k / R@k at that size; single-answer lookup → MRR; whole-ranking or recall-oriented → MAP; first-stage candidate generation → R@k at large `k`.
4. Compute AP by writing the precision at each relevant rank in a column and dividing by $R$, not by the number of hits found. That division is the most common arithmetic slip.
5. Watch the denominators. $R$ (all relevant) versus `k` (the cutoff) versus the number of queries in a mean — swapping any two produces a plausible wrong number.
6. If a question says "one relevant document per query", MAP and MRR are the same number; any option claiming they differ is wrong.
7. Treat "the score did not change" as information: which metrics were invariant tells you which part of the ranking moved.

**Where this leads**: every metric here reads relevance as a yes/no bit.
