1. Binary Loses Levels

Precision@k and Recall@k, Average Precision and MAP and Reciprocal Rank and MRR all read one bit per document: relevant, or not. Set the grades and the squashing threshold, and watch what that bit throws away. Like marking every exam pass or fail: the ranking of the students survives the paperwork only if you keep the scores.

Note which lever moves which number. AP moves only when the threshold pushes a document across the yes/no line, or when a counted document changes rank. nDCG moves whenever any grade or any position moves. That is the whole reason graded metrics exist — and also why the squashing threshold you pick before computing MAP is a modelling decision, not a formatting one.

2. Gain, Then Discount

Cumulative Gain adds the grades up. Reorder the list and it never moves, because addition is commutative. Discounting divides each grade by a function of its rank — and the gain transform decides how much a top grade is worth in the first place. Like paying by the seat: the same four people are in the room, but the front row costs more.

The gain mapping is a utility assumption, and it must be documented alongside the number. Exponential gain says one authoritative answer beats several marginal ones — plausible in medical or legal search, wrong for exploratory browsing where several diverse, moderately relevant results may collectively be better. Two dashboards both labelled "DCG" can be computing materially different quantities.

3. Divide By Ideal

Raw DCG cannot be averaged across queries: one query has far more attainable gain than another. nDCG divides by the ideal ordering of the same judged grades, truncated at the same cutoff. Drag the cutoff, set the grades, and push every grade to 0 to meet the edge case. Like grading on a curve against the best possible answer for that question, not against every other question's.

Two policies must be written down or the number is not reproducible. Zero IDCG: when no judged document has positive relevance the division is undefined, so decide whether those queries are excluded from the average or assigned a value — silently inconsistent handling has flipped experiment conclusions on its own. Unjudged documents: scoring them 0 is convenient but they are not proven irrelevant, and it penalises exactly the new system that surfaces documents outside the original judging pool.

4. MAP Or nDCG

Two systems, the same two relevant documents, different orders. MAP always prefers B — it retrieves the whole relevant set with no interruption. Change what the two documents are worth and watch nDCG disagree. Like judging a shortlist: did you find everyone qualified, or did you put the best candidate at the top?

Neither metric captures all product value. MAP does not express graded utility; nDCG applies little pressure to find every relevant item when k is shallow; neither measures novelty, diversity, latency, trust, harmful content or task completion — a recommender showing ten near-identical highly relevant items can post excellent nDCG and a poor experience. And both weight every query equally by default regardless of traffic, so report macro averages alongside traffic-weighted summaries and per-query deltas. A metric value without its variation is weak evidence.
