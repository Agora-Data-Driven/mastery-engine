**The big idea**: **Precision@k and Recall@k: What Ranking Changes**, **Average Precision and MAP**, **Reciprocal Rank and MRR**, **Cumulative Gain and Discounting: nDCG** and **MAP vs nDCG: Choosing by Relevance Model** all assume the same gift: that somebody handed you a set of queries and a set of relevance judgments. This lesson builds that gift. A retrieval metric is only as credible as the queries and judgments underneath it, so building a test collection means sampling information needs, finding documents worth judging, and labelling them with known limitations. The objective is not a frozen leaderboard but a **repeatable instrument** that detects useful changes without hiding important user groups. Three moving parts — which queries you sample, which documents you can afford to judge, who produces the labels — and one judgment call: how much of a public benchmark transfers.

**Key concepts**

- **Golden Query Sets: Sampling Real Queries — "golden" means trusted, versioned and deliberately constructed, not perfect or permanent.** A golden query set is a maintained collection of queries representing the product's information needs, with enough judgment data for repeatable evaluation. Every metric you have learned is an average *over this set*, so the set defines what the number can see.
- **Production logs are the best sampling frame, and a uniform sample of them is a trap.** Logs expose real wording, spelling errors, identifiers and ambiguous short queries. But if 60 percent of traffic is navigational, a uniform 100-query set holds about **60 navigational cases** and one or two rare safety, multilingual or no-answer cases: too few to diagnose anything. **Stratified sampling** reserves capacity for meaningful classes and *records the weights* needed to estimate overall performance, so a deliberately non-traffic-shaped set still yields a traffic-level estimate.
- **Strata follow plausible failure mechanisms, not arbitrary buckets.** Useful axes: query frequency, length, language, device, geography, intent, difficulty, business-critical workflows. A support search might separate exact error codes, symptom descriptions, how-to questions, policy questions and account-specific requests, each exercising different tokenization and ranking behaviour.
- **Deduplicate carefully, and keep the collection current.** Exact duplicates overweight one popular wording, but collapsing `ERR_CONNECTION_RESET` into "browser says connection reset" erases a variant testing entirely different processing. Strip secrets and personal data. Language and inventory drift, so hold **800 queries as a stable core and refresh 200 each quarter**: trends stay interpretable while coverage stays current.
- **Every query needs enough context to define intent, and context is not free.** `jaguar speed` is an animal or a car; session context, locale and filters resolve it but create privacy and reproducibility problems. Record only what assessors and ranking systems may use, and note the adjudicated intent.
- **Never let system success be the admission rule.** Keeping only queries with clicks favours the incumbent and excludes the abandonment and zero-result failures you are trying to fix. Clicks are behaviour under one ranking, not labels. Report macro (each query equal) **and** traffic-weighted numbers, and slice by stratum: a **five-point average gain can conceal a severe regression on low-frequency compliance queries**.
- **Pooling: Judging Relevance Feasibly at Scale — judge the union of several systems' top results, not the corpus.** Exhaustive judging is impossible, so take the top-*k* from several retrieval systems, deduplicate, and judge that union. **Four systems contributing their top 20 with heavy overlap may yield 35 unique documents** rather than 80 slots, and rather than the whole collection. Pooling concentrates effort where competitive systems expect relevance, and yields reusable labels.
- **Pool depth is a budget decision with a stability test attached.** At depth 10 labels are cheap but cover only obvious winners; at depth 100 recall improves and cost grows. Measure how metrics stabilise as depth increases: **if system order changes substantially between depth 20 and 50, the shallow pool is weak evidence** about the ordering you wanted.
- **Pooling bias is the major limitation, and it punishes the systems you most want to find.** Unjudged documents are treated as irrelevant at evaluation time, so a novel system retrieving relevant material *outside* the contributors' pools scores as though that material were junk. Concretely: a challenger with six relevant documents in its top ten, against a lexical-only pool that judged two, loses four contributions to its DCG entirely, and the incumbent wins a comparison it should have lost. Add a dense contributor or deepen the pool and the verdict on identical systems can flip.
- **Mitigation is coverage work, not a formula.** Use diverse contributors (lexical, semantic, freshness-oriented, specialised), judge the top results of every serious new system, refresh pools after major model changes, and sample unjudged results from new systems to estimate their relevance yield. Random documents serve as controls but yield little. Metrics for incomplete judgments help; none replaces inspecting coverage.
- **Judging must be blinded to contributing system and rank**, or an assessor infers that a top-ranked result deserves a high label and the evaluation measures the ranking it should be testing. Randomise presentation, define grades with examples, measure agreement on overlapping assignments, and *adjudicate* conflicts exposing rubric ambiguity. Record provenance (system versions, retrieval dates, depth per contributor, snapshots) because documents change after labelling. The stopping question is never "is everything judged"; it is **whether remaining incompleteness could reverse the decision**, answered with saturation curves and audits.
- **LLM-as-Relevance-Judge: Uses and Failure Modes — a measurement instrument with calibration error, not an oracle.** Give a model the query, an optional intent description and a candidate document; it returns a grade and rationale. Good uses: preliminary labels, rubric testing, disagreement triage, scaling where human review is expensive. The prompt must define relevance *operationally* (does topicality, completeness, authority, recency or harmfulness affect the grade?) with boundary examples and a structured output such as `{grade, rationale, evidence}`.
- **The failure modes are systematic, and paired counterexamples measure them.** Position bias appears when candidates are shown in ranked order or with incumbent scores; verbosity bias favours long documents repeating the query language; style bias favours polished prose over terse correct records; citation-like formatting is mistaken for authority. Change only the suspected feature and the grade difference *is* the bias. Blinding and randomising inputs kills the first.
- **Hallucinated support is caught by evidence, not by confidence.** Require the judge to point at passages, then verify the cited text exists, which is what catches an adversarial document carrying the query terms with the wrong answer. A confident rationale is not proof; high-stakes cases still go to humans.
- **Calibration is a confusion matrix, not an accuracy number — and grade drift attacks nDCG multiplicatively.** Draw a human-labelled sample from the relevant query classes and compare agreement, confusion matrices and per-grade precision, never one number. With exponential gain $2^g - 1$, grade 2 is worth 3 and grade 3 is worth 7, so a judge mapping human 2 to 3 multiplies that document's contribution by $7/3 \approx 2.33$ before any discount. Three inflated 2s near the top move nDCG far more than "one grade off" sounds. Recalibrate the prompt, or **collapse grades** when the distinction is unreliable.
- **Judge and system can share a bias, and versions move under you.** A retriever and judge from similar training distributions reward the same familiar phrasing: use multiple judge families or human audits. Version the model identifier, prompt, decoding settings, truncation and rubric, and recalibrate before adopting a new judge. Route by confidence (auto-accept clear grade-0 cases, escalate uncertain ones, audit random accepted ones) but validate that confidence predicts correctness first.
- **Public Benchmarks in Context: MS MARCO and BEIR — shared reference points, not proof of production quality.** **MS MARCO** grew from real search queries paired with passages or documents and relevance information, and became central to passage ranking and learned retrieval, pushing rerankers, dense retrieval and efficient first-stage systems; its labels are relatively sparse, so unjudged relevant passages and dataset-specific supervision must be weighed. **BEIR** is a heterogeneous suite for zero-shot retrieval across many datasets and domains; its value is breadth, since a method strong on a web-style corpus may struggle on scientific claims, biomedical text, arguments or question answering. Report the average *alongside* per-dataset numbers or you cannot tell a broad gain from a concentrated one.
- **Transfer depends on matching mechanisms; contamination and test-set tuning both inflate scores.** A support product full of short error codes needs exact lexical behaviour a semantic benchmark barely represents, and a multilingual catalogue cannot be validated by English-only results. Benchmark text may sit in model training data, so strong scores can reflect familiarity; use held-out private tests and newer data. Tuning prompts, fusion weights or checkpoints on test results turns the test set into development data. Sound order: reproduce baselines to catch indexing and metric bugs, test across heterogeneous datasets, then decide on a private golden set, reporting corpus, query split, metric cutoff and resource budget — a one-point nDCG gain bought with a hundredfold latency increase is irrelevant to production.

**Rules to remember**

- The metric is only as credible as the query set and the judgments underneath it.
- Uniform sampling of logs buys the head of the distribution only; stratify, and record the weights.
- Never admit queries on the basis that the current system succeeded on them.
- Report macro and traffic-weighted, and slice by stratum every time.
- Pooling judges the union of contributors' top-*k*; unjudged means *scored as irrelevant*.
- A pool from one retrieval family cannot fairly evaluate another, and if ranking order moves with depth the pool is too shallow to decide.
- Blind the assessor — and the LLM judge — to system, rank and score.
- Verify a judge's quoted evidence actually exists in the document.
- Version model, prompt, decoding, rubric and truncation; a provider update silently relabels your collection.
- Public benchmarks verify implementations; a private golden set decides product questions.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Building the set from queries you already answer well.** Convenience and click-gated sampling encode the incumbent's strengths as the task definition.
- **Reading one headline number.** The average rises five points while the compliance stratum collapses; both facts sit in the same run.
- **Comparing a semantic retriever against a lexical-only pool.** The textbook pooling-bias failure, and it looks exactly like a worse system.
- **Treating "unjudged" as "irrelevant" without saying so.** It is the default in most evaluation scripts, and the assumption doing the damage.
- **Showing the judge the ranked order.** The evaluation then agrees with whatever produced that order, and a fluent rationale seals it — verbosity, polish and citation formatting all raise grades without raising relevance.
- **Reporting BEIR as one average**, which hides where a gain lives — or tuning on a public test set and still calling it a test result.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Ask what the number is an average *over*. Most scenarios are about the sampling frame, not the formula.
2. When a stratum is described as rare, the answer is usually detection: too few queries in a class means no diagnosis, whatever the headline says.
3. For pooling, identify contributors and depth first, then ask whose relevant documents fall *outside* that union. That system is penalised, and the bias always favours the incumbent.
4. For judge questions, name the failure mode (position, verbosity, style, authority-by-formatting, hallucinated evidence) and the paired test isolating it.
5. Where grades appear, exponential gain makes each step worth more than the last, so calibration errors near the top of a list dominate.
6. For benchmarks, ask whether the *mechanism* transfers — language, lexical exactness, terminology, freshness, intent. If not, the score is not evidence about your product.
