**The big idea**: **Golden Query Sets: Sampling Real Queries** and **Pooling: Judging Relevance Feasibly at Scale** built you an instrument, **LLM-as-Relevance-Judge: Uses and Failure Modes** warned that it has biases of its own, **Public Benchmarks in Context: MS MARCO and BEIR** put it in perspective, and **Precision@k and Recall@k**, **Average Precision and MAP**, **Reciprocal Rank and MRR** and **Cumulative Gain and Discounting: nDCG** taught you to read its dial. This lesson is about not being fooled by the reading. Experiments fail when a precise number is mistaken for complete evidence. Offline metrics, online behaviour, statistical tests and qualitative error analysis are four instruments, each measuring a different part of performance and each with its own way of lying — and discipline means knowing which is which before you ship.

**Key concepts**

- **Offline evaluation holds the inputs fixed — its strength and its limit.** A fixed query set plus fixed judgments applied to candidate rankings is fast, repeatable and safe: dozens of configurations compared without exposing a user, and because the inputs are constant, per-query differences can be inspected and reproduced. Excellent for catching regressions and diagnosing mechanics.
- **The central offline lie is that judgments only approximate user value.** A label says a document is relevant. It does not say whether the wording inspires trust, whether the answer is understandable, or whether the user finishes the task. Add a stale query sample, pools that omitted novel relevant documents, and a cutoff `k` that does not match the interface, and offline evaluation is *internally correct, externally wrong*.
- **Worked case.** A ranker raises nDCG@10 from **0.55 to 0.58** by promoting long authoritative manuals; on a support site, users prefer concise steps and abandon them. The gain is real under those labels and that gain mapping — the measurement model values authority above successful resolution. The remedy is not to discard offline results: revise labels or add task-oriented signals, then confirm online.
- **Online evaluation measures users, usually by A/B test.** Randomly assigning eligible traffic to control and treatment isolates the causal effect. Metrics: successful reformulation rate, click-through, dwell time, save rate, conversion, task completion — with guardrails on latency, errors and harm.
- **Online metrics lie in their own way.** Position bias means high-ranked results collect clicks independently of relevance; a flashy but poor result attracts them too. Long dwell can be careful reading *or* confusion; short dwell can be abandonment *or* an immediate answer. Revenue can rise while trust decays. Every behavioural metric needs a theory connecting it to user value, plus counter-metrics for the gaming paths.
- **Interference and instrumentation break the A/B model before any statistics run.** Recommendations shown to one user change inventory or social signals seen by others; caches mix treatment behaviour; returning users cross devices; logging differences between variants can *manufacture* a metric change. Verify assignment integrity, exposure, event schemas, sample ratios and missing data first. Novelty and learning effects matter too — cover weekly cycles, read the time series.
- **The two form a funnel, and disagreement is diagnostic.** Offline gates reject inferior or unsafe candidates; survivors get a controlled online test; then you return to the offline set to see which queries predicted or contradicted the outcome. Offline up / online down suggests label mismatch, latency cost, presentation effects or gaming. Offline down / online up suggests incomplete judgments, better diversity, or a misleading proxy. Find the mechanism before crowning an instrument.
- **A mean hides the distribution of per-query outcomes.** System A scores nDCG `[0.9, 0.9, 0.1, 0.1]`; system B scores `[0.6, 0.6, 0.4, 0.4]`. Both average **0.5**. A is excellent on half the queries and poor on half; B is consistent. Product risk depends on which pattern you have, and which queries sit in the bad half.
- **Comparison is naturally paired, and pairing is a variance argument.** Both rankers run the same queries, so compute `delta_q = metric_B(q) - metric_A(q)`. Why it matters: $\mathrm{Var}(B-A) = \mathrm{Var}(A) + \mathrm{Var}(B) - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(A,B)$, which on the numbers above is $0.16 + 0.01 - 2(0.04) = 0.09$ — a standard deviation of **0.30** rather than the **0.412** an unpaired analysis carries. Shared difficulty cancels.
- **Variance has identifiable sources, including the metric itself.** Query difficulty, intent, how many relevant documents exist, judgment completeness — and metric discreteness: Reciprocal Rank jumps sharply when the first relevant result moves one position, while nDCG over graded documents is smoother. Report median and quantiles beside the mean, plus the fraction improved, tied and regressed.
- **The paired bootstrap turns per-query deltas into an interval.** Resample queries *with replacement*, recompute the mean paired delta per resample, take percentiles of those means. If 10,000 resamples give a central 95 percent interval of **0.004 to 0.018**, zero is outside it and the change is unlikely to be sampling noise. The interval narrows as $1/\sqrt{n}$ in *queries*, not resamples.
- **Randomization (permutation) tests are the other paired route.** Under a null of exchangeable system labels, swap A and B *within each pair* at random and recompute the mean difference; the p-value is the fraction of randomized differences at least as extreme as observed. That is evidence against a null — not importance, and not the probability the new system is good.
- **Statistical significance is not practical significance.** On a very large query set a mean nDCG increase of **0.0002** can be statistically clear and operationally irrelevant; a large estimate with a wide interval argues for more data, not rejection. Define a minimum practically important effect first, then read the estimate and interval against it.
- **Multiple comparisons manufacture discoveries.** Try 100 configurations, report the smallest p-value, and chance looks like progress: at 5 percent per independent test the chance of at least one spurious "win" is $1 - 0.95^{100} \approx 0.994$. Separate exploration from confirmation, keep an untouched final test set, correct for multiplicity, log the failures.
- **Query dependence invalidates naïve resampling.** Near-duplicates, or many queries from one session, are not independent evidence. Resample at the cluster level — user, intent group, session — and online, use the randomization unit.
- **Error analysis by query class explains *why* the aggregate moved.** Start from per-query deltas, inspect the largest regressions and improvements, assign each query to classes tied to actionable mechanisms. You want repeated failure patterns, not a story built on three examples.
- **Two label families, answering different questions.** *Intent*: navigational, informational, transactional, exact identifier, entity, broad topic, multi-hop, multilingual, freshness-sensitive, no-answer — product-specific classes (policies, project codes, exact SKU, compatibility) are usually stronger. *Failure mechanism*: tokenization mismatch, synonym gap, overaggressive stemming, stale document, missing index coverage, poor length normalization, semantic false positive, filter failure, reranker truncation. Write guidelines so analysts apply them consistently.
- **Report a class the way you report the experiment**: query count, baseline, mean delta, uncertainty, win rate, severe regressions, plus traffic or risk weights. **3 queries and a 0.20 gain is a lead, not proof. 500 queries with a 0.02 loss demands attention even if the overall number rises.** Slicing after the fact is p-hacking: preregister a core taxonomy, treat new classes as hypotheses for the *next* evaluation.
- **The worked slice.** A hybrid retriever's mean nDCG rises **0.015**; slicing shows broad natural-language queries improve **0.04** while exact error-code queries fall **0.12**. Inspection finds semantic candidates consuming the limited reranking pool and squeezing out exact lexical matches. The fix is protected exact-match candidates or query-dependent fusion — not reverting hybrid.
- **Qualitative inspection needs a worksheet, and it must separate label errors from ranking errors.** Record query and intent, both rankings, labels, score contributions, candidate-source coverage, filters, suspected root cause. A useful *unjudged* page means the evaluation set needs repair.
- **Counterfactual debugging localizes the loss to one stage.** In order: does the document exist in the corpus, is it indexed, does it enter lexical or semantic candidates, survive fusion, reach the reranker, survive filters? Each stage has a different remedy; a top-k miss identifies none of them. Then prioritize by impact, frequency, severity and tractability — a tokenizer bug may help thousands of queries, a rare compliance failure may outrank a larger average gain — and turn root causes into golden-set regression suites, without letting past incidents dominate it.

**Rules to remember**

- Offline evidence is a gate, online evidence the verdict, neither authoritative alone. Offline can be internally correct and externally wrong — labels model value, they are not value.
- Every behavioural metric needs a theory linking it to user value and a counter-metric for gaming.
- Check assignment, exposure, schemas, sample ratios and missing data *before* interpreting an online result.
- Compare paired, per query; report the distribution — median, quantiles, win/tie/loss — never the mean alone.
- Intervals answer "how big, how sure"; p-values answer "how surprising under a null". Neither answers "should we ship".
- Define the minimum practically important effect before you look at the result, and resample at the unit of independence.
- Fix the corpus snapshot, the ordering and the metric code first — an interval around a buggy metric is precise misinformation.
- Three queries is a lead; five hundred with a small loss is a priority; and slices discovered after the fact are hypotheses for the next experiment, not findings in this one.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Treating an offline win as a decision.** The 0.55 → 0.58 manual-promoting ranker is a real gain under the labels and still wrong.
- **Reading dwell time as satisfaction**, or reporting a pooled online mean over a novelty period. Long dwell can mean confused; learning effects hide in an average.
- **Quoting a mean and stopping.** `[0.9, 0.9, 0.1, 0.1]` and `[0.6, 0.6, 0.4, 0.4]` are one number and two different products.
- **Unpaired analysis of paired data**, or bootstrapping over dependent queries — one inflates the variance you must overcome, the other makes the interval tighter than the evidence supports.
- **Confusing statistical and practical significance in either direction.** A 0.0002 gain clearing a threshold is not progress; a promising gain with a wide interval is not a rejection. And the best of 100 runs reported as the only run carries a ~99 percent chance of a spurious winner.
- **Blaming the ranker for a label error** — an unjudged but useful result is a defect in the evaluation set — or **stopping at "it missed at top-k"**, which cannot separate indexing from fusion from filtering.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Name the instrument, then its characteristic lie. Offline → labels approximate value. Online → bias, ambiguity, interference, novelty. Test statistic → silent about importance.
2. When offline and online disagree, do not pick a winner: the expected answer names a *mechanism* — label mismatch, latency, presentation, gaming, incomplete judgments, diversity, a bad proxy.
3. For any significance question, check pairing first, then the resampling unit, then whether the claim is about size (interval) or surprise (p-value).
4. Whenever a scenario mentions many configurations, metrics or slices, multiplicity is almost certainly the point.
5. For class-level findings, look at `n` before the delta: small `n` with a big gain is a lead, large `n` with a small loss is a priority.
6. When a class regresses, trace the pipeline stage rather than proposing a rollback — the exact-error-code case is a reranking-pool problem, not an argument against hybrid retrieval.
7. Watch for absolutes: "significant therefore ship", "online is ground truth", "the mean improved so the system did".
