**The big idea**: IR 104 taught you to *measure* a ranking — **Precision@k and Recall@k: What Ranking Changes**, **Average Precision and MAP** — and IR 103 handed you a weight that simply worked, **Inverse Document Frequency: Rare Terms Carry Signal**, with no account of why it should. This lesson closes both loops. BM25 looks like a wall of arbitrary constants until you see where it comes from: a chain of probability arguments beginning with one decision-theoretic claim about what an optimal ranker computes. Here we derive its first three links — the Probability Ranking Principle, the Binary Independence Model, and the Robertson–Spärck Jones term weight — using only odds, Bayes' rule and conditional independence. The payoff is concrete: the idf inside every BM25 implementation is no heuristic someone eyeballed. It falls out as the special case of "we know nothing about which documents are relevant."

**Key concepts**

- **The Probability Ranking Principle (PRP), Robertson 1977: present documents in order of decreasing $P(R=1 \mid d, q)$, estimated as well as the available evidence allows.** Model relevance as a binary random variable $R \in \{0,1\}$ per document; every classical model here computes something rank-equivalent to that probability.
- **Why that ordering is optimal and not merely plausible.** Assume the estimates are correct; fix any cutoff $k$. The expected number of relevant documents in the top $k$ is the sum of the $k$ probabilities you put there — expectation is linear, so this holds regardless of any dependence between documents' relevance. That sum is maximised by choosing the $k$ largest, and one descending sort achieves this *for every $k$ simultaneously*, so PRP maximises expected precision@k and recall@k at all cutoffs at once. With candidates at $P(R{=}1) = 0.9, 0.7, 0.4, 0.2$, expected precision@2 is $(0.9 + 0.7)/2 = 0.80$; swap the second and third and it drops to $(0.9 + 0.4)/2 = 0.65$ — expected relevant results given away for nothing.
- **The decision-theoretic form, and why calibration is a separate property from ranking.** If showing a non-relevant document costs $C_{fp}$ and missing a relevant one costs $C_{fn}$, the Bayes-optimal rule retrieves $d$ when $P(R=1 \mid d, q) > C_{fp}/(C_{fp}+C_{fn})$. With $C_{fp} = 1$, $C_{fn} = 4$ — missing an answer is four times worse than noise — the threshold is $0.2$. Deciding which retrieved chunks deserve context-window budget in RAG *is* this rule, and it only works on calibrated probabilities. Ranking needs only the correct ordering; thresholding needs the actual numbers.
- **Rank-equivalence is the engine of every derivation that follows.** Any strictly monotone transform of the probability — the odds $O = P/(1-P)$, a logarithm — preserves the ordering, and any positive factor identical across documents can be discarded. That licence collapses an intractable-looking posterior into a sum over query terms.
- **What PRP quietly assumes, and when experts knowingly violate it.** It treats each document's usefulness as independent of what else is ranked, so ten near-duplicates of one answer all score high and PRP fills the top ten with them. Maximal Marginal Relevance, and deduplication before assembling a RAG context, break PRP deliberately for that reason. It also conditions on the query as stated, not the intent behind it, and its optimality is a claim about *correct* probabilities — a badly estimated model ranked "optimally" is still bad.
- **The Binary Independence Model (BIM) is the first workable estimate of $P(R=1 \mid d, q)$, and its title names its two simplifications.** *Binary*: each document is an incidence vector $\vec{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_V)$ over the vocabulary, $x_t = 1$ if term $t$ appears — term frequency discarded entirely. *Independence*: term occurrences are conditionally independent given the relevance class, the move a naive Bayes classifier makes. Two assumptions ride along: relevance is binary, and each document's relevance is independent of the others'.
- **The derivation, in five moves.** (1) Rank by odds rather than probability — monotone, and it makes Bayes collapse: $O(R \mid \vec{x}, q) = \frac{P(R=1\mid q)}{P(R=0\mid q)} \cdot \frac{P(\vec{x}\mid R=1,q)}{P(\vec{x}\mid R=0,q)}$, because $P(\vec{x} \mid q)$ cancels. (2) The prior odds are identical for every document, so drop them. (3) Conditional independence factors the likelihood ratio across terms; with $p_t = P(x_t{=}1 \mid R{=}1, q)$ and $u_t = P(x_t{=}1 \mid R{=}0, q)$ it is $\prod_{t: x_t=1} \frac{p_t}{u_t} \prod_{t: x_t=0} \frac{1-p_t}{1-u_t}$. (4) For terms *not in the query*, assume $p_t = u_t$; those factors become 1 and both products run over query terms only. (5) The second product still ranges over query terms *absent* from the document, so it varies per document. Fix it algebraically: for each query term present, multiply by $\frac{1-p_t}{1-u_t}$ and divide it back out. The multiplied copies complete that product over *all* query terms — document-independent, hence droppable — and the divided copies join the first.
- **What survives is the Retrieval Status Value (RSV).** $O(R \mid \vec{x}, q) \propto \prod_{t \in q \cap d} \frac{p_t(1-u_t)}{u_t(1-p_t)}$, and one more log step gives $RSV(d) = \sum_{t \in q \cap d} \log \frac{p_t(1-u_t)}{u_t(1-p_t)}$. Micro-example: query `gke autoscaler` with $p_{gke} = 0.6$, $u_{gke} = 0.02$, $p_{auto} = 0.4$, $u_{auto} = 0.01$. The `gke` weight is $\ln \frac{0.6 \times 0.98}{0.02 \times 0.4} = \ln 73.5 \approx 4.30$, `autoscaler` $\ln \frac{0.4 \times 0.99}{0.01 \times 0.6} = \ln 66 \approx 4.19$. Both terms: $\approx 8.49$. Only `gke`: $4.30$. Neither: $0$.
- **The *shape* of the result outlives every assumption used to reach it.** A sum, over query terms present in the document, of a per-term weight is precisely what an inverted index makes cheap: walk the postings lists of the query terms only, accumulate per document, never touch the rest of the collection. BIM's assumptions are false in detail — in `new york` the terms are wildly dependent, a binary vector cannot tell one mention from thirty, length is invisible — and the last two are exactly what BM25 repairs. The additive-over-matched-terms skeleton is permanent.
- **The RSJ term weight is that per-term log-odds, read as a difference.** $c_t = \log \frac{p_t(1-u_t)}{u_t(1-p_t)}$ is $\log\frac{p_t}{1-p_t}$ — how strongly relevant documents attract the term — minus $\log\frac{u_t}{1-u_t}$, how strongly non-relevant ones do. With $N$ documents of which $n_t$ contain $t$, and $R$ judged relevant of which $r_t$ contain $t$, the contingency table has cells $r_t$, $n_t - r_t$, $R - r_t$ and $N - n_t - R + r_t$. Raw maximum-likelihood estimates $\hat{p}_t = r_t/R$ and $\hat{u}_t = (n_t-r_t)/(N-R)$ explode whenever a cell is zero: a term in every judged-relevant document gives $\hat{p}_t = 1$ and infinite log-odds. Add $0.5$ to every cell — a continuity correction in the spirit of a Jeffreys prior — giving $c_t = \log \frac{(r_t+0.5)/(R-r_t+0.5)}{(n_t-r_t+0.5)/(N-n_t-R+r_t+0.5)}$.
- **Worked example with feedback.** $N = 100{,}000$, `gke` with $n_t = 2{,}000$; a user judges $R = 20$ documents relevant, $r_t = 15$ containing the term. Relevant-side odds $15.5/5.5 = 2.82$; non-relevant side $1985.5$ occurrences against $97995.5$ non-occurrences, inverted $97995.5/1985.5 = 49.4$. So $c_t = \ln(2.82 \times 49.4) = \ln 139.1 \approx 4.94$ — against $3.89$ with no feedback.
- **The idf connection: set $R = r_t = 0$ and watch inverse document frequency appear.** The relevant-side ratio becomes $0.5/0.5 = 1$ — equivalently, with zero evidence assume $p_t = 0.5$, a maximum-ignorance prior that cancels — leaving $c_t = \log\frac{N - n_t + 0.5}{n_t + 0.5}$. A second route agrees: with no judgments the relevant set is a negligible sliver, so the non-relevant distribution is essentially the collection distribution, $u_t \approx n_t/N$, and with $p_t = 0.5$ the weight is $\log\frac{N-n_t}{n_t} \approx \log\frac{N}{n_t}$ for rare terms. Spärck Jones proposed $\log(N/n_t)$ in 1972 as pure empiricism. The RSJ derivation shows it is the zero-knowledge limit of a principled relevance weight, and the smoothed form is verbatim the idf inside BM25.
- **The numbers, and the sting in the tail.** With $N = 100{,}000$ and natural logs: $n_t = 1{,}000$ gives $\ln(99000.5/1000.5) \approx 4.59$; $n_t = 10$ gives $\approx 9.16$ against the classic $\ln 10000 \approx 9.21$, so the two variants agree closely where it matters. But a stopword-like $n_t = 90{,}000$ gives $\ln(10000.5/90000.5) \approx -2.20$. The weight goes **negative** for any term in more than half the documents, because under the model a near-ubiquitous term is mild evidence *against* relevance — coherent, but awkward: a document is penalised for containing a common query word. Practical systems floor or reformulate it.
- **Where the full weight, not just its idf limit, earns its keep.** Anywhere real relevance signal exists — and modern systems have one lying around: clicks. Treating clicked results as judged-relevant lets you estimate $r_t$ and $R$ per query, which sharpened `gke` from 3.89 to 4.94 above. The caveats are real: clicks are biased toward whatever already ranked high, and with $R$ in single digits the $0.5$ corrections dominate a very noisy $\hat{p}_t$.

**Rules to remember**

- Rank by $P(R=1 \mid d, q)$; the descending sort is optimal at every cutoff at once, by linearity of expectation.
- Ranking survives any strictly monotone transform; thresholding does not. Calibration is a separate requirement.
- Odds first, then logs — that pair is what turns Bayes into a sum over terms.
- Drop any factor identical for every document. The derivation uses that move three times.
- BIM discards term frequency and document length by construction — precisely the two BM25 repairs.
- $p_t$ is occurrence in the relevant class, $u_t$ in the non-relevant class; RSJ is the difference of their log-odds.
- The $+0.5$ in every cell stops a zero cell producing an infinite weight. It is not cosmetic.
- $R = r_t = 0$ turns RSJ into $\log\frac{N - n_t + 0.5}{n_t + 0.5}$: idf is the no-knowledge case, not an independent idea.

**Common pitfalls**

- Treating PRP as an empirical finding rather than a theorem conditional on the probabilities being correct.
- Forgetting PRP scores each document alone, so it has no concept of redundancy — then being surprised a diversity method must violate it.
- Thresholding a score only ever built to rank. Dropping constants and taking logs was legal because ordering was all that mattered.
- Believing BIM is useless because its independence assumption is false. It buys a tractable factorisation, and the resulting shape survives.
- Reading "binary" as "one occurrence is enough evidence". It means occurrence versus non-occurrence is all that is recorded.
- Losing the multiply-and-divide step and concluding the absent-term product cannot be removed.
- Treating idf as an assumption of BIM rather than the limit of the RSJ weight with no judgments.
- Assuming every weight is positive and shrinks smoothly with frequency; past $n_t > N/2$ it flips sign.
- Taking clicks as unbiased relevance judgments.

**How to approach the questions**

1. When asked *why* an ordering is optimal, reach for linearity of expectation and "one sort serves every cutoff at once".
2. If a question separates ranking from filtering, thresholding or cost, the answer involves calibration.
3. In derivation questions, name which assumption licenses which step: independence factors the product, the non-query assumption kills those factors, rank-equivalence drops the constants.
4. For any "which factor can be dropped" question, ask: is it the same for every document?
5. Compute RSJ from the smoothed formula rather than recalling a value; keep the four cells straight and which ratio is inverted.
6. When a question sets $R = 0$ or says "no relevance information", expect idf.
7. Watch for distractors attributing term-frequency saturation or length normalisation to BIM. It has neither; that is next lesson.
