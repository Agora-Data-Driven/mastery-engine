1. Sorting By Probability

IR 104 gave you the rulers — precision@k, recall@k, MAP — for scoring a ranking after the fact. The Probability Ranking Principle says which ranking to hand them: sort by P(R=1 | d, q). Reorder these four candidates and watch expected precision@k move at every cutoff at once. Like packing a bag heaviest-first: one order is best no matter where you stop.

The proof needs no independence between documents: expectation is linear, so E[relevant in top k] is just the sum of the k probabilities you placed there, however those relevance events are correlated. What PRP does assume is that each document's usefulness is independent of what else is on the page — which is why ten near-duplicates all score high, and why Maximal Marginal Relevance and dedup-before-RAG break PRP on purpose.

2. Rank Or Threshold

Ranking needs only the right order. Deciding which chunks are worth context-window budget needs the right number. Move the two costs, then flip the score to log-odds and watch the order survive while the cutoff stops meaning anything. Like a thermometer relabelled in Fahrenheit: the hot days are still the hot days, but "above 30" is now nonsense.

This is the whole licence the rest of the lesson runs on. Odds, logs and dropping a document-independent factor are all legal because only the order is being defended. The moment you threshold, filter, or spend a budget on a score, you have quietly assumed calibration that the derivation never promised.

3. Deriving The RSV

Six moves take an intractable posterior to a sum over the query terms a document contains. Tap each one: the expression on top is what you are ranking by, the note below says which assumption bought the step. Like cancelling a fraction — the thing you are left with looks nothing like the thing you started with, and it is the same number.

Look at what the last line is: a sum, over query terms present in the document, of a per-term weight. That is exactly the computation an inverted index makes cheap — walk the postings of the query terms, accumulate per document, never touch the rest of the collection. BIM's assumptions are false in detail; this shape is the part that survived into BM25.

4. The RSJ Weight

Each log term in the RSV is the Robertson–Spärck Jones weight, and estimating it is a 2×2 counting problem. Drag the counts and watch the weight; drag the judgments to zero and watch it become idf. Like a drug trial: what matters is not how many patients improved, but how differently the treated and untreated groups behaved.

Modern systems have relevance judgments lying around: clicks. Treat clicked results as judged-relevant and you can estimate r_t and R per query. The caveats are real — clicks are biased toward whatever already ranked high, and with R in single digits the 0.5 corrections dominate a very noisy estimate. That is the machinery this course's final lesson returns to as relevance feedback.

5. Where idf Comes From

Spärck Jones proposed log(N / n_t) in 1972 as pure empiricism. The RSJ derivation says it is the no-knowledge limit of a principled weight — and the two curves agree exactly where retrieval cares. Drag the document frequency across five orders of magnitude. Like a surname: "Zhukovsky" identifies someone in a room, "Smith" barely narrows it down, and past half the room a name tells you nothing at all.

The negative tail is theoretically coherent and operationally awkward: under the model, presence of a near-ubiquitous term is mild evidence against relevance, so a document can be penalised for containing a common query word. Practical systems floor or reformulate the weight — you will meet Lucene's specific fix when tuning BM25.
