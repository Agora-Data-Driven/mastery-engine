1. From BIM To BM25

The Binary Independence Model gave you a sum of per-term weights over matched query terms, and The RSJ Term Weight and Its idf Connection gave you the weight itself. Both survive here untouched. Only the multiplier in front of each weight is new — drag the term count and the document length and watch which models notice. Like swapping a light switch for a dimmer: the wiring is the same, only the control is new.

The two blindnesses this repairs are not cosmetic. A page mentioning a term thirty times is indistinguishable to BIM from one mentioning it once, and a 50-word snippet is judged by the same yardstick as a 5,000-word manual. Everything in this lesson is those two holes, closed by exactly two constants.

2. The Saturation Curve

Robertson and Walker threw away the exact 2-Poisson expression and kept its shape: zero at tf = 0, rising, concave, bounded. Drag k1 and watch the curve travel between two models you already own — BIM at one end, raw tf at the other. Like eating pizza: the first slice is transformative, the eighth is barely food.

Why this shape and not another? Because it is the shape of the eliteness weight. A document is elite for a term if it is genuinely about that concept; tf is only noisy evidence about that hidden variable, and once you believe the document is elite, further repetitions cannot make it more elite. The asymptote is not a hack to cap spam — it is what "no more evidence available" looks like when you write it down.

3. The Length Dial

Before you can normalise length you have to answer a question about your corpus: is a long document long because it is wordy, or because it covers more? b is where you write that answer down. Drag it, and drag the document. Like grading an essay per page instead of per word — first you must decide whether long means padded or thorough.

Two operational warnings. avgdl is a collection-wide statistic: it drifts as the corpus grows, and re-chunking a RAG corpus from 1,000-token to 200-token chunks changes every document's B even though b never moved. And the failure modes are symmetric — too high on API references and legal codes buries the comprehensive answers; too low on forum threads and transcripts lets bloat win on word count alone.

4. Doc A Versus Doc B

Both repairs, assembled, on one query: vector index against a tight 300-word note and a sprawling 1,500-word page that contains 2.5× the raw counts. Tap the formula to read its anatomy, then move the two constants and watch the winner change. Like a race where the handicap is written in the rulebook, not by the runners.

Know what this formula does not contain: no proximity (adjacent or ten paragraphs apart scores the same), no semantics (k8s contributes nothing to a kubernetes query), and nothing per query term beyond idf. Those gaps — not scoring quality on the terms it does match — are why BM25 is paired with dense retrieval in a hybrid stack rather than replaced by it.

5. Tuning By Symptom

Grid search finds the pair; the symptom tells you which direction it will move and whether the problem is a constant at all. Tap what your result list is doing wrong, then pick the collection you are actually indexing. Like a doctor reading symptoms instead of handing everyone the same pill.

Two things the grid will not tell you. Overfitting is easy: a judgment pool built from last quarter's head queries will happily choose a pair that regresses the long tail, so keep the query sample representative and re-validate after any change to chunking, fields or analyzers — each silently changes the length and frequency statistics the constants were tuned against. And BM25 scores are not comparable across collections at all, since N, n_t and avgdl are collection statistics. That bites hardest in hybrid retrieval, which is exactly why reciprocal rank fusion — which throws the scores away and keeps only the ranks — is the robust default for combining a sparse and a dense list.
