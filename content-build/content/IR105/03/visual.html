1. Sum Versus Product

The Assembled BM25 Formula added per-term weights together. Query likelihood multiplies per-term probabilities instead — and that single structural difference is the whole reason smoothing exists. Drag the count of timeout in a 100-token document down to zero and watch the two sides part company. Like a team score: add up the players and one zero barely shows; multiply them and one zero wipes out the team.

Both models reward the term and both divide by document length — The Role of b did it with a tuned dial, tf/|d| does it by construction. The difference is not accuracy, it is what happens at zero: a sum degrades, a product collapses. Everything in this lesson after this panel is about repairing that collapse without throwing away the probabilistic model that caused it.

2. Ranking By Likelihood

Rank by P(q|d): the probability that each document's own word distribution would emit the query. Move the counts and the lengths and watch raw totals lose to proportions — then swap the query's word order and watch nothing at all happen. Like judging two bakeries by the share of their output that is sourdough, not by how many loaves they baked.

A high P(q|d) is not the probability that the document is relevant — it is a compatibility signal between the query and an estimated distribution. A fluent but irrelevant page can share terms, and a genuinely relevant page written entirely in synonyms can have almost no lexical likelihood. Relevance judgments still decide the smoother, the fields and the extra features.

3. One Missing Term

Three documents, the query database timeout, and one dial: how much of the collection each document borrows. At 0 you are unsmoothed and two very different documents tie at zero; at 1 every document is the collection and they all tie again. Like judging a chef on one tasting: unseen is not the same as impossible, but assume they can cook everything and the tasting told you nothing.

The collection model is P(t|C) = cf(t)/|C|: timeout occurring 10,000 times in 100 million tokens gives 0.0001. Note what smoothing quietly imports — a term that is common in the collection has a large background probability, so seeing it is unsurprising, while a rare term's occurrence produces a much stronger likelihood ratio. That is rarity weighting, arrived at from a completely different derivation than the RSJ weight.

4. Lambda Versus Mu

Two smoothers, both mixing the same two models, with parameters that are not on the same scale. Drag each and compare the smoothed probability of a term that is present against one that is absent. Like diluting a drink: lambda is "one part in five, always"; mu is "add 1,000 ml of water" — which means something completely different to a shot than to a jug.

Increasing lambda and increasing mu both mean more background influence, so the direction is shared and the scale is not: lambda is a fraction in [0, 1], mu is counted in tokens. A lambda of 0.2 is ordinary; a mu of 0.2 is nothing at all. Neither family wins by theorem — tokenization, fields, passages and the definition of your labels all move the observed optimum.

5. How Much To Borrow

The one structural difference, drawn: Jelinek-Mercer borrows the same fraction from the collection whatever the document is, while Dirichlet borrows mu/(|d|+mu) — heavily from a ten-token stub, barely from a long article. Like a witness statement: you lean on background assumptions when someone saw two seconds of an event, and much less when they watched for an hour.

The practical decision is a modelling judgment, not a lookup: Jelinek-Mercer says every document borrows the same fraction; Dirichlet says shorter evidence should borrow more. Pick the assumption that matches your corpus, then verify with relevance evaluation and error analysis. Tune on held-out queries against the target metric, plot a broad grid rather than one point, and prefer a flat region to a narrow optimum that will drift as the collection changes.
