**The big idea**: **Ranking by Probability of Relevance**, **The Binary Independence Model** and **The RSJ Term Weight and Its idf Connection** built you a scoring function; **Term Frequency Saturation: The Role of k1**, **Length Normalization: The Role of b**, **The Assembled BM25 Formula** and **Tuning k1 and b in Practice** made it empirically strong; **Query Likelihood: Documents as Language Models**, **Why Smoothing Is Mandatory: The Zero-Frequency Problem** and **Jelinek-Mercer vs Dirichlet Smoothing** rebuilt the same target generatively. Every one of those improved how a *document* is represented or scored while treating the query as given and final. Relevance feedback changes the other side of the match. It turns retrieval from a one-shot query into an iterative process: evidence about which results were useful or not useful changes the *query representation*, so the next ranking can express the information need more fully. The same core idea appears in classic vector algorithms, probabilistic language models, automatic expansion and modern learned retrieval pipelines — which is why this is simultaneously the last classical lesson of the course and the direct ancestor of what neural systems do today.

**Key concepts**

- **Rocchio Relevance Feedback in Vector Space moves the query toward the relevant centroid and away from the nonrelevant one.** Given an original query vector `q0`, judged relevant document vectors `D_r` and judged nonrelevant vectors `D_nr`, the updated query is `q_m = alpha*q0 + beta*(1/|D_r|)*sum(d in D_r) d - gamma*(1/|D_nr|)*sum(d in D_nr) d`. A centroid is simply the coordinate-wise mean vector, so the mechanism is pure geometry: alpha preserves the original wording, beta pulls toward the relevant cluster, gamma pushes away from known distractors. Without this, the only way a missing but useful term can enter the query is for the user to think of it unaided.
- **Work the numbers.** A two-term query is `q0 = [1,0]`. Two relevant documents are `[1,1]` and `[0,2]`, so the relevant centroid is `[0.5,1.5]`. One nonrelevant document is `[0,1]`. With `alpha = 1`, `beta = 0.8`, `gamma = 0.2`: `[1,0] + 0.8*[0.5,1.5] - 0.2*[0,1] = [1,0] + [0.4,1.2] - [0,0.2] = [1.4,1.0]`. The second term was **absent from the original query** and now carries weight 1.0, because it characterises the relevant examples. That single number is the whole point of the method.
- **The three parameters express trust, and they are deliberately asymmetric.** A high alpha anchors the update to the user's own wording and limits drift. A high beta lets relevant examples introduce vocabulary strongly. Gamma is usually the smallest, because nonrelevance is *diverse*: documents can fail for many unrelated reasons, so their centroid may not describe one coherent direction at all. When explicit nonrelevant judgments are scarce, the negative term is often dropped entirely.
- **Representation and post-processing are part of the algorithm.** Document vectors normally use tf-idf or another retrieval-aware weighting, or common terms dominate the centroid and the update learns nothing. After the update, negative coordinates may be clipped to zero when the system expects nonnegative query weights, and the ranker may keep only the highest-weight expansion terms to control query cost and interpretability.
- **Rocchio assumes one coherent relevant region.** If a query has multiple valid intents, the centroid can land *between* clusters and emphasise terms belonging to neither useful interpretation. Mixing animal and automobile feedback for `jaguar` produces an unstable expansion. Intent selection, clustering or separate reformulations beat one averaged vector there.
- **Feedback quality matters more than algebraic elegance.** One mislabeled relevant document introduces off-topic terms; near-duplicate relevant documents overweight a single source; click-derived judgments carry position and presentation bias, making them implicit signals rather than clean labels. Sample diverse positives and deduplicate them. **With only one positive example, shrink the update toward `q0`** — a centroid estimated from a single point has high variance, and more independent examples are what justify a larger move.
- **Transparency is a control surface.** Show retained, added and suppressed terms with their supporting documents; let a user drop an expansion term or undo a judgment and recompute deterministically. Repeated rounds are allowed, but each must preserve provenance and an original-query anchor, or small early mistakes compound.
- **Pseudo-Relevance Feedback and RM3 remove the human from the loop — and pay for it.** Pseudo-relevance feedback (PRF) simply *assumes* the top documents of an initial retrieval are relevant. The pipeline is: run the original query, take the top m feedback documents, extract high-value terms, build an expanded query, retrieve again. This removes interaction cost and can improve recall, but it converts ranking errors directly into expansion evidence.
- **RM3 interpolates a relevance language model with the original query model.** Conceptually `P(t|q_expanded) = lambda*P(t|q_original) + (1-lambda)*P(t|R)`, where `P(t|R)` estimates how characteristic term t is of the pseudo-relevant set, often weighting each feedback document by how well it explains the original query. Numbers: the original query model gives 0.5 each to `database` and `timeout`; the feedback model gives 0.35 to `connection`, 0.25 to `pool`, 0.20 to `database` and 0.20 to `timeout`. With original-query weight 0.6, `database` becomes $0.6(0.5) + 0.4(0.20) = 0.38$, `timeout` likewise 0.38, `connection` $0.4(0.35) = 0.14$ and `pool` $0.4(0.25) = 0.10$. The original intent stays dominant while related vocabulary broadens recall — that is exactly the interpolation shape you already met in Jelinek-Mercer smoothing, applied to the query instead of the document.
- **Naming conventions vary, so read the configuration.** Some implementations define the interpolation parameter as the *feedback* weight rather than the original-query weight. A value of 0.6 therefore means opposite things in two systems; documentation must say which side lambda weights.
- **RM3 must be collection-aware.** Simply selecting the most frequent feedback words lets terms that occur everywhere win. Background correction, stop-word handling, field choice and document length all shape the feedback model: titles yield focused terms, full bodies give coverage plus noise.
- **Query drift is the main failure mode.** For an ambiguous query the initial top results may favour the wrong intent and then reinforce it. For a rare exact identifier the top documents can share generic boilerplate that dilutes the identifier itself. Practical controls: anchor strongly to original terms, limit the expansion count, require discriminative terms, and disable PRF for selected query classes.
- **Depth and term count are non-monotonic.** Too few feedback documents make the estimate fragile and duplicate-driven; too many pull in weaker or mixed-intent results. More expansion terms first improve vocabulary coverage, then add noise and lengthen posting traversal. Tune the two jointly on held-out queries.
- **Evaluate the whole two-pass system, latency included.** Slice by initial retrieval quality: PRF helps when early results are good enough to supply vocabulary and harms when the first pass is wrong. An **oracle** run using truly relevant feedback documents estimates the ceiling; the gap between oracle and pseudo-feedback is the measured cost of selection errors. Caching the second pass is fine only if the cache key includes the first-pass index version and every feedback parameter, or stale expansions silently preserve obsolete vocabulary.
- **Relevance Feedback as the Ancestor of Modern Query Expansion: same move, different generator.** Rocchio adds directions supported by relevant vectors; RM3 adds terms supported by pseudo-relevant language models; learned systems add tokens, sparse weights, dense vectors, synthetic passages or structured constraints supported by a model. All of them bridge the mismatch between short user wording and document vocabulary. Sparse learned expansion assigns weight to terms not literally present — a query for `heart attack` activating `myocardial infarction` — while keeping inverted-index matching and term-level explanations. Dense encoders expand implicitly by mapping related language nearby; generative rewriting produces alternate queries or a hypothetical answer passage whose vocabulary guides retrieval.
- **Local versus global expansion is a standing choice.** Local methods (Rocchio, PRF) use results for the *current* query and adapt to context, but inherit first-pass mistakes. Global methods (thesauri, synonym graphs, click logs, trained models) are available before retrieval but can add contextually wrong meanings.
- **The inherited lessons are anchoring, diversity, cost and access.** An unconstrained generated rewrite can drop an exact identifier, negate a condition, or commit to one ambiguous intent — so systems retrieve with both original and expanded representations and fuse. Five paraphrases of one mistaken interpretation create false confidence, not coverage: deduplicate and cap any single channel. Expansion also costs postings traversal, vector-search calls and model latency, and expansion drawn from inaccessible documents leaks vocabulary across permissions — apply access control *before* feedback extraction.

**Rules to remember**

- `q_m = alpha*q0 + beta*centroid(D_r) - gamma*centroid(D_nr)`; a centroid is the coordinate-wise mean.
- Gamma is normally smallest, because nonrelevance has no single direction.
- One positive example ⇒ shrink toward the original query. Variance, not stubbornness.
- PRF = "assume the top m are relevant". Its evidence is only as good as the first pass.
- `P(t|q_expanded) = lambda*P(t|q_original) + (1-lambda)*P(t|R)` — and check which side lambda names.
- Always keep an anchor to the original query, classically and neurally.
- Frequency alone is not evidence; a feedback model needs background correction.
- Feedback depth and expansion term count are non-monotonic and must be tuned together.
- Expansion changes recall, latency and exposure at once. Evaluate all three.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Treating Rocchio's gamma like beta.** Symmetric weights over-trust an incoherent nonrelevant centroid.
- **Running Rocchio on raw term counts.** Without tf-idf-style weighting, common words dominate the centroid and the "learned" expansion is noise.
- **Averaging across intents.** For an ambiguous query the centroid lands between clusters and helps neither.
- **Trusting duplicates.** Three near-identical relevant documents are one piece of evidence, not three.
- **Confusing explicit feedback with pseudo-relevance feedback.** One has labels; the other has assumptions.
- **Reading lambda backwards.** The same 0.6 can mean "mostly original" or "mostly feedback" depending on the implementation.
- **Judging PRF on top-k relevance alone.** It is a two-pass system; latency and drift rate are part of the result.
- **Expecting one global expansion setting.** If it helps broad informational queries and harms product codes, route it conditionally.
- **Assuming a neural rewrite escapes these failure modes.** Anchoring, evidence quality, ambiguity, drift, cost and evaluation still decide the outcome.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For any Rocchio arithmetic, compute the two centroids first, then scale by beta and gamma, then add `alpha*q0`. Most distractors come from forgetting the `1/|D_r|` average or from applying gamma with the wrong sign.
2. When a question changes a single parameter, say what *direction* it moves the query, not just that it "changes weights": alpha holds position, beta pulls in, gamma pushes off.
3. For interpolation questions, check that the reported probabilities sum sensibly and remember that the original terms should still dominate at a typical anchor weight.
4. If a scenario reports that results got *worse* after expansion, look at the first pass and the query class before blaming the formula — drift starts upstream.
5. Distinguish local from global expansion explicitly; distractors swap "uses this query's results" with "uses collection-wide resources".
6. Watch absolute claims. "PRF improves recall" is conditional on the first pass; "the rewrite understands the query" is not an evaluation result.
