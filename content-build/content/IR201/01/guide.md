**The big idea**: every ranker you have built so far scores a document by the terms it *literally contains*. **The Inverted Index: Dictionary and Postings** gave one postings list per vocabulary term. **Inverse Document Frequency: Rare Terms Carry Signal** and **The Assembled BM25 Formula** decided how much credit a matched term earns, with **Term Frequency Saturation: The Role of k1** keeping that credit honest. **Cosine Similarity as a Ranking Function** compared texts in a space with one axis per term. **Relevance Feedback as the Ancestor of Modern Query Expansion** was the first attempt to reach past the words the user typed. All of it is exact on term *identity*, and that is the gap this lesson attacks: a document about `automobile repair` and a query about `car mechanic` share no active coordinate at all, so the strongest lexical machinery in the world scores their overlap at zero. Dense retrieval places texts in a learned vector space where semantic relatedness can survive different wording. Two routes get there — compress the count matrix with SVD, or learn the coordinates outright — and understanding the transition makes dense retrieval an engineering tool rather than a mysterious model call.

**Key concepts**

- **Vocabulary mismatch is a coordinate problem.** A sparse representation assigns one coordinate per vocabulary term, so two texts that mean the same thing in different words activate disjoint coordinates and their dot product is literally zero. BM25 rewards exact overlap, term rarity and document-length normalization; without expansion it cannot give direct lexical credit to a synonym that never appears.
- **Worked numbers.** Three documents: D1 `automobile mechanic repairs engines`, D2 `car insurance claim form`, D3 `bicycle mechanic replaces chains`. For query `car mechanic`, exact term overlap sees D1 matching only `mechanic`, D2 matching only `car`, and D3 matching only `mechanic`. Depending on inverse document frequency and length, **D2 or D3 can outrank D1** — the one document that satisfies the combined intent. The system has independent evidence for two query terms and no learned knowledge that `car` and `automobile` are related.
- **Mismatch is much broader than synonyms.** An abbreviation differs from its expansion (`ACL` versus `access control list`). A concept is expressed at different specificity (`database outage` versus `primary replica unavailable`). A user describes a symptom while the document names the cause. Analyzers and morphological normalization fix *surface* variation such as `indexes` versus `index` and stop there — they do not map arbitrary descriptions onto one concept.
- **Query expansion is the classical bridge, and it has a cost curve.** A synonym rule rewrites the query to `(car OR automobile) AND mechanic`: transparent and precise in a curated domain, but maintaining every paraphrase is expensive, and broad synonym sets destroy intent — treating `java` as both a language and a beverage expands ambiguity instead of resolving it. Token identity is not semantic identity, however many rules you write.
- **LSA compresses the term-document matrix instead of expanding the query.** Let $A$ have $m$ terms and $n$ documents, entries being counts or a weight such as TF-IDF. Singular value decomposition factors it as $A = U \Sigma V^{\top}$: columns of $U$ are orthonormal directions in term space, columns of $V$ directions in document space, and the singular values order how much structure each direction carries. Equivalently $A = \sum_i \sigma_i u_i v_i^{\top}$, so keeping the $k$ largest terms of that sum gives the rank-$k$ approximation $A_k = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^{\top}$ — the same low-rank idea as principal component methods, applied to co-occurrence.
- **Worked numbers.** Vocabulary `[car, automobile, engine, policy]` with D1 `car engine` = [1,0,1,0], D2 `automobile engine` = [0,1,1,0], D3 `car policy` = [1,0,0,1]. The singular values are 1.848, 1.414, 0.765, so the energies $\sigma_i^2$ are 3.414, 2.000, 0.586 and sum to 6 — exactly the number of ones in the matrix. Rank 2 therefore retains about **90%** of the structure. And it manufactures soft matching: in $A_2$ the `car` row carries weight **0.104** in D2, a document that never contained the word `car`. Cosine similarity between D1 and D2 rises from **0.500** raw to **0.679** at rank 2, and the term pair `car`/`automobile`, which never co-occur, rises from exactly **0** to **0.086** — similar coordinates for terms that never appear together.
- **A query must be folded into the same space.** One common convention places documents in $\Sigma_k V_k^{\top}$, with the compatible query coordinate $q^{\top} U_k$ under the same term weighting. The library convention matters far less than the rule: never compare vectors produced under different weighting or scaling conventions.
- **Choosing $k$ is a bias-variance tradeoff, and both ends are visible.** At $k = 1$ every document cosine in that toy corpus is exactly **1.000** — `automobile engine` and `car policy` become indistinguishable, senses merged into one broad theme. At $k = 3$ you are back to the original sparse matrix and its noise: `car`/`automobile` returns to a cosine of exactly **0**. Rank 2 is where denoising and generalization live.
- **LSA's limits are structural, not tuning problems.** The decomposition is *global*: adding documents shifts the latent directions, so coordinates are not stable independent encodings, and refreshing classical SVD over a large changing vocabulary is expensive. Each term gets one vector, so `bank` blends its financial and river senses — LSA cannot condition a word on its sentence. And it learns only from the indexed corpus, so weak co-occurrence evidence yields weak semantics.
- **Learned embeddings: meaning as geometry.** A learned text encoder maps a variable-length token sequence to a fixed-dimensional vector, with parameters adjusted during training so texts that should match receive compatible vectors and mismatched texts are separated. Individual coordinates carry no stable human label; meaning lives in the pattern of direction and magnitude across dimensions.
- **Attention is exactly what LSA lacked.** Contextual layers let each token representation depend on its neighbours, so `bank` in `river bank erosion` differs internally from `bank approved the loan` — the polysemy limit, removed. A pooling rule or designated output then combines contextual token states into one text vector, and the same encoder embeds new text without rebuilding any corpus-wide factorization.
- **Worked numbers.** A two-dimensional teaching model gives q(`car mechanic`) = [0.90, 0.30], d1(`automobile repair shop`) = [0.84, 0.36], d2(`car insurance policy`) = [0.55, −0.30], d3(`bicycle repair shop`) = [0.62, 0.45]. Dot products: $0.90 \times 0.84 + 0.30 \times 0.36 = 0.864$ for d1, $0.405$ for d2, $0.693$ for d3. Geometry lifts the *paraphrase* above the exact-word distractor, while d3 stays plausibly similar because it shares repair intent — semantic similarity is **graded**, not logically exact, and a metadata filter or a later scoring stage may be needed to enforce vehicle type.
- **The fix creates new failure modes.** A semantic encoder can collapse important distinctions and retrieve `bicycle mechanic` because both texts concern repair work. It can underweight exact identifiers, product codes, negation, dates and rare names. Sparse retrieval is often superior for `ERR_CONNECTION_RESET`, invoice `A19-884`, or an exact legal phrase. Hybrid retrieval preserves exact-match strength while adding semantic candidates.
- **Serving shape.** Precompute document embeddings, store them with IDs and metadata; at request time encode the query, find high-similarity vectors, apply allowed filters, return candidates. Encoding and indexing are separate concerns, and changing the model changes the coordinates — query and document vectors must come from compatible model versions.
- **Embedding quality is task-dependent.** A general model may place topically related texts together while a retrieval task needs *answer* relevance: `How do I cancel a subscription?` should retrieve the cancellation procedure, not a document about subscription analytics. Training objectives, formatting, language coverage and input truncation all shape the geometry. Dimension count alone measures nothing.
- **Chunking decides what a document vector means.** One vector for a 100-page manual blends many subjects and hides the relevant paragraph; very small chunks preserve focus but lose context and inflate the index. Chunk at semantic boundaries, carry titles or section paths into the encoded text, and choose the size with end-to-end retrieval labels.
- **Similarity is not authorization.** Embeddings can encode undesirable correlations from training data and leak information through overly broad retrieval. Apply access filters before results reach the user, keep current ACL metadata in the indexing pipeline, and make deletion of a source remove or invalidate its chunks and vectors.

**Rules to remember**

- Token identity is not semantic identity. Analyzers fix surface variation; they never fix concept-level mismatch.
- Expansion is precise and curated; embeddings are broad and learned. Both answer the same problem.
- SVD truncation is what creates soft matching: the rank-$k$ reconstruction puts non-zero weight where the raw matrix had a zero.
- Small $k$ over-merges senses, large $k$ restores noise. There is no universally correct value.
- LSA is global and per-term; encoders are incremental and contextual. That is the whole difference.
- Never mix vectors from different weightings, scalings or model versions in one similarity computation.
- Dense similarity is graded. A high score means "related", never "satisfies the constraint".
- Sparse still wins on identifiers, codes, negation and exact phrases. Diagnose the query distribution first.
- Filters run before results are returned; similarity confers no permission.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Assuming dense retrieval subsumes lexical retrieval.** It contributes candidates lexical matching misses and *loses* ones lexical matching would have caught exactly. The answer is usually hybrid, not replacement.
- **Blaming vocabulary mismatch for every miss.** If failures are mostly bad filters, stale content, or documents never indexed, embeddings solve nothing. Segment queries by exact identifiers, descriptions, abbreviations and paraphrases, then compare recall at a candidate cutoff.
- **Expanding synonyms indiscriminately.** `java` as language and beverage widens the candidate set and destroys intent — precision falls without recall rising where it matters.
- **Treating LSA coordinates as stable encodings.** Add a large batch of documents and the decomposition moves — exactly the property an encoder does not have.
- **Expecting one vector per term to handle polysemy.** `bank` has one row in the matrix. No choice of $k$ separates the river from the loan.
- **Proving the geometry by eyeballing a 2-D projection.** A few plotted points build intuition and cannot demonstrate neighbourhood quality in hundreds of dimensions. Measure recall at k, ranking metrics, latency and per-segment performance instead.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For any "why did this document not rank" question, ask first whether the query and the document share *terms*. If they do not, the sparse score is zero by construction and no weighting scheme rescues it.
2. When a question offers expansion and embeddings as rivals, check the scenario's scale and stability. A small curated domain favours rules; open paraphrase favours learned geometry.
3. For SVD questions, track what truncation *does*: it fills zeros in, raises the similarity of terms that share contexts, and destroys distinctions when $k$ is too small. Watch for the degenerate ends — cosine 1.000 at $k = 1$, the original matrix at full rank.
4. Distinguish "global refactorization" from "encode one new document". That single property separates LSA from a learned encoder in most distractors.
5. Read dense-retrieval scores as graded, never as boolean. A distractor that says a high cosine "guarantees" the constraint is satisfied is wrong; filters and reranking exist for that.
6. When a scenario names an identifier, a product code, a date or an exact phrase, expect the lexical answer to be the strong one, and hybrid to be the safest option offered.
