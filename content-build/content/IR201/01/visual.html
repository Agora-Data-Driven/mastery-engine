1. Words Versus Meaning

Everything you built in BM25 and the inverted index scores a document by the terms it literally contains, weighted by idf. Here is what that costs. Query: car mechanic. Drag how rare car is, and switch the mechanism. Like matching people by the exact words on their business card, rather than by what they do.

Sparse retrieval is not being done badly here — it is being done perfectly. A coordinate exists for car and a different one for automobile, so their dot product is exactly zero whatever weighting you choose. Dense retrieval does not weight the terms better; it stops using terms as coordinates at all.

2. Kinds Of Mismatch

"Vocabulary mismatch" is six different problems wearing one name, and the right fix is different for each — including the ones where an embedding is the wrong answer. Tap a pair. Like a lost-property desk: some things are found by the label, some by the description, and some only by the serial number.

This is why experts diagnose the query distribution before replacing anything. Segment real queries into exact identifiers, natural-language descriptions, abbreviations and paraphrases, then compare recall at a candidate cutoff per segment. If relevant documents keep failing to enter the sparse candidate set for descriptive queries, semantic retrieval has a clear job. If the failures are bad filters, stale content or missing documents, embeddings solve none of it.

3. Truncating The Matrix

LSA takes the term-document matrix and throws singular values away. Watch what truncation actually does to the numbers: it fills zeros in. Vocabulary [car, automobile, engine, policy], three documents, drag the rank. Like squinting at a mosaic until the picture appears and the individual tiles stop mattering.

A query lives in the original weighted term space and must be folded into the same latent space with the same weighting and the same decomposition — one common convention places documents in Sigma_k V_k transpose and the compatible query coordinate at q transpose U_k. The library's exact convention matters far less than the rule: never compare vectors produced under different weighting or scaling. And the decomposition is global — add a large batch of documents and every coordinate on this screen moves, which is why LSA coordinates are not stable independent encodings.

4. Rotate The Query

The lesson's two-dimensional teaching model, live. Three documents sit fixed in the space; swing the query vector and watch the ranking change — because a dot product is just a projection. Like pointing a torch in a dark room: what lights up depends entirely on which way you aim.

Nothing here is a metaphor: this is what a bi-encoder does, at 768 dimensions instead of two. Which is also the warning — a few plotted points build intuition and cannot demonstrate neighbourhood quality in hundreds of dimensions. Measure recall at k, ranking metrics, latency and per-segment performance on representative queries, and inspect the cases where dense and lexical disagree. That is where the information is.

5. One Vector Per Chunk

A document embedding is a single point, so chunking decides what that point actually means. Take a 100-page manual (call a page 500 words) and drag the chunk size. Like summarising a book in one sentence versus one sentence per paragraph — one hides everything, the other forgets what chapter you were in.

Two judgments the numbers never show. Similarity is not authorization: embeddings can leak through overly broad retrieval, so access filters must run before results reach the user, the indexing pipeline must carry current ACL metadata, and deleting a source must remove or invalidate its chunks and vectors. And chunk size is chosen with end-to-end retrieval labels, never by eye — the same discipline as evaluating the mismatch itself.
