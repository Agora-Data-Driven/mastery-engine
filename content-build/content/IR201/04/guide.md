**The big idea**: **Learned Embeddings: Meaning as Geometry** made relevance proximity; **Dual-Tower Architecture: Encode Separately, Compare by Dot Product** with **Similarity Metrics: Cosine vs Dot Product vs Euclidean** made it cheap to serve; and **IVF: k-Means Partitioning of the Vector Space**, **HNSW: Layered Greedy Graph Search** and **Product Quantization: Compressing Vectors** answered **Why Exact k-NN Does Not Scale** inside **The Recall-Latency-Memory Triangle**. All took the vectors as given; this lesson goes after the vectors themselves. An embedding index inherits the judgments encoded during training: which pairs count as matches, which nonmatches are confusing, which distinctions the vector must preserve. Production quality therefore needs more than a model name — the contrastive objective, adaptation without overfitting, the right evaluation, and a decision about how much precision and dimensionality the service can afford.

**Key concepts**

- **The Contrastive Training Objective is cross-entropy over candidate documents.** Training starts from positive query-document pairs, and the positive should outscore its competitors. For one positive $d^+$ and negatives $d_1 \dots d_n$ the loss is the negative log of $\exp(\text{score}(q,d^+)/\tau)$ divided by the sum of $\exp(\text{score}(q,d_j)/\tau)$ over all candidates. Worked: logits $[3,1,0]$ at temperature 1 give $e^3 = 20.09$ over a denominator $20.09 + 2.72 + 1 = 23.81$, so $p^+ = 0.844$ and the loss is $-\ln 0.844 \approx 0.17$. Swap the negative at 1 for a hard one at 2.8 and $e^{2.8} = 16.44$ lifts the denominator to 37.53: $p^+$ falls to 0.535, the loss rises to 0.625. Same positive — only the company it kept changed.
- **Temperature controls softmax sharpness, and the derivative says why.** Differentiating gives $\partial L/\partial s_j = (p_j - y_j)/\tau$, with $y_j$ one for the positive and zero otherwise. A smaller $\tau$ both magnifies score differences inside the softmax and divides the gradient by a smaller number — stronger gradients around close candidates — while overly small values destabilise optimization and overweight mislabeled negatives. A candidate already scored far below the positive has $p_j \approx 0$ and contributes almost nothing at any $\tau$. Normalized embeddings with dot products make the score cosine similarity; unnormalized training also learns through norms.
- **The objective shapes relative geometry, not absolute truth — and scores are not calibrated.** Positives teach what should be close, negatives teach which distinctions matter, the sampling distribution sets the difficulty, and duplicates, false negatives and leaked labels corrupt the signal. Evaluate on groups or time periods separated from training, or memorized near-duplicates masquerade as generalization. A dot product of 0.72 from one model is also not comparable with 0.72 from another: ranking happens *within* a query, so any absolute threshold needs separate calibration on held-out data, including queries with no relevant document.
- **In-Batch Negatives reuse the other positives in the batch — and assume they are irrelevant.** With B aligned pairs, one matrix multiplication of the B query vectors against the B document vectors gives the whole $B \times B$ score matrix: the diagonal holds the positives and each query gets roughly $B-1$ free negatives. At $B=64$ that is 63 negatives per query and 4,032 negative pairs from a single matmul. But when a batch holds duplicate intents or several valid answers, the loss pushes a genuinely useful neighbour away. Deduplicate, group known positives, or mask false-negative pairs out.
- **In-Batch Negatives vs Hard Negative Mining: random negatives go stale.** A billing query and a cooking passage already sit far apart, so after basic training their $p_j$ is near zero and their gradient negligible. Hard-negative mining retrieves documents that score highly but are judged nonrelevant: a passage about *changing a subscription plan* is a useful negative for `cancel my subscription` because it shares vocabulary and topic while failing the requested action. Mine with a lexical retriever, the current dense model, or a stronger teacher, then filter known positives — the hardest candidates are disproportionately mislabeled *relevant* passages, so hardness is not quality. Mix easy and hard, refresh as the model improves. Training loss rising while validation retrieval improves means the harder task is working.
- **Fine-Tuning an Embedder on Domain Data adapts it to your relevance relation.** Build positives from human judgments, clicked results with bias correction, accepted answers, or generated pairs verified against source content, and preserve production query style, formatting, languages and chunk boundaries — a model trained on polished synthetic questions fails on abbreviated support queries. Split by document family, customer or time when those groups share language, and baseline the base model, a lexical retriever and the production hybrid *first*: the goal is a system gain, not a lower training loss.
- **Read the segment scorecard, not the headline.** Suppose base recall@20 is 0.78 overall and 0.61 on internal acronyms; fine-tuning lifts them to 0.80 and 0.75 but drops multilingual recall from 0.74 to 0.58. Blend them: with a fraction $m$ of multilingual traffic, tuned beats base only while $0.02 > 0.18m$ — under about 11% multilingual traffic. The two-point overall gain decided nothing. Parameter-efficient adapters cut cost; full fine-tuning offers more capacity and more catastrophic-forgetting risk.
- **A retrained model is a new vector space, so it is an index migration.** Every document is re-embedded, and the training data, model, instructions, tokenizer, pooling, normalization and maximum lengths all need versioning. A safe rollout holds authorization, chunking and candidate width constant, routes a controlled share of queries to the new index behind shadow evaluation and dual writes, and logs which index served each request. Move the model and the reranker together and attribution collapses.
- **MTEB is comparative evidence, not a production requirement.** The Massive Text Embedding Benchmark evaluates embedding models across many datasets and task families — retrieval, semantic textual similarity, classification, clustering, reranking-related uses — but its aggregate mixes task families your service never runs. Choose slices matching your language, domain, input length and task; contamination, licensing, context limits and size count too. Shortlist on public evidence, then run your own held-out queries through the complete pipeline. Model A at recall@20 0.86 with 768 dimensions can be the worse buy than model B at 0.85 with 384, if B halves index memory and clears every segment floor.
- **Instruction-Tuned Embedding Models take a task or role prefix.** Strings such as `query: find a support procedure` and `passage: ...` let one model organize different tasks and asymmetric roles. Follow the model card exactly: omitting a required query instruction places queries in a geometry incompatible with the indexed documents — and nothing errors. Never invent a prefix because it sounds descriptive.
- **Matryoshka Embeddings: Truncatable Dimensions are trained to be truncatable.** Matryoshka representation learning applies the objective at several prefix lengths, nesting coarse useful structure inside the larger representation, so a 768-dimensional output can be cut to its first 384 or 256, then normalized and searched per the model's contract. Ordinary embeddings offer no such guarantee. For ten million float32 vectors, 768 dimensions is 30.72 GB raw and 256 is 10.24 GB.
- **Truncation is not free, and the floor decides.** Suppose recall@20 is 0.88 at 768, 0.872 at 384 and 0.84 at 128: against a target of 0.86, 384 is a strong candidate and 128 is out. Rebuild and benchmark the *actual* ANN index, since lower dimensions change graph behaviour and latency too. Store the chosen dimension in the index version; query and document prefixes must match.
- **Scalar and Binary Quantization: Recall per Dollar starts with bits.** Scalar quantization maps each coordinate into 8-bit integers via a scale and offset — a fourfold cut from float32. Over $[-1,1]$ with 256 levels the bin width is $2/255 = 0.00784$, so 0.503 reconstructs near 0.506; outliers widen the range and waste resolution, so calibration and clipping policy matter. Binary quantization keeps one bit per dimension, usually the sign: 768 dimensions become 96 bytes instead of 3,072, a 32-fold reduction scored by fast Hamming operations — but magnitude is gone and recall can fall much harder, so binary generates candidates that are then reranked with scalar or full-precision vectors.
- **Recall per dollar is a whole-system number.** Quantization is not truncation — truncation drops trailing dimensions from a model trained for that prefix, quantization reduces precision within the dimensions you keep, and combined their errors compound. The bill covers RAM, storage, network, replicas, encoding, index construction and reranking. If int8 cuts memory fourfold while recall@20 slips 0.88 → 0.877, it buys more replicas or a bigger corpus; if binary codes fall to 0.79 and double the reranker's work, the saving evaporates. Take the cheapest configuration meeting the quality floors, rechecking as corpus, traffic and pricing change.

**Rules to remember**

- Contrastive loss is cross-entropy over candidates: $[3,1,0]$ at $\tau=1$ gives $p^+ = 0.844$, loss 0.17.
- $\partial L/\partial s_j = (p_j - y_j)/\tau$. Small $\tau$ sharpens and amplifies — including every labelling error.
- Training fixes relative geometry only. Scores are not calibrated across models or across segments.
- In-batch negatives: B pairs, one matmul, $B-1$ negatives per query, and an assumption that can be false.
- Hard negatives share vocabulary and topic but fail the requested action. Filter known positives; refresh them.
- Judge fine-tuning on segments and traffic mix, never on the overall delta. It is a new vector space: re-embed everything, version everything, migrate deliberately.
- MTEB shortlists; your own held-out queries through the full pipeline decide. Follow its prefixes exactly.
- Matryoshka prefixes are trained; ordinary prefixes are not. 10M × 768 float32 = 30.72 GB; 256 dims = 10.24 GB.
- int8 ≈ 4× smaller at bin width $2/255$; binary = 96 bytes for 768 dims, 32×, magnitude discarded.
- Evaluate each dimension-and-precision configuration; never multiply savings on paper.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Treating a similarity score as a probability of relevance.** Thresholds need separate calibration, including on queries with no answer.
- **Letting duplicate intents sit in one batch.** The loss then teaches the model to separate two correct answers.
- **Assuming harder negatives are better negatives.** The hardest mined candidates are disproportionately unlabelled positives.
- **Declaring victory on training loss.** The comparison is against the base model, a lexical baseline and the production hybrid.
- **Reading only overall recall after fine-tuning.** A collapsed segment hides inside a two-point average.
- **Forgetting the re-embedding bill.** A swapped model over an old index is silently wrong.
- **Dropping or improvising the instruction prefix.** Queries land in a geometry the documents do not share, and nothing errors.
- **Truncating an ordinary embedding.** Without Matryoshka training the first 256 dimensions guarantee nothing.
- **Counting only vector RAM.** Replicas, encoding, index build and reranking share the invoice.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Work the softmax by hand — exponentiate, sum, divide. The arithmetic settles most distractors and shows which candidate the gradient is really pushing against.
2. Separate *what the model learned* from *what the index does*. Recall lost to bad negative sampling cannot be recovered by `nprobe` or `efSearch`.
3. When a metric moves, ask which one: training loss, ANN recall, segment recall and end-to-end relevance can move in opposite directions.
4. On a fine-tuning scenario, find the segment that got worse and the traffic share it carries. The answer usually hides there.
5. For dimension or precision questions, compute the bytes — dimensions × bytes per value × vectors — then check recall against the stated floor, not the table's biggest number.
6. Watch for absolutes. "Bigger batches are better", "binary is 32× cheaper", "the top MTEB model" are conditional, and the condition sits in one clause.
