1. From Index To Model

Last lesson's dials all lived in the index and took the vectors as given. This lesson's dials live in the model. Switch sides and tap a dial to see who owns it and what it can actually move. Like a library: last lesson tuned how fast you walk the shelves, this one decides what gets shelved next to what.

The connection is one sentence: an embedding index inherits the judgments encoded during training — which pairs count as matches, which nonmatches are confusing, which distinctions the vector must preserve. No value of nprobe or efSearch recovers a distinction the loss never taught, because the index is only ever agreeing with the embedder.

2. Softmax Over Candidates

The contrastive loss is cross-entropy over the candidate documents for one query. Drag the competitor's score and the temperature, and watch the positive's probability, the loss and the gradient move together. Like grading on a curve: what you score matters far less than who else sat the exam.

The loss does not make scores calibrated probabilities of relevance. A dot product of 0.72 from one model is not comparable with 0.72 from another, and one model's distribution shifts by query segment. Candidate generation ranks within a query, which hides this — until someone hard-codes an absolute threshold, which must be calibrated on held-out data including queries with no relevant document at all.

3. The Batch Is The Negatives

One matrix multiplication of B queries against B documents scores every pair at once: the diagonal is the positives and everything else is a free negative. Grow the batch, mine harder, or let a duplicate intent slip in. Like a speed-dating night: everyone in the room is automatically "not your match" — until two people in the room wanted the same thing.

Hardness is not automatically quality. The very hardest mined candidates are disproportionately mislabeled relevant passages, so mix easy and hard negatives, filter known positives, refresh the mined set as the model improves, and read the top examples yourself. One honest signal: training loss rising while validation retrieval improves means the harder task is doing real work.

4. Fine-Tune Or Shortlist

Two ways to get a better embedder — train your own, or pick someone else's — and one way to break either of them. Move the traffic mix, then the quality floor, then drop the instruction prefix. Like hiring: the average score on the CV never decides it, the one skill your job actually needs does.

Whichever route you take, a new or fine-tuned model is a new vector space: every document is re-embedded and the index is migrated. Version the training data, model, instructions, tokenizer, pooling, normalization and maximum lengths; ship behind shadow evaluation, dual writes and a rollback artifact. And change one thing at a time — move the model and the reranker together and attribution is gone.

5. Cheaper Vectors

Two independent ways to shrink an index — drop trailing dimensions, or spend fewer bits on the ones you keep. Pick a combination and watch the memory fall and the evidence run out. Like shrinking a photo: fewer pixels and fewer colours per pixel are different economies, and only one of them was designed in.

Recall per dollar is a whole-system number: RAM, storage, network, replicas, encoding, index construction and reranking. Measure relevance, ANN recall, latency and total infrastructure at the chosen candidate width, then take the cheapest configuration that meets the quality floors — and recheck the arithmetic as corpus size, traffic mix, hardware pricing and reliability requirements change over the system's lifetime.
