**The big idea**: IR 201 closed on **The Recall-Latency-Memory Triangle** — you tuned an ANN index until its recall@k was good enough at a latency you could pay for, with a **Bi-Encoder** (**Dual-Tower Architecture: Encode Separately, Compare by Dot Product**) supplying the vectors — and IR 104 gave you **Precision@k and Recall@k** as the language for "good enough". This lesson joins the two and reinterprets that recall number: in a production retriever it is not a quality score but a *ceiling*. A real system rarely asks one model to search the whole corpus and produce the final ordering. It runs a **funnel**: inexpensive stages protect recall over many items, while increasingly expensive stages spend computation on fewer candidates to improve precision. It succeeds only when candidate budgets, filters and latency objectives are treated as one end-to-end system, because every stage's output width is the next stage's input cost.

**Key concepts**

- **The recall stage performs candidate generation, and its only real obligation is coverage.** Given a corpus of N items it cheaply selects a set C several orders of magnitude smaller — a million chunks down to 200 — drawing on BM25, dense nearest-neighbor search, curated rules and metadata-aware retrievers. It is not asked to order well; it is asked not to lose what later stages need.
- **The precision stage orders or classifies candidates using richer evidence.** More features, joint query-document comparison, business constraints, near-duplicate collapsing, passage selection for an answer generator. What it cannot do is recover a relevant document absent from C.
- **That asymmetry is the whole architecture.** Early false negatives are usually permanent; early false positives can be removed later. So the cheap stage is tuned for recall and tolerates junk; the expensive stage is tuned for precision and trusts the set it was handed.
- **The ceiling, derived.** For binary relevance with R relevant items, if only r enter the funnel, every later cutoff k returns a subset of C, so $\text{recall}@k \le r/R$ however good the ranking model is. Worked: one million chunks, five labeled relevant chunks, a generator returning 200 chunks containing four. Candidate recall is $4/5 = 0.8$. A perfect reranker places all four in the top ten: precision@10 $= 4/10 = 0.4$, recall@10 $= 0.8$. The fifth is unreachable at any price. What *does* still improve is metrics emphasizing the top positions, since the precision stage can lift those r items upward — which is why a team reports candidate coverage and final ordering quality as two numbers. One number cannot distinguish missing evidence from bad ordering.
- **A recall claim without a cutoff is not a claim.** `Recall stage has high recall` is incomplete. `candidate recall@200 is 0.96 on adjudicated support queries` names the cutoff — recall rises as more documents are admitted — and names the population. Measure by query segment: exact identifiers, paraphrases and multilingual queries favor different retrievers.
- **Real funnels have more than two stages, and every transition is a contract.** Retrieve 1,000 lexical and dense candidates; fuse and deduplicate to 300; lightweight scorer on 100; expensive relevance model on 30; enforce display diversity and return 10. Each transition needs input width, output width, score meaning, timeout, fallback, filters and diagnostic identifiers, or a stage silently truncates candidates or reinterprets scores.
- **Scores should not cross a boundary untransformed.** A BM25 score, a vector similarity and a classifier probability arise from different scales. Merge on ranks or a calibrated fusion rule, and hand the next stage stable IDs plus features it explicitly understands. Preserve each source's original rank and score — otherwise, when a document falls, you cannot tell whether it entered low everywhere or was damaged in fusion.
- **Authorization belongs before exposure and ideally before expensive scoring.** A vector engine that searches globally and filters afterward returns too few eligible candidates and wastes compute; a reranker fed unauthorized text has crossed a security boundary even if the UI hides the result. Apply tenant and ACL constraints inside candidate generation, and recheck at the serving boundary.
- **Deduplication is a stage decision with metric consequences.** Chunking yields near-identical passages from one source; admitting all of them crowds other evidence out of a fixed candidate set and inflates offline metrics when duplicates share labels. Deduplicate by stable source or content signature at a defined point.
- **Fallbacks are modes, not accidents.** If the dense service times out, a lexical candidate set still serves a degraded response; if the reranker fails, the fused order is returned. A fallback is separately evaluated, logs which path served, and holds authorization.
- **Stage-wise attribution decides where extra computation belongs.** For every relevant document missing from the final top k, ask: did no source find it, did fusion discard it, did deduplication collapse it, did a filter remove it, or did the precision stage misorder it? Tuning a reranker cannot repair candidate recall; widening retrieval cannot alone fix a wrong final scorer.
- **A candidate budget is the number of items passed between stages, and it converts a quality goal into resource demand.** If a reranker scores each query-document pair independently, compute grows roughly linearly with depth: 50 to 100 roughly doubles pair scoring, token processing and intermediate data, though batching improves hardware utilization.
- **Budgeting starts from the final objective and works backward.** Require a relevant answer in the final top ten for 95 percent of answerable queries, then measure candidate success@k — the fraction of queries with at least one relevant candidate. Measured: depth 20 → 0.90 at 22 ms p95; 50 → 0.95 at 47 ms; 100 → 0.968 at 88 ms; 200 → 0.973 at 169 ms. With a 60 ms allocation and a 0.95 floor, depth 50 is the natural starting point; depth 100 has quality headroom but violates latency. The marginal arithmetic is the real lesson: 20→50 buys +0.05 over 30 candidates (~0.0017 each), 50→100 +0.018 over 50 (~0.00036), 100→200 +0.005 over 100 (~0.00005). A flat curve is wasted compute.
- **Per-source budgets do not add up.** Allocating 100 results each to BM25 and dense search does not guarantee 200 unique candidates: overlap on 60 makes the union 140. The overlap is itself informative — high agreement suggests strong obvious candidates, while each source's unique tail supplies complementary recall.
- **Fixed budgets are predictable; adaptive budgets trade that for average cost.** A cautious adaptive policy routes on *observable query classes*, not an opaque confidence threshold: validated identifier syntax depth 20, ordinary text depth 50, multilingual or low-agreement queries depth 100. Evaluate each route separately, enforce a global maximum, and fall back to the wider route when classification is uncertain — a wrong cheap-route decision is an unrecoverable early false negative.
- **Budget filters before committing capacity.** Retrieve 50 vectors, discover only five satisfy an ACL filter, and you did not run the experiment you thought: reranking 5 survivors is not reranking 50 eligible candidates. Prefer filter-aware retrieval. With post-filtering only, over-fetch on measured selectivity — at eligibility rate p the expected eligible count from n candidates is $n p$, so ~500 fetched for 50 eligible at $p = 0.1$ — but tenant skew and vector neighborhoods break independence, so cap the work and use observed percentiles.
- **Token length is a second budget.** A reranker with maximum sequence length L splits tokens between query and document. Fifty 2,000-token documents are not fifty 200-token passages if truncation and batching differ. Chunk upstream, truncate deterministically, and measure how often relevance evidence falls beyond the retained span.
- **Parallelism reduces latency, not total work.** Scoring 100 pairs in batches of 25 is four batch steps; running them concurrently raises accelerator contention and memory. Use bounded queues, batch timeouts and admission control — under overload a deliberately smaller depth beats unbounded queueing that makes every request miss its deadline. Network counts too: fetching full bodies for 500 candidates can dominate latency even when scoring is fast.
- **Capacity arithmetic links the per-request budget to fleet size.** At 200 queries per second and depth 50 the precision service must sustain $200 \times 50 = 10{,}000$ pair evaluations per second before retries or shadow traffic. A model server good for 2,500 pairs per second at that token length needs $10{,}000 / 2{,}500 = 4$ fully utilized replicas in the ideal case; headroom, uneven batches and p95 objectives require more. Raising depth to 100 doubles the arrival rate even though retrieval latency is unchanged.

**Rules to remember**

- The recall stage sets a hard ceiling: recall@k ≤ r/R for every later k. Rerankers reorder; they never recover.
- Always state recall with a cutoff and a relevance definition. "High recall" is not a number.
- Report candidate coverage and final ordering quality separately, or you cannot tell which one broke.
- Every stage transition is a contract: input width, output width, score meaning, timeout, fallback, filters, IDs.
- Ranks travel across stages safely; raw scores from different scales do not.
- Depth is chosen backward from a quality floor and a latency allocation, not forward from what is affordable.
- Two sources at depth 100 each give between 100 and 200 unique candidates, never automatically 200.
- The correct budget is the *smallest* configuration meeting the quality floor under representative concurrency, with headroom and a defined degraded mode.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Trying to fix candidate recall with a better reranker.** The missing document is not in C, so no precision-stage compute is the fix. The fix is upstream.
- **Quoting recall without a cutoff.** Recall rises with depth, so an unqualified figure is unfalsifiable and usually flattering.
- **Passing raw scores between stages.** BM25, cosine similarity and a probability are three scales, and averaging them silently weights whichever has the largest range.
- **Filtering after retrieval without adjusting depth.** 50 retrieved at 10 percent eligibility is a 5-candidate rerank wearing a 50-candidate label.
- **Reading `n × p` as a guarantee.** It is an expectation under an independence assumption that vector neighborhoods and tenant skew break. Use percentiles.
- **Adaptive routing on an uncalibrated confidence score.** A wrong cheap-route decision starves a hard query into a permanent early false negative — the error class the funnel cannot undo.
- **Choosing depth on a quiet benchmark.** An offline sweep finds the frontier; online traffic validates cost. Log actual depth, source contribution, union size, filter attrition, tokens, batch count and queue time, and plot marginal gain per candidate.
- **Counting near-duplicate passages as coverage.** Ten chunks of one document inside a 200-item budget is one piece of evidence taking five percent of the funnel.

**How to approach the questions**

1. First ask *which stage* the scenario is about. If a relevant document is missing entirely it is a recall-stage question, however much the story is about the reranker.
2. Given R relevant items and how many entered the candidate set, compute r/R immediately — that is the ceiling, and most distractors quote a number above it.
3. Separate precision@k from recall@k at the same k. Four relevant in a top ten is 0.4 precision and 0.8 recall against five known relevant.
4. For budgeting questions check *both* constraints — quality floor and latency or capacity allocation. The answer is the smallest depth satisfying both, not the best quality.
5. When two sources have per-source budgets, never add them. Find the stated overlap and take the union.
6. When a filter appears, ask whether it runs before or after the budget is spent. That ordering usually is the question.
7. For capacity, multiply: QPS × depth = pair evaluations per second, then divide by per-replica throughput and round up.
