The recall stage of the funnel protects coverage with cheap per-document signals; someone still has to read the shortlist carefully. The cross-encoder is the standard instrument for that careful reading: a transformer that scores a query and a document together, in one pass, with full token-level interaction. It is the most accurate ranker most teams can deploy, and because it can only judge one pair at a time, it is exactly the model that forces the funnel architecture from the previous lesson. This lesson covers its mechanism, its tradeoff against the bi-encoder, and the engineering of how many candidates it should rescore.

## Cross-Encoders: Joint Query-Document Attention

A cross-encoder consumes the query and the document as a single input sequence: `[CLS] query tokens [SEP] document tokens [SEP]`. That sequence passes through a transformer encoder, and in every layer of self-attention each query token attends to every document token and every document token attends back. A lightweight scoring head, typically one linear layer over the `[CLS]` representation, emits a single relevance logit per pair. Training uses labeled pairs (relevant versus irrelevant, or graded labels) with a classification or ranking loss, and the negative examples matter enormously: models trained with hard negatives mined by BM25 or a bi-encoder learn the distinctions that production traffic actually requires.

Joint attention is the entire point. Because the representation of each document token is computed while looking at the query, the model can resolve ambiguity, bind constraints, and weigh phrases in a way that per-document computation never can. Three recurring wins illustrate this. Disambiguation: for the query `jaguar 0-60 time`, the token `jaguar` is read jointly with `0-60`, so the car sense is favored over the animal. Negation: for `exercises that do not load the knee`, attention can bind `do not` to `load the knee`, while lexical matching and most embedding models treat those as strong positive evidence for knee exercises. Numeric and unit constraints: for `laptop under 4 pounds`, the model can relate `weighs 3.8 lb` to the constraint token by token, including the unit conversion.

The cost structure follows directly from the mechanism. The pair encoding depends on both inputs, so nothing about a document can be precomputed for a query that has not yet arrived. Scoring n candidates costs n forward passes through the network, batched for hardware efficiency, and attention inside each pass grows quadratically with sequence length. Concretely, a base-sized cross-encoder on a modern inference GPU processes on the order of 1,500 to 3,000 short pairs per second; on CPU it is one to two orders of magnitude slower. With a 512-token window, a query of 30 tokens leaves 480 or so for the document, so full documents must be truncated or the pipeline must rerank focused chunks instead of whole pages.

A worked budget makes this tangible. Suppose passages average 180 tokens and batches of 32 pairs take about 18 ms. Rescoring 100 candidates needs four batches, roughly 60 to 75 ms of model time once queuing is included at the tail. That number is the direct input to the funnel budgeting from the previous lesson: it is why rerank depth is measured in the tens or low hundreds, never in the corpus size.

The judgment layer has four parts. First, raw logits are not calibrated probabilities: they order pairs within one model reasonably well, but blending them arithmetically with BM25 scores or with another model's logits without calibration produces erratic rankings. Second, truncation can silently delete the decisive evidence, so the recall stage should retrieve chunks small enough that the relevant span survives inside the window; measure the truncation rate and treat it as a quality metric. Third, cross-encoders trained on short web queries degrade off-distribution, for example on long legal or medical documents, so evaluate by content segment rather than trusting one aggregate. Fourth, batch by token budget rather than pair count, because fifty 500-token pairs and fifty 150-token pairs are very different amounts of work.

## Bi-Encoder vs Cross-Encoder: Effectiveness vs Efficiency

Recall the bi-encoder from IR 201: encode query and document independently into fixed-size vectors, then compare them with a dot product or cosine. Documents are encoded once, offline, and loaded into an approximate nearest-neighbor index; at query time the system encodes the query once and searches the index. The per-pair online cost is effectively one multiply after a single query encoding, which is why a bi-encoder can scan millions of passages in tens of milliseconds.

The asymmetry between the two architectures is informational. A bi-encoder must compress everything a document might ever need to say to any future query into one fixed vector, say 768 floats, before the query is known. A cross-encoder defers the comparison until the query is known and then uses the full token sequences of both sides. Per judged pair, that is orders of magnitude more representational capacity, and it shows in measurements: on standard passage-ranking benchmarks, a fine-tuned cross-encoder reranking a first-stage top-100 consistently beats the ranking produced by a same-scale bi-encoder alone, typically by several points of nDCG or MRR. The gap concentrates in predictable places: exact lexical signals such as part numbers and rare names, which a vector smooths over; negation and conjunctive constraints; fine-grained numeric conditions; and out-of-domain text where the bi-encoder was never trained.

The efficiency verdict is equally decisive in the opposite direction. Scoring a corpus of one million passages with a cross-encoder at 32 pairs per 18 ms batch would take over nine minutes per query. No serving architecture tolerates that, which is why the two models are complements rather than competitors: the bi-encoder (and BM25 beside it) owns the recall stage and produces the shortlist, and the cross-encoder owns the precision stage over that shortlist. Every serious production ranker is this pair, not a choice between them.

The gap is not fixed. Domain fine-tuning with mined hard negatives, larger embedding dimensions, and better ANN indexes all narrow it from the bi-encoder side; out-of-domain zero-shot traffic and constraint-heavy queries widen it. There is also a legitimate single-stage exception: with a small corpus, say a few thousand documents, and modest query rates, brute-force cross-encoding every candidate is affordable, and the funnel collapses into one simpler stage. Internal tools with small corpora should take that simplification rather than operating machinery they do not need.

The professional comparison rule is to evaluate pipelines, not models. A bi-encoder top-10 and a bi-encoder top-100 plus cross-encoder rerank are different systems with different latencies and prices; compare them at equal p95 latency and equal infrastructure cost, and report the retriever-reranker pair as the unit of analysis. A team that reports only model-level benchmark numbers will systematically overinvest in the reranker and underinvest in candidate recall.

## Rerank Depth: How Many Candidates to Rescore

Rerank depth k is the number of first-stage candidates the cross-encoder actually rescores; everything deeper keeps its retrieval order or is discarded. Choosing k is a two-sided economic decision. Too shallow, and the candidate ceiling binds: if relevant documents tend to sit near rank 150 in the first stage and k is 50, the reranker never sees them, final metrics stay flat, and upgrading the reranker accomplishes nothing. Too deep, and model cost grows roughly linearly in k while the marginal gain flattens, because retrieval scores at depth are noisy and the candidates there are mostly irrelevant. Worse, the deep region is off-distribution for a reranker trained on top-100 negatives, so its scores there are less trustworthy, and confidently wrong rescoring of very off-topic pairs can occasionally hurt the final order.

The selection method is a sweep, not a guess. On labeled queries, rerank at depths 20, 50, 100, 200 and record final metric and latency at each point. A representative result:

| Depth | nDCG@10 | p95 latency |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 | 0.410 | 25 ms |
| 50 | 0.440 | 50 ms |
| 100 | 0.452 | 95 ms |
| 200 | 0.455 | 180 ms |

Under a 100 ms tail budget, depth 100 is the justified operating point: it buys most of the available quality, while depth 200 adds 0.003 of nDCG for nearly double the latency. The knee of the curve, not the largest affordable number, is the answer.

Depth is a property of the retriever-reranker pair, not of the reranker alone. When the first stage improves, for example after adding the hybrid fusion of the next lesson, relevant documents move from rank 150 toward rank 40, and a shallower depth now buys the same final quality at lower cost. Recompute the sweep whenever either stage changes materially: new embedder, new fusion, new reranker, or a corpus whose size doubled.

The fleet arithmetic connects depth to capacity exactly as the previous lesson's budgeting predicts. Pair throughput equals query rate times depth: 200 queries per second at depth 100 is 20,000 pairs per second, and at 2,500 pairs per second per replica that is eight replicas before headroom, retries, and shadow traffic. Halving depth halves the reranking fleet. This is why depth debates are capacity debates in disguise.

Several practical rules protect quality at the boundary. Deduplicate before rescoring: near-identical chunks from one source waste pair budget and crowd out diverse evidence, so collapse by source document or content hash first. Order by the reranker within the top k and keep retrieval order below k, rather than arithmetically blending uncalibrated scores across the boundary; if blending is required, calibrate first. Adapt depth by observable query class: an exact part-number query needs depth 20, an ambiguous natural-language question may merit 150, but gate on the class, never on uncalibrated model confidence, and always enforce a hard maximum. Finally, treat fallback as part of the depth decision: if the reranker times out, serving the retrieval order is the evaluated degraded mode, so the first-stage order must remain acceptable on its own.

A cautionary narrative ties these together. A team doubled depth from 100 to 200 to chase a small offline gain, p95 latency doubled, autoscaling lagged during a traffic peak, batch timeouts fired, and the fallback path served unreranked retrieval order to a third of peak queries. Production quality ended up worse than at depth 100. The depth chosen on a quiet benchmark was not the depth the system could sustain under representative concurrency, and the sweep must therefore include load, not just labels.
