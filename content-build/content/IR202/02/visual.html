1. Funnel Meets Reranker

Recall Stage vs Precision Stage: The Ranking Funnel said the cheap stage protects coverage and someone still has to read the shortlist carefully. Budgeting Candidates per Stage said each count is set by what the next stage can afford. Here is the model that occupies the precision stage — drag how many candidates it reads and watch the budget decide the architecture. Like a job ad: a keyword filter reads ten thousand CVs in a second, a human reads twelve properly.

The reranker does not merely fit the funnel — it is what forces it. Because a cross-encoder can only judge one query-document pair at a time, and nothing about a document can be precomputed for a query that has not arrived, the corpus can never be its input. Some cheaper stage must hand it a shortlist first. Every number on this panel is the previous lesson's candidate budget, priced.

2. Reading Both Together

A cross-encoder takes one sequence — [CLS] query [SEP] document [SEP] — and in every layer each query token attends to every document token and back. Pick a query, then flip how the pair is encoded and watch the verdict change. Like reading a question and an answer side by side, instead of memorising the answer first and being handed the question afterwards.

The scoring head is one linear layer over the [CLS] representation, emitting a single relevance logit per pair — and that logit is not a calibrated probability. It orders pairs within one model reasonably well; adding, averaging or thresholding it against a BM25 score or another model's logit, without calibration, produces erratic rankings. What the model learns to separate is set by its negatives: hard negatives mined by BM25 or a bi-encoder are what teach it the distinctions real traffic requires.

3. Compress Now Or Later

The bi-encoder compresses a document into 768 floats before the query is known; the cross-encoder waits and then reads both token sequences. Drag the corpus size and watch which bargain the hardware allows. Like writing a one-line summary of every book in advance, versus rereading the relevant pages once someone finally tells you the question.

The gap is not fixed. Domain fine-tuning with mined hard negatives, larger embedding dimensions and better ANN indexes all narrow it from the bi-encoder side; zero-shot out-of-domain traffic and constraint-heavy queries widen it. And compare pipelines, not models: a bi-encoder top-10 and a bi-encoder top-100 plus cross-encoder rerank are different systems with different latencies and prices. Judge them at equal p95 latency and equal infrastructure cost, or you will systematically overinvest in the reranker and underinvest in candidate recall.

4. Finding The Knee

Rerank depth k is how many candidates get rescored; everything deeper keeps its retrieval order. Set a tail-latency budget, then change the first stage and watch the answer move. Like buying insurance: the first policy covers most of the risk, and the third costs as much again for a rounding error.

Three rules protect quality at the boundary. Deduplicate before rescoring — near-identical chunks from one source waste pair budget and crowd out diverse evidence, so collapse by source document or content hash first. Order by the reranker within the top k and keep retrieval order below k, rather than blending uncalibrated scores across the boundary. And adapt depth by observable query class — an exact part-number query needs 20, an ambiguous natural-language question may merit 150 — gating on the class, never on the model's own confidence, always under a hard maximum.

5. Depth Is Capacity

Pair throughput is query rate × depth, and a replica does about 2,500 pairs a second. Move either dial and the fleet moves with it — then hit peak and watch what the fallback path serves. Like a restaurant: the price of a dish is one thing, how many cooks you must keep on payroll to serve it at eight o'clock is another.

A real incident, and the reason the sweep must include load. A team doubled depth from 100 to 200 to chase 0.003 of nDCG. p95 latency doubled, autoscaling lagged during a traffic peak, batch timeouts fired, and the fallback served unreranked retrieval order to a third of peak queries. Production quality ended up worse than at depth 100. The depth chosen on a quiet benchmark was not the depth the system could sustain under representative concurrency — which is also why the first-stage order has to be acceptable on its own.
