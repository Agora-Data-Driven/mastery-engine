The previous lesson fused two different retrievers to cover two different failure modes. Learned sparse retrieval attacks the same problem from inside one system: it keeps the inverted index from IR 101, with all its operational virtues, and replaces the hand-crafted term statistics with weights a neural model assigns. The result matches or beats dense retrieval on many benchmarks while preserving exact-match behavior, per-term explainability, and the entire Lucene-style serving stack. This lesson covers the two expansion strategies, how neural weights live on classic postings, and what shipped systems look like.

## SPLADE: Learned Term Expansion and Impact Weights

SPLADE, the Sparse Lexical and Expansion model, reuses a masked language model transformer. Text passes through the encoder, and at every token position the masked-language-model head produces a distribution over the entire vocabulary, roughly 30,000 terms. Each position's distribution is squashed with log(1 + ReLU(x)) and the per-position results are max-pooled into one vector over the vocabulary. Two properties make this vector special. It is sparse: a sparsity regularizer during training drives most entries to exactly zero, typically leaving a few dozen to a couple hundred active terms. And it contains terms absent from the original text, because the language model head naturally activates related vocabulary: encoding `heart attack symptoms` may assign positive weight to `myocardial`, `cardiac`, and `chest pain`. That is learned expansion, and it is the vocabulary-mismatch fix operating inside one model.

The same encoder runs on documents at ingest and on queries at search time. The retrieval score is the dot product of the two sparse vectors, which is a sum over shared terms of the product of their weights. This is exactly the algebra of an inverted index: each term's posting list stores per-document impact weights instead of term frequencies, and scoring walks the lists of the query's active terms. BM25's tf and IDF are simply replaced by contextual, learned weights.

Contextuality is the deep difference from IR 103's term weighting. Under BM25, a term's contribution is fixed by corpus statistics: `jaguar` always has the same IDF. Under SPLADE, the weight of `jaguar` in a document depends on the surrounding text, high with `0-60` and `horsepower`, modulated differently with `rainforest` and `prey`. The weights are trained end-to-end on relevance data such as MS MARCO with mined hard negatives, so they encode what actually separates relevant from merely term-sharing documents.

The sparsity budget is an explicit engineering dial. Without regularization the model activates too many terms, postings grow, and query latency dies. Training with a FLOPS-style regularizer lets you choose the effectiveness-versus-index-size operating point, and distillation variants push quality further at the same sparsity.

Judgment points: expansion occasionally activates wrong-sense terms, adding noise that a reranker must absorb; real-valued weights must be quantized for storage, costing a little accuracy; and the model version becomes part of the index's identity, since every document's weights came from one specific checkpoint.

## Document Expansion with doc2query

doc2query attacks vocabulary mismatch from the opposite side: do not change the scoring at all, change the documents. A sequence-to-sequence model, classically T5, is trained on pairs of queries and the documents that answered them, learning to generate queries a document could plausibly answer. At index time, each document is run through the generator, and a set of predicted queries, commonly 10 to 40, is appended to the document text. The augmented document is then indexed by plain BM25 in a plain inverted index.

The mechanism works through two channels at once. Expansion: the generated queries are written in the vocabulary of real searchers, so the document now literally contains terms users type, closing the mismatch gap. Reweighting: the generator tends to repeat the document's salient concepts across its predictions, inflating those terms' frequencies, which BM25 correctly interprets as importance. On MS MARCO, doc2query historically gave BM25 a large relative improvement, on the order of double-digit percentage gains in ranking metrics, for zero change to the serving stack.

The costs are equally concrete. Generating 10 queries for a 1 million document corpus is 10 million model inferences, an offline batch job with real compute cost that must rerun whenever documents change. The index grows substantially, often by a third or more, because every document carries its appended queries. Hallucinated predictions can pollute the index with off-topic terms. And the expansions freeze at generation time: a better generator released next year helps only after a full re-expansion pass.

The contrast with SPLADE clarifies the design space. doc2query expands at index time and keeps classical tf-based scoring; SPLADE expands at encode time for every text and scores with learned weights. doc2query is simpler to bolt onto an existing BM25 system, since nothing about scoring or the index format changes. SPLADE is more precise, because weights are contextual and continuous rather than implicit in term repetition, and its expansion happens for queries too, not just documents. A team with a stable corpus and a working BM25 cluster often tries doc2query first; a team willing to run inference at ingest usually graduates to impact-weighted models.

## Impact-Sorted Indexes: Neural Weights on the Classic Inverted Index

The inverted index from IR 101 stores postings as (docID, tf, positions). Learned sparse retrieval stores (docID, weight), where weight is the model's impact score for that term in that document, quantized to one or two bytes. Everything else about the index survives: dictionary, skip structures, blocked compression, segment merges, memory mapping. This is a scoring upgrade, not an infrastructure replacement, and that is the architectural appeal.

The sorting decision changes. Classical postings are docID-sorted, which enables merge intersection. Impact-sorted postings are ordered by descending weight, which enables aggressive early termination. Recall WAND and MaxScore from IR 103: dynamic pruning needs per-list score upper bounds to skip documents that cannot crack the top k. With impact sorting, the maximum weight in each remaining block of a list is known a priori, so the bounds are tight, and with quantized weights the bounds are exact rather than estimated. Processing only the highest-impact postings for each query term yields the same top-k results as exhaustive scoring at a fraction of the comparisons.

Quantization deserves care. The model emits real-valued weights; the index stores a byte or two. Uniform or learned quantization maps the weight range into 256 levels, and small weights are often thresholded to zero outright, enforcing sparsity at index time. The accuracy cost of 8-bit quantization is typically small, and it buys a compact, cache-friendly index whose scores remain deterministic.

Two operational consequences stand out. First, learned weights free the index from corpus statistics: BM25's IDF shifts as the corpus grows and changes, subtly altering scores under your feet, while a learned impact weight depends only on the document and the model checkpoint, so incremental document updates are clean and localized. Second, the coupling moves to the model version: upgrading the encoder invalidates every stored weight, so a model change means a full re-encode and index rebuild, the same lifecycle discipline dense embedding models impose.

Query latency in this world looks like BM25 with extra terms. The query encodes to a sparse vector; scoring walks the postings of its active terms; expansion terms add lists to walk, which is why the sparsity budget from training shows up directly as serving latency. Impact sorting with early termination claws much of that back, and the whole workload runs comfortably on CPUs.

## Shipped Learned Sparse: ELSER and Neural Sparse Search

Learned sparse is not a paper idea; it ships in mainstream engines. Elastic's ELSER, the Elastic Learned Sparse EncodeR, is a production expansion model served as a managed inference endpoint. Documents pass through it at ingest, producing weighted tokens stored in a rank_features-style field; queries pass through a lighter encoding path; scoring is the sparse dot product executed by the same engine that runs BM25. Later versions improved effectiveness and trimmed inference cost, and the capability is tied to Elastic's commercial tiers, which is a real procurement consideration.

OpenSearch's neural sparse search offers the equivalent in the open ecosystem. Sparse encoding models from Hugging Face, including published SPLADE checkpoints, deploy through the ML Commons framework and write into rank_features fields. Two serving modes matter in practice. Document-only encoding runs the heavy model on documents and a lightweight or lexical path on queries, minimizing query latency. Bi-encoder mode encodes both sides with full models for maximum quality. OpenSearch also supports combining neural sparse scores with BM25 in hybrid queries when teams want belt and suspenders.

The operational profile against dense vectors is the deciding comparison. A learned sparse index lives in the same engine, the same cluster, the same ACL model, and the same runbook as the BM25 system the team already operates; there is no second storage system to reconcile. Memory economics favor sparse at scale: a few hundred quantized term weights per document, with no float vectors and no proximity graph. Serving runs on CPUs, which simplifies capacity planning. Zero-shot transfer to new domains has been strong on benchmarks such as BEIR, often beating bi-encoders that were never fine-tuned on the target domain.

Choose learned sparse when traffic mixes exact-match and paraphrase queries, when the team already runs Lucene-family infrastructure, when CPU-only serving is a constraint, or when per-term score explainability matters for debugging and trust. Dense retrieval still wins for multilingual and multimodal corpora, for very short documents where term sparsity gives little grip, and where a managed vector database is already the operational home. The summary judgment: learned sparse is the inverted index taught to read. You keep every mechanism from IR 101, buy a large fraction of IR 201's semantics, and pay with ingest-time inference, quantized weights, and model-versioned indexes that demand full rebuilds when the encoder changes.
