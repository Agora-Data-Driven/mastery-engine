1. One Index, Not Two

The Atrium Pattern: BM25 + Vectors + RRF + Reranker covered two failure modes with two retrievers and a Reciprocal Rank Fusion step. Learned sparse covers both from inside a single inverted index. Tap an architecture and watch the parts list — and the operational bill — change. Like fixing a draughty house: bolt on a second heater, or insulate the walls you already have.

Nothing here says the fused stack was wrong — it is the pattern you ship. The claim is narrower and it is operational: a learned sparse index lives in the same engine, cluster, ACL model and runbook as the BM25 system you already run, so there is no second storage system to reconcile. The reranker still sits behind it either way.

2. The Sparsity Dial

SPLADE runs an MLM head over ~30,000 vocabulary terms at every token position, squashes each with log(1 + ReLU(x)) and max-pools them into one vector. A sparsity regulariser then decides how many of those 30,000 entries survive. Drag it. Like a spice rack: too many jars and you can never find anything; too few and every dish tastes the same.

The deep difference from BM25 is not sparsity, it is contextuality. BM25 fixes jaguar's contribution by corpus statistics; SPLADE weights it high beside 0-60 and horsepower, differently beside rainforest and prey. The weights are trained end-to-end on relevance data such as MS MARCO with mined hard negatives.

3. Two Ways To Expand

Both fix vocabulary mismatch; they disagree about where. doc2query rewrites the documents and leaves scoring alone; SPLADE rewrites the weights and leaves the text alone. Pick a side, then drag doc2query's one dial. Like a shop that either reprints every label in customer words, or trains the staff to understand what customers say.

A team with a stable corpus and a working BM25 cluster usually tries doc2query first, because nothing about scoring or the index format changes. A team willing to run inference at ingest graduates to impact-weighted models, because contextual continuous weights beat importance signalled by repetition — and because only SPLADE expands the query as well as the document.

4. Impact-Sorted Postings

Classical postings are docID-sorted, which is what enables merge intersection. Impact-sorted postings are ordered by descending weight, which is what enables early termination. Flip the order, set k, and count the postings actually examined. Like a pile of job applications: sorted by surname you read them all; sorted by score you stop after the shortlist.

Two operational consequences. Learned weights free the index from corpus statistics — BM25's IDF shifts as the corpus grows, altering scores under your feet, while an impact weight depends only on the document and the checkpoint, so incremental updates stay local. But the coupling moves to the model version: upgrading the encoder invalidates every stored weight, so a model change means a full re-encode and index rebuild.

5. Shipped Learned Sparse

This is not a paper idea. Pick a shipped configuration to see where the heavy model runs, then tap a constraint to see which family it argues for. Like choosing a boiler: the spec sheet matters less than whether it fits the pipework you already have.

The summary judgment: learned sparse is the inverted index taught to read. You keep every mechanism from IR 101, buy a large fraction of IR 201's semantics, and pay with ingest-time inference, quantised weights, and model-versioned indexes that demand a full rebuild when the encoder changes.
