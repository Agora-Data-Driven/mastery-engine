1. The Interaction Spectrum

Bi-Encoder vs Cross-Encoder: Effectiveness vs Efficiency gave you the two ends and told you to pick one. They are the ends of a spectrum, and late interaction is the engineered midpoint. Tap an architecture; drag how many candidates each query must score. Like hiring: skim CVs filed months ago, sit every applicant down for a real interview, or file each CV sentence separately and match your questions against them line by line.

Read the middle column as a choice about when the interaction happens, not about how big the model is. Nothing in late interaction attends across the query and the document — the document side never sees the query at all. What changed is that the evidence was kept at token granularity instead of being averaged into one vector before it could be used.

2. MaxSim Token By Token

The score is max over the document, then sum over the query. Tap a query token to see which document token won its max, then drag the one similarity that separates the two documents. Like a checklist: each item scores against the best thing in the box that answers it, and the box's total is how many items it covered.

Two serving details hide in this picture. The fifth query token is a placeholder mask token — ColBERT augments the query with them so the encoder can expand it with learned context, which is why a four-word query has five vectors. And on the document side, the most redundant token vectors can be pruned from the index with little quality loss, because a max only ever needs the best counterpart.

3. The Token Vector Bill

Keeping every token costs 30–50× a single vector, and scoring MaxSim over a whole corpus is worse. PLAID answers both with three cuts — step through them and watch which number each one attacks. Like a warehouse: first shrink every box, then only walk the aisles that could hold what you want, and only open the boxes on the shelf you reached.

Notice the shape of the three cuts: coarse centroids narrow the field, then exact residuals decide the order. PLAID is the ranking funnel from lesson 01 rebuilt inside one model — centroids are the recall stage, residuals the precision stage. The compression factor and the activated fraction used here are illustrative dials; the real values depend on the index and the query, and only the shape generalises.

4. Teacher And Student

The best relevance model you can build is usually too slow to serve, so you train a cheap student on the expensive teacher's own scores. Three things to feel: what the loss matches, why soft scores beat labels, and where the bill lands. Like an apprentice watching a master grade work: the grades themselves are the lesson, and the gaps between them are the real lesson.

Four failure modes deserve respect, and two of them are immune to more data. Inherited bias: the teacher's systematic errors become the student's training targets, so audit the teacher first. Capacity ceiling: a student far smaller than the teacher cannot fit its function. Distribution mismatch: distilled on web queries, served on support tickets, failing silently. Cascading: distilling a distilled model compounds error — keep one generation between them.
