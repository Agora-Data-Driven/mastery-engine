**The big idea**: IR 202 left you with a funnel you can tune end to end — **Recall Stage vs Precision Stage: The Ranking Funnel**, **Reciprocal Rank Fusion**, **The Atrium Pattern: BM25 + Vectors + RRF + Reranker** — running on the geometry IR 201 gave you in **Learned Embeddings: Meaning as Geometry**. Every one of those stages operates on a unit that somebody else chose. Chunking is where it gets chosen: the first irreversible decision in a retrieval-augmented system, fixing the unit of indexing and therefore the ceiling on what any downstream retriever, reranker or LLM can ever see. A hybrid retriever with perfect BM25 tuning and a state-of-the-art reranker still fails if the fact the user needs was split across two chunks or drowned inside one bloated chunk. And re-chunking means re-embedding and re-indexing the whole corpus, so these choices are expensive to revisit and deserve deliberate engineering rather than a library default.

**Key concepts**

- **A chunk is the atomic unit your index stores**: one BM25 posting target, one embedding vector, one candidate the retriever can return. Nothing downstream returns half a chunk or splices two together. Sizes are in tokens, not characters, because both embedding models and LLM context windows meter in tokens; a common working range is 200 to 800 tokens.
- **Embedding dilution is the mechanism behind "small chunks retrieve precisely".** An embedding model compresses a chunk into a single vector, and that vector behaves like a weighted blend of the meanings inside the text. A 1,500-token chunk covering refund policy, shipping times and warranty claims lands near the centroid of those three topics, roughly $\frac{1}{3}(v_{\text{refund}} + v_{\text{ship}} + v_{\text{warranty}})$, so its cosine similarity against a focused refund query is dragged down by the two components irrelevant to it. Every extra topic packed into a chunk costs similarity for all the others.
- **Small chunks invert the failure: they fragment meaning.** A 100-token chunk may contain "It must be submitted within 30 days" with no indication of what "it" is, because the referent lived two sentences earlier. Pronouns, definitions and multi-step arguments get severed, and the LLM receives shards it cannot interpret. Large chunks carry full context but blur the vectors, rank relevant chunks lower, and spend more of the generation window on mostly irrelevant text. That is the **core tradeoff** of chunk size: precision versus completeness. You are choosing which way to fail.
- **Overlap is the standard hedge against boundary damage, and its cost is exactly computable.** A sliding window of size $s$ tokens with overlap $o$ advances with stride $s - o$, so each chunk repeats its predecessor's last $o$ tokens and a fact straddling one boundary survives intact somewhere. Over a document of length $L$ the chunk count is about $\frac{L - o}{s - o}$, so the index inflates by the factor $\frac{s}{s - o}$. A 10,000-token document at $s = 500$, $o = 0$ yields 20 chunks; at $o = 100$ the stride is 400 and you get $\frac{9900}{400} \approx 25$ — a 25% increase in vectors to embed, store and search, exactly the predicted $500/400 = 1.25$. Overlap of 10 to 20 percent of chunk size is the usual range; 50 percent doubles the index for marginal extra protection.
- **Worked boundary example.** A 2,000-token policy document, chunk size 500, overlap 100: chunk 1 spans tokens 0–500, chunk 2 spans 400–900, chunk 3 spans 800–1300. A sentence occupying tokens 480–520 is cut in chunk 1 but whole in chunk 2. Without overlap it exists nowhere intact in the index, and no retriever setting recovers it.
- **Overlap has a second-order failure mode: near-duplicate crowding.** Two adjacent chunks sharing 20 percent of their text often earn nearly identical scores for a query that hits the shared region, so both occupy top-k slots and one limited retrieval slot is wasted on redundancy. Production systems deduplicate near-identical hits before assembling context.
- **The expert move is to stop assuming the retrieval unit must equal the generation unit.** In **small-to-big retrieval** (also called parent-document retrieval) you embed small, precise child chunks for matching, but when a child matches you return its larger parent section to the LLM. Sharp vectors and complete context at once, paid for in extra storage and a parent-lookup step. Judgment guide: short factoid queries over homogeneous text tolerate small chunks; synthesis-heavy questions over dense documents favour larger chunks or small-to-big.
- **Structure-aware chunking splits along boundaries the document already declares.** The workhorse is recursive splitting with a separator hierarchy: try the strongest separator first (blank lines between paragraphs), and only if a piece still exceeds the size limit fall back to weaker ones — single newlines, then sentence boundaries, then words. Chunks come out as large as allowed and almost never cut inside a semantic unit. Format-specific structure does better still: Markdown splits on heading levels, HTML on DOM elements, code on function and class boundaries, PDFs on layout blocks. Some elements must be **atomic**: a table split mid-row indexes as gibberish in both halves, and a function split mid-body loses its signature or its return.
- **Semantic chunking manufactures boundaries where none are marked.** Split the text into sentences, embed each one (or a small sliding window of sentences, to stabilise the signal), and compute the cosine distance between each adjacent pair, $1 - \cos\theta = 1 - \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert}$ — exactly the complement of the cosine similarity you already use for retrieval. While consecutive sentences discuss one topic the distances stay small; when the topic shifts, the distance spikes. Place a boundary wherever it exceeds a threshold, commonly a percentile of that document's own distance distribution. Worked example: distances of 0.11, 0.09, 0.14, 0.58, 0.12, 0.10 with a 95th-percentile threshold of about 0.40 yields exactly one boundary, before the sentence that produced 0.58 — splitting the passage into its two actual topics. Lower the percentile to the 80th and smaller shifts fire, producing more and smaller chunks.
- **Splitting destroys global context by construction, and contextual enrichment repairs it at index time.** "The termination notice period is 60 days" is useless in an index of 400 contracts: which party, which agreement? "Returns are accepted within 30 days" from one product page will match return-policy queries about every product. Enrichment injects document-level context into each chunk *before* embedding and *before* BM25 indexing, so both signals carry it.
- **Breadcrumb prepending is the cheapest and highest-yield form.** Prefix each chunk with its document title and the heading path containing it: `Acme MSA 2025 > 9. Termination > 9.2 Notice`. The query "Acme termination notice period" now matches lexically on "Acme" and "termination" through BM25 and semantically through the embedding, where before it matched neither. The **headers** preserved during structure-aware splitting are exactly the material for these breadcrumbs, which is why the two techniques compose. A related pattern prepends short document-level **summaries** or key entities — product, client, date range — to every chunk.
- **LLM-generated contextual enrichment is the heavyweight form**, popularised as contextual retrieval (Anthropic, 2024). For each chunk an LLM receives the full document plus the chunk and writes 50 to 100 tokens situating it: "This clause is from the Acme master services agreement and specifies the notice period the customer must give to terminate for convenience." That text is prepended before embedding and before BM25 indexing. Anthropic reported that contextual embeddings cut top-20 retrieval failure rates by roughly 35 percent, and contextual embeddings plus contextual BM25 by roughly 49 percent; treat the exact figures as benchmark-specific, but the direction is well replicated. The objection is cost — one LLM call per chunk with the whole document in the prompt — answered by prompt caching: the document is a stable prefix reused across all of its chunks' calls, so you pay full input price roughly once per document rather than once per chunk.

**Rules to remember**

- Chunk sizes are in tokens, not characters. 200–800 tokens is the ordinary working range.
- Stride is $s - o$ and index inflation is $\frac{s}{s - o}$. Use 10–20% overlap; 50% doubles the index.
- Overlap buys boundary safety and pays in near-duplicates. Deduplicate before assembling context.
- The retrieval unit need not be the generation unit. That is what small-to-big exists for.
- Every embedding model has a maximum input length and truncates beyond it **without error** — many cap around 512 tokens, others accept 8,000 or more. A chunk longer than the cap has a tail permanently invisible to vector search.
- Structure first; reach for semantic chunking only where structure is absent or unreliable.
- Tables, code blocks and lists are atomic units, oversized or not.
- Structure-aware splits are deterministic and reproducible across re-index runs; semantic boundaries move when the embedding model changes.
- Index the enriched text, but store the original chunk boundaries for generation and citation.
- Enrichment is derived data with an invalidation problem: re-enrich on re-index or it goes stale silently.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Accepting the library default.** It is the one decision the whole pipeline inherits and the most expensive to revisit.
- **Chunking past the embedding model's input cap.** The chunk sits in your index looking fine; its tail was truncated at embed time and can never match. Nothing errors.
- **Turning overlap up "to be safe".** At 50 percent you have doubled the vector count and multiplied near-duplicate crowding: top-k now holds two copies of one passage.
- **Reaching for semantic chunking on documents that have structure.** In published comparisons it beats naive fixed-size splitting only modestly and rarely beats good structure-aware splitting, while costing an embedding call per sentence and producing variable-length chunks that still need minimum and maximum caps.
- **Ignoring determinism.** Semantic boundaries shift when the embedding model changes, which complicates incremental re-indexing.
- **Sending the enriched text to the LLM.** The prefix exists to improve matching; duplicated on every chunk it wastes generation-window tokens and muddies citation.
- **Trusting an LLM enrichment blindly.** A hallucinated context is worse than none: it injects confident falsehoods into the index itself, where they attract queries the chunk cannot answer. Spot-check, and constrain the prompt to describe rather than infer.
- **Enriching a fast-churning corpus.** When a document is edited its chunks' contexts go stale silently — LLM enrichment suits mostly-static corpora.

**How to approach the questions**

1. When a scenario reports a retrieval failure, first ask whether the fact could ever have been retrieved: was it whole inside one chunk? Downstream tuning never fixes a split fact.
2. Convert any size/overlap statement into stride and inflation factor, $s - o$ and $\frac{s}{s - o}$, before comparing options. Distractors quote counts computed as if overlap were free.
3. For "which chunker" questions, test whether the document has reliable structure. Markdown, HTML and code point to structure-aware; transcripts, chat logs, OCR output and scraped text with mangled markup point to semantic.
4. For semantic chunking, remember the threshold is a percentile of that document's own distances — lowering it produces more and smaller chunks, never fewer.
5. Separate deterministic enrichment (title, headers, summary prefix) from generative enrichment (LLM context): cost, staleness and hallucination risk all differ, and "the cheapest improvement" almost always means breadcrumbs.
6. Ask *when* something happens. Enrichment and splitting are index time; deduplication, ordering and budgeting are query time.
7. Watch for absolute claims. "Semantic chunking is better" and "bigger chunks are better" are both false as stated — every claim here is conditional on query shape and corpus.
