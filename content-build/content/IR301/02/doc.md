Retrieval produces a ranked list; the LLM consumes a prompt. Everything between those two artifacts is context assembly, and it is where otherwise-good pipelines quietly lose accuracy: duplicates burn the token budget, the best evidence lands where the model attends worst, un-filtered candidates leak the wrong tenant or the wrong year, and over-stuffed prompts bury the answer under noise. Assembly is also where citations are made possible or impossible, because the model can only attribute what you labeled.

## Selecting and Ordering Retrieved Context

The ranked list your fused retriever emits (for example BM25 and vector lists merged by reciprocal rank fusion, then reranked) is not yet a context. Selection decides which candidates enter the prompt; ordering decides where they sit.

Selection starts with **deduplication**. Overlapping chunks from the same document, or the same passage syndicated across documents, arrive as near-duplicates with near-identical scores. Detect them by source position (adjacent chunk IDs from one document), by high pairwise cosine similarity between chunk embeddings, or by shingled text overlap, and keep one representative, optionally merging adjacent chunks back into a contiguous span. Without this, a top-8 context can carry three copies of one paragraph and omit the second-best source entirely.

Selection also manages **diversity** when a query has multiple facets. Maximal marginal relevance (MMR) makes the tradeoff explicit: iteratively pick the candidate $d$ maximizing $\lambda \cdot \text{sim}(q, d) - (1 - \lambda)\max_{s \in S} \text{sim}(d, s)$, where $S$ is the already-selected set. The first term rewards relevance to the query; the second penalizes similarity to what you already chose. With $\lambda = 1$ you get pure relevance ranking; with $\lambda$ around 0.5 to 0.7 the second pick is pushed away from the first, so a question like "compare our refund policies for retail and wholesale" pulls one chunk from each policy instead of two from the more heavily indexed one.

Ordering matters because LLMs do not attend uniformly across long prompts. The "lost in the middle" result (Liu et al., 2023) showed that when the answer-bearing document is placed in the middle of a long context, accuracy drops substantially compared to placing it first or last; performance traces a U-shape across positions. Practical orderings that respect this: put the strongest evidence first (or split strongest-first and second-strongest-last), and keep chunks from the same document adjacent and in original document order so the model reads coherent sequences rather than shuffled fragments. Grouping by source also makes citation labeling cleaner. The judgment call: with small contexts (three to five short chunks) ordering barely matters and stable, deterministic layouts are worth more than micro-optimized positions; the effect grows with context length, so it is the 20-chunk assemblies that need edge placement.

## Citation-Grounded Extraction: Attributing Spans to Sources

A RAG answer without citations asks the user to trust a paraphrase. Citations turn the answer into a verifiable claim: this sentence came from that source. They also create the audit surface that lets you catch hallucination.

The baseline mechanism is **labeled context plus instructed markers**. At assembly time, give every chunk a stable identifier and render it into the prompt with that label, for example a block per chunk carrying `source_id: 12`, the document title, and the text. The system prompt instructs the model to append markers like `[12]` to each claim it derives from a source, and to answer only from the provided sources. Because you control assembly, you can map each `source_id` back to document, chunk, and character offsets when rendering the final answer with links.

Marker citation has a known weakness: models emit **plausible but wrong labels**, citing `[3]` for a claim that actually came from `[7]`, or citing a source that does not support the claim at all. Span-grounded extraction tightens this: require the model to quote the exact supporting span (or emit start and end offsets) alongside each claim, then **verify mechanically**. Exact string match against the labeled chunk is the strictest check; small tolerance for whitespace and punctuation via normalized or fuzzy matching handles harmless drift. A quote that fails to match its cited chunk is rejected or re-generated. This validation is cheap, deterministic, and catches fabricated quotes outright. Some model APIs now support citations natively, returning machine-verifiable references to spans of the supplied documents alongside the generated text, which removes the parse-and-verify step you would otherwise build yourself.

Note what quote verification does and does not prove: a matched span proves the text exists in the source, not that it supports the claim; full support-checking (does the span entail the sentence?) is an evaluation concern treated later in this course. Judgment layer: sentence-level citations are the useful granularity, since answer-level citations ("sources: 1, 3, 9") are unfalsifiable decoration, while word-level attribution is noise. Expect and handle the failure modes: uncited sentences (decide whether policy allows them, for example connective prose), citations of chunks that were retrieved but irrelevant, and the model refusing to answer when the sources genuinely lack the fact, which is the correct behavior and should be permitted by the prompt.

## Metadata Filters in Context Assembly

Every chunk should carry structured metadata alongside its text and vector: source document ID, tenant or client, author, document type, language, status, created and updated timestamps. Filters over this metadata are how assembly guarantees properties that similarity scores never can. Similarity says "this chunk resembles the query"; only a filter can say "this chunk is from the 2025 contract, for client Acme, in the published state." How a vector index executes filtered search efficiently is data-engineering territory covered elsewhere; here the concern is applying filters correctly.

Filters enter the pipeline from three places. **Explicit user constraints**: UI facets or query parameters (a date range picker, a project selector). **Inferred constraints**: an LLM or parser extracts filters from the query itself, mapping "last quarter's invoices from Acme" to `type = invoice AND client = acme AND date >= 2026-04-01`. **System defaults**: status = published, language = user's locale, and, in multi-tenant systems, the caller's tenant, applied unconditionally on every request rather than trusted to a prompt (the security consequences are treated in a later lesson).

The load-bearing mechanical distinction is **pre-filter versus post-filter**. Pre-filtering constrains the candidate set before or during index search, so the top-k you receive already satisfies the filter. Post-filtering retrieves top-k on similarity alone and then discards non-matching hits; if 8 of 10 hits fail the filter, you assemble context from 2 chunks, and the model answers from starvation. If you must post-filter, overfetch: estimate the pass rate $p$ and fetch about $k / p$ candidates to end up with $k$ survivors, at the cost of scoring and transferring the extras.

Inferred filters add a failure mode worth engineering around: the extractor hallucinates a constraint (a wrong year, a client name that does not exist) and the filtered search returns nothing at all, which reads to the user as "we have no such documents." The robust pattern is a **relaxation ladder**: run with all filters; if results are empty or sparse, drop inferred filters first (never the security-relevant defaults), retry, and surface which constraints were relaxed. Worked example: "What did we quote Initech for onboarding in 2024?" yields `client = initech AND type = quote AND year = 2024`; zero hits because the quote is dated January 2025; relaxing the inferred year finds it, and the answer notes the actual date. Judgment: filters multiply precision when metadata is trustworthy and complete, but a filter over a sparsely populated field silently excludes every unlabeled document, so audit metadata coverage before enforcing a field.

## Context Window Budgeting: Relevance Density over Volume

The prompt is a budget: system instructions, conversation history, the question, retrieved context, and a reserve for the answer all share one window. Assembly's job is to spend the context allocation for maximum answer quality, and the governing idea is **relevance density**: useful tokens divided by total tokens. A 6,000-token context in which 500 tokens matter is not "more evidence" than a 900-token context in which 500 tokens matter; it is the same evidence plus 5,100 tokens of distraction, cost, and latency.

Stuffing degrades quality through three mechanisms already visible in this lesson and the last: position effects mean the marginal chunk lands mid-context where attention is weakest; off-topic chunks act as **distractors** that the model may quote or blend into the answer precisely because you presented them as relevant; and every irrelevant token spends money and time, since input cost and prompt processing scale with prompt length. The empirical pattern to internalize is that answer quality as a function of appended chunks rises, plateaus, and then declines, while cost rises linearly forever. Filling the window because it is there pays the linear cost for the declining segment of the quality curve.

Budget arithmetic makes this concrete. Suppose an 8,000-token window with 600 tokens of system prompt, 400 of conversation history, 100 of question, and 1,500 reserved for the answer: the context allocation is $8000 - 600 - 400 - 100 - 1500 = 5400$ tokens, about 13 chunks at 400 tokens each. That 13 is a ceiling, not a target. The better policy is **dynamic k**: rank candidates, then admit chunks while they clear a relevance bar, stopping early when scores collapse. Reranker scores (unlike raw RRF rank positions, which carry no magnitude) support real thresholds: if candidate scores run 0.92, 0.88, 0.85, 0.41, 0.39, admit three chunks, not thirteen. Padding to a fixed k is exactly how the 0.41 distractor gets into the prompt.

When strong candidates genuinely exceed the budget, prefer increasing density over raising volume: deduplicate and merge overlapping spans (recovering tokens overlap spent), strip enrichment breadcrumbs already duplicated across sibling chunks, or compress long chunks extractively, keeping only the sentences that address the query. Compression trades an extra processing step and some risk of cutting needed context for a denser prompt; it earns its keep when chunks are long and diffusely relevant. Judgment layer: budget generously for multi-document synthesis questions, where breadth is the point, and stingily for factoid lookups, where one dense chunk beats ten; and when quality problems appear, check density before checking k, because the most common failure is not too little context but too much of the wrong kind.
