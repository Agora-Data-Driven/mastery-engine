**The big idea**: **Chunk Size and Overlap: The Core Tradeoffs**, **Structure-Aware and Semantic Chunking** and **Contextual Chunk Enrichment: Titles, Headers, Summaries** all ended at the moment a chunk entered the index. This lesson is what happens when those chunks come back out. Retrieval produces a ranked list; the LLM consumes a prompt. Everything between those two artifacts is context assembly, and it is where otherwise-good pipelines quietly lose accuracy: duplicates burn the token budget, the best evidence lands where the model attends worst, un-filtered candidates leak the wrong tenant or the wrong year, and over-stuffed prompts bury the answer under noise. Assembly is also where citations become possible or impossible, because the model can only attribute what you labeled.

**Key concepts**

- **A ranked list is not a context.** What your fused retriever emits — BM25 and vector lists merged by reciprocal rank fusion, then reranked — is raw material. **Selecting** decides which candidates enter the prompt; **ordering** decides where they sit.
- **Deduplication is the first selection step, and overlap is what makes it necessary.** Overlapping chunks from one document, or a passage syndicated across documents, arrive as near-duplicates with near-identical scores. Detect them by source position (adjacent chunk IDs), by high pairwise cosine between chunk embeddings, or by shingled text overlap; keep one representative, optionally merging neighbours back into a contiguous span. Without this a top-8 context can carry **three copies of one paragraph and omit the second-best source entirely**.
- **Maximal marginal relevance makes the diversity tradeoff explicit.** Iteratively pick the candidate $d$ maximizing $\lambda \cdot \text{sim}(q, d) - (1 - \lambda)\max_{s \in S} \text{sim}(d, s)$, where $S$ is what you have already selected. The first term rewards relevance; the second subtracts similarity to your current set, driven by the *closest* thing already chosen rather than the average. At $\lambda = 1$ the second term vanishes and you get pure relevance ranking; around 0.5 to 0.7 the second pick is pushed away from the first, so "compare our refund policies for retail and wholesale" pulls one chunk from each policy instead of two from the more heavily indexed one.
- **Ordering matters because LLMs do not attend uniformly across a long prompt.** The "lost in the middle" result (Liu et al., 2023) showed an answer-bearing document placed in the *middle* of a long context is recovered substantially less often than the same document placed first or last; accuracy traces a U-shape across positions. Practical orderings: strongest evidence first, or strongest first and second-strongest last; and keep same-document chunks adjacent and in original order, so the model reads coherent sequences rather than shuffled fragments. Grouping by source also makes citation labeling cleaner. **The effect scales with length** — at three to five short chunks a stable, deterministic layout is worth more, while 20-chunk assemblies need edge placement.
- **Citation-grounded extraction starts with labeled context plus instructed markers.** At assembly time give every chunk a stable identifier and render it into the prompt with that label — a block per chunk carrying `source_id: 12`, the title, and the text. The system prompt instructs the model to append markers like `[12]` to each claim and to answer only from the provided sources. Because you control assembly, each `source_id` maps back to document, chunk and character offsets when you render the answer with links.
- **Markers fail plausibly, which is the worst way to fail.** Models emit well-formed but wrong labels — citing `[3]` for a claim that came from `[7]`, or citing a source that does not support the claim at all. Nothing looks broken.
- **Attributing spans to sources is the fix: quote, then verify mechanically.** Require the exact supporting span (or start and end offsets) alongside each claim, then check it against the labeled chunk in code. Exact string match is strictest; normalized or fuzzy matching absorbs harmless whitespace and punctuation drift. A quote that fails to match its cited chunk is rejected or re-generated — cheap, deterministic, fatal to fabricated quotes. Some model APIs now return machine-verifiable citations natively.
- **Know exactly what verification proves.** A matched span proves the text exists in the source — not that it supports the claim. Support-checking (does the span entail the sentence?) is an evaluation concern later in this course. Sentence-level citations are the useful granularity: answer-level ones ("sources: 1, 3, 9") are unfalsifiable decoration and word-level attribution is noise. Expect uncited connective prose, citations of retrieved-but-irrelevant chunks, and refusal when the sources genuinely lack the fact — the last is correct, and your prompt must permit it.
- **Metadata filters assert what similarity never can.** Every chunk carries structured fields alongside its text and vector: source document ID, tenant or client, author, type, language, status, timestamps. Similarity says "this chunk resembles the query"; only a filter says "this chunk is from the 2025 contract, for client Acme, in the published state."
- **Filters enter assembly from three places.** *Explicit user constraints* — facets, a date-range picker. *Inferred constraints* — an LLM or parser reads them out of the query, mapping "last quarter's invoices from Acme" to `type = invoice AND client = acme AND date >= 2026-04-01`. *System defaults* — status = published, the user's locale, and in multi-tenant systems the caller's tenant, applied unconditionally on every request rather than trusted to a prompt.
- **Pre-filter versus post-filter is the load-bearing distinction.** Pre-filtering constrains the candidate set before or during index search, so the top-k you receive already satisfies the filter. Post-filtering retrieves top-k on similarity alone and then discards non-matching hits: if 8 of 10 hits fail you assemble context from 2 chunks and the model answers from starvation. If you must post-filter, overfetch — with pass rate $p$ the expected survivors from $k'$ candidates are $k'p$, so setting $k'p = k$ gives $k' = k/p$. Ten survivors at $p = 0.2$ means fetching about 50, and paying to score and transfer the extras.
- **Inferred filters need a relaxation ladder.** The extractor hallucinates a constraint — a wrong year, a client name that does not exist — the search returns nothing, and the user reads that as "we have no such documents." Run with all filters; if results are empty or sparse, drop *inferred* filters first (never the security-relevant defaults), retry, and surface which constraints were relaxed. Worked example: "What did we quote Initech for onboarding in 2024?" yields `client = initech AND type = quote AND year = 2024` and zero hits, because the quote is dated January 2025; relaxing the inferred year finds it, and the answer notes the real date.
- **Filters multiply precision only when the metadata is complete.** A filter over a sparsely populated field silently excludes every unlabeled document. Audit coverage first.
- **The prompt is a budget, and the arithmetic is simple.** System instructions, history, the question, retrieved context and an answer reserve share one window. With an 8,000-token window, 600 of system prompt, 400 of history, 100 of question and 1,500 reserved for the answer, the context allocation is $8000 - 600 - 400 - 100 - 1500 = 5400$ tokens — about 13 chunks at 400 tokens each. **That 13 is a ceiling, not a target.**
- **Relevance density is the governing idea: useful tokens divided by total tokens.** A 6,000-token context in which 500 tokens matter is not more evidence than a 900-token context in which the same 500 matter; it is the same evidence plus 5,100 tokens of distraction, cost and latency — density 8% against 56%.
- **Stuffing degrades quality through three mechanisms.** Position effects put the marginal chunk mid-context where attention is weakest. Off-topic chunks act as **distractors** the model may quote or blend in, precisely because you presented them as relevant. And every irrelevant token spends money and time. Answer quality against appended chunks rises, plateaus, then declines, while cost rises linearly forever.
- **Dynamic k beats fixed k.** Rank candidates, then admit chunks while they clear a relevance bar, stopping when scores collapse. Reranker scores support real thresholds because they carry magnitude, unlike raw RRF rank positions. With scores running 0.92, 0.88, 0.85, 0.41, 0.39 you admit three, not thirteen — padding to a fixed k is exactly how the 0.41 distractor gets in.
- **Over budget, raise density rather than volume.** Merge overlapping spans to recover the tokens overlap spent; strip enrichment breadcrumbs already duplicated across sibling chunks; or compress long chunks extractively to the sentences that address the query. Compression costs a step and risks cutting needed context; it earns its keep when chunks are long and diffusely relevant.

**Rules to remember**

- Deduplicate before you order, order before you budget. Each step assumes the previous one ran.
- MMR at $\lambda = 1$ is plain relevance ranking. The penalty uses the *maximum* similarity to the selected set.
- Strongest evidence goes at an edge. Same-document chunks stay adjacent and in document order.
- Ordering barely matters at 3–5 chunks, and matters at 20.
- A citation you cannot verify in code is decoration. Match quotes sentence by sentence; a match proves existence, not support.
- Pre-filter when the index allows it. If you post-filter, overfetch $k/p$.
- Security-relevant filters — tenant, ACL, status — are system defaults, never rungs on the relaxation ladder.
- The context allocation is window minus system, history, question and answer reserve — a ceiling.
- Density before k: the usual failure is too much of the wrong context, not too little of the right.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Treating the reranked list as the context.** Top-k straight into the prompt skips dedupe, diversity, ordering and budgeting in one move.
- **Padding to a fixed k because the window has room.** You buy the declining half of the quality curve at full cost.
- **Burying the best chunk in the middle** of a 20-chunk assembly, then blaming the retriever for a miss it did not cause.
- **Trusting markers.** `[3]` on a claim from `[7]` passes every syntactic check you have. And a *verified* quote is still not a verified claim: existence is not entailment.
- **Post-filtering without overfetching**, then reading a thin answer as a retrieval-quality problem rather than starvation.
- **Letting an inferred filter return zero and calling it an answer.** "No such documents" from a hallucinated year is a false negative you shipped.
- **Relaxing the tenant or status filter** when the ladder runs out of inferred constraints. A security incident, not a fallback.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Ask *when* something happens. Chunking and enrichment are index time; dedupe, filtering, ordering and budgeting are query time. Distractors move an operation across that line.
2. For selection questions, count the facets. One facet wants relevance ($\lambda$ high); "compare X and Y" wants diversity.
3. For ordering questions, count the chunks first. At 4 the answer is "it barely matters"; at 20 it is "put it at an edge."
4. For citation questions, separate three layers: markers (cheap, unverifiable), quoted spans (mechanically verifiable), entailment (an evaluation problem, not an assembly one).
5. For filter questions, identify the filter's *origin* first. Inferred filters may be relaxed; system defaults may not.
6. Convert post-filter scenarios into $k/p$ before comparing options, and check whether the candidate count already includes the overfetch.
7. For budget questions, compute the allocation by subtraction, then ask whether the option argues for volume or density. Density is almost always intended.
8. Watch for absolutes. "More context is better" and "always rerank to a fixed 10" are both false as stated.
