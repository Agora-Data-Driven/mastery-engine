1. From Chunks To Prompt

Chunk Size and Overlap ended the moment a chunk entered the index. Here is the bill for that overlap, arriving at the other end: adjacent chunks score alike, so the ranked list hands you the same paragraph three times. Drag the overlap you chose, then deduplicate. Like packing a suitcase from a shopping list where four lines are the same jumper.

Detection has three usable signals and they catch different things: adjacent chunk IDs from one document (cheap, exact, and the only one that fires at zero overlap), high pairwise cosine between chunk embeddings (catches the same passage syndicated across different documents), and shingled text overlap (catches near-verbatim boilerplate). Keeping one representative is the minimum; merging the neighbours back into one contiguous span is better, because it also recovers the tokens the overlap spent.

2. Select And Order

Two decisions, in this order. Selecting runs maximal marginal relevance — drag lambda and watch the second pick jump facets. Ordering then decides where the winners sit, and attention across a long prompt is U-shaped. Like seating guests: who gets invited, then who sits next to the host — and a long table has a dead middle.

Grouping by document is not a mistake — it keeps a source's chunks readable in their original order and makes citation labeling cleaner. It becomes one only when the group ordering is arbitrary and drops the strongest evidence into the dead zone. The reconciling move is to group by document and order the groups by their best member's score.

3. Verify The Quote

The model returns six sentences with markers and quoted spans. Switch the verifier and watch which sentences survive — a marker is a claim about a claim, a quote is a string your code can check. Tap any row for the detail. Like a receipt: "I spent it on supplies" versus the line item you can hold up against the statement.

Sentence-level is the useful granularity. An answer-level footer ("sources: 3, 7, 12") is unfalsifiable decoration — no mechanical check can be written against it — and word-level attribution is noise. And note the one behaviour that looks like a failure and is not: a model that refuses because the assembled sources genuinely lack the fact is correct, and your prompt has to permit it or you have instructed it to fabricate.

4. Pre-Filter Or Post

Metadata filters assert what similarity never can — this tenant, this year, this status. Where the filter runs decides whether you get k chunks or two. Drag the pass rate, then walk the relaxation ladder. Like a bouncer at the door versus one inside: same guest list, very different room at the end of the night.

Filters multiply precision when the metadata is trustworthy and complete. A filter over a sparsely populated field silently excludes every unlabeled document — the query looks like it found nothing when it in fact refused to look. Audit coverage on a field before you enforce it, and note that the filter's origin is what licenses relaxation: inferred constraints may be dropped, system defaults such as tenant, ACL and status may never be.

5. Density Over Volume

The window is a budget and the ceiling is not a target. Drag the number of chunks you admit and watch three things move in different directions: relevance density, answer quality, and cost. Like a suitcase with a weight limit — the last five things you cram in are why you cannot find your passport.

When strong candidates genuinely exceed the budget, raise density rather than volume: deduplicate and merge overlapping spans (visual 1 recovered those tokens), strip enrichment breadcrumbs already repeated across sibling chunks, or compress long chunks extractively down to the sentences that address the query. Budget generously for multi-document synthesis, where breadth is the point, and stingily for factoid lookups, where one dense chunk beats ten.
