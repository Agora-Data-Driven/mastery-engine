1. Filter Or Boundary

Metadata Filters in Context Assembly used filters to improve relevance, and Faithfulness measured whether the answer stayed inside the context you assembled. Same machinery, one new consequence: the context is the ceiling of what an answer can leak. Choose where the permission check happens. Like a bouncer: at the door, or reading names off the guest list after everyone is already inside.

Hiding the citation in the UI does not undo anything: once unauthorized text is in the context, the breach has already happened. That is why retrieval — not the generator, and not the renderer — is the enforcement point for ACL-Aware Retrieval: Document-Level Permissions.

2. Post-Filter Starvation

Post-filtering has an arithmetic, and it is brutal. Expected eligible yield is n × p: fetch size times the fraction of the corpus this user may read. Drag both and watch a rerank depth of 50 survive or collapse. Like panning for gold: the less gold in the river, the more river you have to move.

The derivation is the thing to carry into the exam: each candidate is eligible with probability p, so expected yield is np; holding rerank depth k needs np ≥ k, hence n ≥ k/p. Cost scales as 1/p — halve someone's eligibility and you double their fetch. Pre-filtering makes the whole curve flat.

3. The Isolation Ladder

Pick a rung of tenant isolation, then switch the non-filter controls on and off. A perfect tenant filter closes exactly one of five leak channels — the other four are cache, embeddings, logs and fine-tuning. Like flat walls in a shared building: the front door being locked says nothing about the ventilation, the post room or the shared bins.

The judgment for a growing SaaS: namespaces inside one index, tenant context injected by the gateway, the response layer re-verifying every returned document, and per-tenant indexes reserved for the largest or most regulated customers. Rehearse off-boarding before you need it — and make tenant_id a first-class field on day one, because retrofitting it into a shared index is a full rebuild with an incident attached.

4. The Poisoned Ticket

Step through a real indirect injection, stage by stage, and check each stage for a malfunction. You will not find one — which is the point. Like a courier who delivers a parcel bomb perfectly: correct address, correct time, no error anywhere in the chain.

Retrieval is what makes the attack precise: an attacker who understands the vertical plants content engineered to rank for target queries — an SEO attack on your index — so the payload arrives at the moment of maximum relevance. The other shapes to recognise: exfiltration through rendered links or images, tool abuse (send_email), persistence across sessions, and citation laundering.

5. Blast Radius Control

Assume the injection succeeds — because as of early 2026 no complete defense exists. Switch the five layers on and off, and watch what a success is able to do. The goal is not prevention; it is turning a breach into a wrong answer. Like a ship's bulkheads: they do not stop the hole, they stop the hole from sinking the ship.

Red-team per source tier — curated docs, user-generated tickets and external web content each deserve their own payloads and their own trust tier. Plant injection payloads in the golden set so every release re-proves the defenses, monitor outputs for exfiltration shapes (unexpected URLs, odd query strings, tool calls nobody requested), and treat any detection as a security incident with a playbook, not a quality bug to backlog.
