A RAG answer is a chain of model calls, and the user experiences the sum of all of them. Latency and cost engineering is the discipline of budgeting that sum stage by stage, caching what legitimately repeats, and choosing deliberately between bigger contexts and better retrieval. None of this is guesswork: every stage has a measured distribution, every cache has an invalidation contract, and the long-context question has a definite cost shape.

## The RAG Latency Budget: Retrieve, Rerank, Generate

End-to-end latency decomposes into an optional rewrite, retrieval, reranking, context assembly, and generation, which itself splits into time-to-first-token and decode time. Streaming changes what users perceive: decode time is hidden behind progressive rendering, but everything before the first token is dead time the user spends waiting. The pre-generation block is therefore the latency frontier that engineering actually controls.

A representative budget for an eight-second target with streaming: rewrite 0.2 s or skipped, parallel retrieval legs 0.3 to 0.5 s since BM25 and the ANN service run concurrently, reranking 0.5 to 1.5 s depending on depth as IR 202 established, assembly effectively free, time-to-first-token 0.5 to 1.5 s, and decode two to five seconds depending on answer length. The pre-generation block must fit in roughly two seconds or the product feels stalled no matter how fast tokens then stream.

Allocations are derived backward from the objective, exactly as the funnel budgets were. Measure each stage's p50 and p95 in production, because benchmark numbers lie about queueing. A subtlety: the chain's p95 is not the sum of stage p95s, since stages do not all hit their worst case on the same request, but budgeting with the sum of p95s is the safe approximation under correlated load. A measured example from a healthy system:

| Stage | p50 | p95 | Budget |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Rewrite | 120 ms | 280 ms | 300 ms |
| Parallel retrieval | 180 ms | 450 ms | 500 ms |
| Rerank 50 | 380 ms | 900 ms | 1,000 ms |
| Time to first token | 600 ms | 1,400 ms | 1,500 ms |
| Decode (streamed) | 2.8 s | 5.5 s | user-tolerated |

The p95 column sums to about 3.7 s before streaming begins, which is the figure the SLO must absorb. Parallelize what the dependency graph allows: retrieval legs concurrently, and the raw-query embedding can run alongside the rewrite as a hedge, so a slow rewriter never blocks retrieval entirely.

Decode time dominates long answers and is pure multiplication: output tokens times per-token latency. Four hundred tokens at 15 ms per token is six seconds. Controls are output caps, prompts that demand concision, streaming, and routing simple query classes to a smaller, faster model. Every stage also needs a deadline with an evaluated fallback, the pattern from IR 202: reranker timeout serves the fused order, model timeout retries once then degrades gracefully, and queues are bounded so overload sheds load rather than compounding it.

The cost ledger runs in parallel. The dominant variable cost is input tokens: ten chunks of 500 tokens is 5,000 input tokens per query, and at 100,000 queries a day that is 500 million input tokens daily, a number worth pricing explicitly. Add reranker accelerator-seconds and output tokens, and track cost per answered query as a first-class metric next to p95. Quality work that doubles input tokens is a pricing decision, not just a relevance one. Two levers move it: context assembly discipline, sending eight focused chunks instead of fifteen loose ones is a direct input-token cut, and query-class routing, since a third of traffic is usually simple enough for a smaller, cheaper generator with no measurable quality loss on that class. Both levers are invisible to users when the evaluation layer from the earlier lesson verifies the savings did not come out of answer quality.

## Caching Layers: Embeddings, Results, Answers

Three caches sit at different points of the chain, with different savings and different liabilities. The embedding cache maps normalized query text to its vector, saving tens of milliseconds and one model call; it pays when queries repeat verbatim, as navigational and FAQ traffic does, and normalization, lowercasing and whitespace collapsing, is what makes repetitions actually collide. The retrieval result cache maps query plus filters plus ACL context to the fused or reranked candidate list, saving the entire expensive pre-generation block; this is usually the highest-value layer, because it erases the two slowest pre-generation stages at once. The answer cache maps query plus context hash to the final answer, saving everything, and it is correspondingly the most dangerous, valid only for non-personal, non-time-sensitive answers.

Every cache key must carry the versions that can invalidate it. Embedding keys include the model version, since vectors from different models are incompatible. Result keys include the index generation, or a re-index silently serves stale candidates, and the tenant plus ACL hash, or the cache becomes the cross-tenant leak from the security lesson. Answer keys include corpus generation and prompt version, with a time-to-live as backstop. Some things are never cached: personalized answers, answers derived from revoked documents, and time-dependent facts like status pages.

Semantic caching keys by embedding similarity rather than exact text, answering near-duplicate queries from cache. Hit rates jump, but the cache now serves answers to questions that are merely similar, and similar is not equal when a user asks about the Team plan and gets the Enterprise answer because the embeddings were close. Treat it as experimental: high similarity thresholds, low-stakes query classes only, and the score logged on every hit, with a kill switch that reverts to exact-match keys the day the threshold proves loose.

The economics justify the discipline. A result cache at a 35 percent hit rate removes the pre-generation block from a third of traffic; an answer cache at 20 percent removes the whole chain from a fifth. Put numbers on it: at 100,000 queries a day, a 35 percent result-cache hit rate eliminates roughly 35,000 retrieval-plus-rerank executions, and a 20 percent answer-cache rate eliminates 20,000 full generations, savings that show up in both the daily bill and the p95, since cache hits skip the slowest stages entirely. Caching is the cheapest capacity available. But each layer is a consistency liability, so hit rate and staleness complaints are tracked together: a rising hit rate alongside rising stale-answer reports is a failure mode, not a success.

## Long Context vs Retrieval: Cost and Quality Tradeoffs

Million-token context windows invite an architectural shortcut: skip retrieval and stuff the corpus into the prompt. The comparison deserves real numbers rather than vibes. On variable cost, a long-context call pays input price for the entire stuffed slice on every query: 100,000 tokens of context at 100,000 queries a day is ten billion input tokens daily, while the retrieval path sends perhaps 5,000 focused tokens, a twenty-fold difference that scales with corpus size. Retrieval pays the corpus cost once, at ingest, through embedding and indexing; long context pays it per query, forever. For any corpus much larger than the window and any non-trivial query rate, retrieval wins the cost argument by orders of magnitude.

Latency tells the same story. Prefilling a hundred thousand tokens takes seconds before the first token appears, which reclassifies long-context calls as batch jobs, not interactive ones. The retrieval path reaches first token in about a second. Even where providers offer accelerated long-context processing, the prefill grows with the slice while the retrieval path's cost is essentially flat in corpus size, so the gap widens as the corpus grows rather than shrinking with better hardware.

Quality is subtler and worth measuring rather than assuming. The lost-in-the-middle effect from the evaluation lesson means models underuse the middle of long contexts, and needle tests consistently show recall of a specific fact degrading as context grows. Retrieval's focused context is an attention-precision optimization: fewer, better chunks beat more, noisier ones. But long context genuinely wins for whole-corpus reasoning, cross-document synthesis like `summarize the themes across these thirty contracts`, for corpora small enough to fit entirely, and for eliminating retrieval's failure modes, chunking damage and the recall ceiling, by simply not having them.

Freshness tilts further: a stuffed context is a snapshot, and re-stuffing on every corpus change is a recurring cost, while retrieval reads the live index. Context or prefix caching features, which reuse processed context across calls at reduced cost, blur this line for repeated large contexts, but they do not change the fundamental arithmetic for dynamic corpora.

The decision framework has five questions: How large is the corpus relative to the window? Are queries point lookups or corpus synthesis? What is the query rate times per-query context cost? How fresh must answers be? And what do needle tests and the golden set say on your data, not the vendor's? A concrete hybrid shows the shape the answers usually imply. Interactive support Q&A over a two-million-chunk corpus runs the retrieval path, with focused five-thousand-token contexts at interactive latency. A nightly compliance job that must reason across every contract a customer signed runs a long-context batch pass over that customer's slice, where seconds of prefill are irrelevant and no chunking boundary can hide a clause. An agent preparing a monthly briefing uses retrieval to assemble a task-sized dossier, then a single long-context call to synthesize it. Each workload got the tool its cost, latency, and quality numbers pointed to. The closing judgment: retrieval was never a workaround for small windows. It is a cost, latency, and attention-precision optimization whose logic survives infinite windows, because feeding the model everything means paying to attend to mostly noise. Bigger windows move the break-even point; they do not repeal it.
