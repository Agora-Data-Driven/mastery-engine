**The big idea**: This is the first lesson of the course, so the ground it builds on is your own: recall from **linear algebra** that $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has exactly one solution, no solution, or infinitely many, and that which case you land in is decided by rank rather than by arithmetic luck; recall from **regression** that fitting $y = mx + b$ is estimating a rate of change and an intercept; recall from **calculus** that $|x - c| < \delta$ is the neighbourhood you have written a hundred times. This lesson is that same machinery in one variable, done fast and by hand. Three ideas carry all of it. First, solving a linear equation is a chain of *equivalence-preserving* operations that terminates in one of three places: `x = number`, the identity `0 = 0`, or a contradiction. Second, absolute value is a distance, and a distance is nonnegative, which is exactly why an absolute value equation splits into two ordinary ones. Third, inequalities obey every rule equations do except one: multiplying or dividing by a negative reverses the direction of the sign.

**Key concepts**

- **Equations with parentheses, decimals, and fractions are all the same equation wearing different clothes — strip the clothes first.** The distributive law $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ applies to *every* term inside the parenthesis. Solve $3(x - 2) = 9$: distribute to get $3x - 6 = 9$, add 6 to get $3x = 15$, divide to get $x = 5$. Check: $3(5-2) = 9$. ✓ Distributing to only the first term gives the wrong equation $3x - 2 = 9$ and the wrong answer $x = 11/3$ — that is the single most common error in the whole topic. (Here you could also divide both sides by 3 first: $x - 2 = 3$, so $x = 5$. Both routes are legal because both operations preserve equivalence.)
- **Fractions: multiply *both sides* by the least common denominator.** Solve $x/3 + 2 = 5$: multiply through by 3 to get $x + 6 = 15$, so $x = 9$. Check: $9/3 + 2 = 5$. ✓ With two denominators the LCD earns its keep: $\frac{x}{2} + \frac{x}{3} = 5$ has LCD 6, and multiplying gives $3x + 2x = 30$, so $x = 6$. The LCD is the least common *multiple* of the denominators — for 2 and 3 it is 6, never $2 + 3 = 5$.
- **Decimals: the same equation, rescaled.** Solve $0.5x + 1.5 = 4.5$: subtract 1.5 to get $0.5x = 3$, divide by 0.5 to get $x = 6$. Or multiply everything by 10 first — $5x + 15 = 45$, so $x = 6$ — which is the move you already trust in Python, where `0.1 + 0.2 != 0.3` and integer arithmetic is the exact one.
- **The number of solutions to equations is a trichotomy, and it is the rank argument in one dimension.** Every linear equation in one variable reduces to $ax = b$. If $a \neq 0$ there is exactly one solution, $x = b/a$. If $a = 0$ and $b = 0$ you are left with the true statement $0 = 0$, satisfied by every real number: **infinitely many** solutions, an identity. If $a = 0$ and $b \neq 0$ you are left with a false statement such as $5 = -1$: **no solution**. So a linear equation in one variable has exactly one solution *unless it simplifies otherwise* — degeneracy requires the variable terms to cancel completely, which requires identical coefficients on the two sides.
- **Worked both ways.** $4x + 1 = 4x + 5$: subtract $4x$ and the variable vanishes, leaving $1 = 5$, false — no solution. The two sides are parallel processes that never meet. $2(x + 3) = 2x + 6$: distribute to get $2x + 6 = 2x + 6$, subtract, and you get $0 = 0$ — infinitely many solutions, because the two sides were never actually different expressions. Contrast $2x + 3 = 11$, which reduces to $2x = 8$: coefficient 2 is nonzero, so exactly one solution, $x = 4$.
- **Modeling with linear equations: name the quantities before writing anything down.** In $y = mx + b$, the coefficient $m$ is the **rate of change** of $y$ per unit of $x$, and $b$ is the **starting amount**, the value of $y$ when $x = 0$ — the y-intercept. Evaluating is just substitution: $y = 3x - 5$ at $x = 4$ gives $12 - 5 = 7$. A taxi charging a \$4 starting fee plus \$2 per mile is $C = 2m + 4$: the per-unit charge multiplies the variable, the flat fee stands alone. Writing $C = 4m + 2$ instead models a completely different business — \$4 per mile with a \$2 fee. A line with slope $-2$ through $(0, 6)$ has $b = 6$ (because that point already has $x = 0$), so $y = -2x + 6$. This is a one-feature regression with coefficient $m$ and bias $b$, and the units check is the one you run on any feature column: dollars-per-mile times miles gives dollars, so it can be added to a dollar fee.
- **Absolute value equations split because absolute value measures nonnegative distance.** $|u| = d$ asks "which $u$ sit $d$ away from zero", so for $d > 0$ it is equivalent to $u = d$ **or** $u = -d$. Hence $|x| = 5$ gives $x = 5$ or $x = -5$. Shift the centre and it reads as distance from a point: $|x - 2| = 3$ means "$x$ is 3 away from 2", so $x = 5$ or $x = -1$. With a coefficient, isolate the bars and then split: $|2x + 1| = 7$ gives $2x + 1 = 7 \Rightarrow x = 3$, and $2x + 1 = -7 \Rightarrow 2x = -8 \Rightarrow x = -4$. Two branches, two solutions. The edge cases follow from nonnegativity: $|u| = 0$ has exactly one solution, and $|u| = -3$ has none.
- **Multi-step and compound inequalities: identical steps, one new rule.** Solve $3x - 4 \geq 8$ exactly as you would an equation — add 4 to get $3x \geq 12$, divide by 3 to get $x \geq 4$. But multiplying or dividing both sides by a **negative** number reverses the sign: from $-2x > 6$ you get $x < -3$. The reason is visible on the number line: multiplying by $-1$ reflects through zero, and reflection reverses order. Sanity check with numbers — $3 < 5$, yet $-3 > -5$.
- **"And" is intersection; "or" is union.** The compact chain $2 < x + 1 \leq 6$ is an *and* statement: subtract 1 from all three parts to get $1 < x \leq 5$, the single interval $(1, 5]$. A conjunction always yields the intersection of the two solution sets, and if they do not overlap the answer is the empty set. A disjunction such as $x < -2$ **or** $x \geq 3$ cannot be compressed into one three-part chain at all — its solution set is a **union of two intervals**, $(-\infty, -2) \cup [3, \infty)$, with a gap in the middle. Bracket notation carries the strictness: `<` and `>` give open ends, `≤` and `≥` give closed ones.

**Rules to remember**

- Distribute to every term inside the parenthesis, never just the first.
- Clear fractions by multiplying **both sides** by the LCD; clear decimals by multiplying by a power of 10.
- Every linear equation in one variable becomes $ax = b$: one solution if $a \neq 0$; infinitely many if $a = 0, b = 0$; none if $a = 0, b \neq 0$.
- `0 = 0` (or any true statement) means infinitely many solutions. `5 = -1` (or any false one) means no solution.
- In $y = mx + b$: $m$ is the rate of change, $b$ is the starting amount at $x = 0$.
- $|u| = d$ with $d > 0$ splits into $u = d$ or $u = -d$. $d = 0$ gives one solution; $d < 0$ gives none.
- Reverse the inequality sign when you multiply or divide by a negative — and only then.
- "And" ⇒ intersection ⇒ one interval (or empty). "Or" ⇒ union of two intervals.
- Substitute the answer back into the *original* equation. It costs seconds and catches every arithmetic slip.

**Common pitfalls**

- Distributing $3(x - 2)$ as $3x - 2$. Also forgetting that a leading minus distributes: $-(x - 4) = -x + 4$.
- Adding the denominators instead of taking their least common multiple, or multiplying only the fractional terms by the LCD and leaving the integer terms untouched.
- Reading "no solution" as "$x = 0$". Those are opposites: $x = 0$ is a solution, and no solution means the solution set is empty.
- Reporting only the positive branch of an absolute value equation. $|x - 2| = 3$ has two answers, and the wrong pair $\{1, 6\}$ comes from adding and subtracting 3 in the wrong direction — solve each branch instead of guessing.
- Splitting an absolute value equation before the bars are isolated. In $2|x| + 1 = 9$ you must reach $|x| = 4$ first.
- Forgetting to flip the inequality after dividing by a negative — the one rule with no equation counterpart, and the one distractors are built on.
- Applying an operation to only two parts of a three-part inequality. Every operation touches all three.
- Writing an "or" set as a single interval. Two disjoint pieces need a union, not a chain.

**How to approach the questions**

1. If parentheses, fractions, or decimals appear, clean them up before doing anything else, then re-read what is left. Most of these questions are one honest simplification away from trivial.
2. When a question asks *how many* solutions, do not solve — push all variable terms to one side and look at what survives. A vanished variable plus a true statement means infinitely many; a vanished variable plus a false one means none.
3. For modeling questions, identify which number is the per-unit rate and which is the fixed starting amount, then check that the coefficient multiplies the variable. Distractors are almost always the two swapped.
4. For absolute value, split into two branches immediately, solve both, and state both. If the right-hand side is negative, answer "no real solution" without solving.
5. For inequalities, track every multiplication or division by a negative and flip there. Then translate the result into set language: is this one interval or two?
6. Test a candidate answer with a number. Picking $x = 4$ and evaluating both sides settles most multiple-choice items faster than a full derivation.
