1. Three Possible Fates

You already know the trichotomy: a linear system A x = b has one solution, none, or infinitely many, decided by rank. One variable is the same theorem with n = 1. Drag the four coefficients and watch which fate you land in. Like two walkers on a road: different speeds and they meet once, same speed and they either never meet or were always together.

Notice what has to happen for either degenerate case: the x-terms must cancel exactly, which needs identical coefficients on the two sides. That is why a linear equation in one variable has exactly one solution unless it simplifies otherwise — one solution is the generic case, and the other two are the measure-zero coincidences.

2. Clear The Clutter

Parentheses, fractions and decimals are the same linear equation in three costumes. One cleaning move each, then it is a x = b again. Pick a costume and drag the number in it. Like tidying a desk before you start work — the job never changes, only how long you spend finding the pen.

The cleaning move is always multiplication by something that is not zero, applied to every term on both sides — that is what keeps the equation equivalent rather than merely similar. Multiply only the fractional terms by the LCD, or only the left side, and you have quietly changed which equation you are solving.

3. The Taxi Meter

A model is two numbers with jobs: one is charged per unit, one is charged once. Drag the fee and the rate, then press the swap button to see the distractor every modeling question is built on. Like a phone plan: the monthly line rental and the price per gigabyte are not interchangeable.

The units are the whole argument. Dollars-per-mile × miles = dollars, which can then be added to a dollar fee; dollars × miles is not a thing anyone charges. Run that check on any model you write and the swapped distractor becomes impossible to pick by accident.

4. Distance From A Point

|x − c| = d asks one question: which points sit exactly d away from c? Drag the centre and the distance and count the answers — two, then one, then none. Like asking who lives 3 blocks from the station: there is someone up the road and someone down it.

Isolate the bars before splitting. In 2|x| + 1 = 9 you must reach |x| = 4 first; splitting the unisolated form gives two equations that are not equivalent to the original. And once split, substitute both answers back — the check is what turns a remembered rule into a verified one.

5. And Versus Or

Two things to hold at once: dividing by a negative reverses the sign, and a compound inequality is a set operation — "and" intersects, "or" unites. Drag the coefficient through zero, then switch the connector. Like a job filter: "senior AND remote" narrows the list, "senior OR remote" widens it.

Notice which rules did not change: adding, subtracting and multiplying by a positive all behave exactly as they do for equations. Only the negative multiplier is new, and it is new because reflecting the number line through zero reverses order — 3 < 5 but −3 > −5. Everything else you already knew.
