**The big idea**: **Modeling with linear equations** had you writing $y = mx + b$ and reading $m$ as a rate of change and $b$ as a starting amount; **Number of solutions to equations** had you reduce everything to $ax = b$; and **Equations with parentheses, decimals, and fractions**, **Absolute value equations** and **Multi-step and compound inequalities** all drilled one move — rearrange without changing the solution set. This lesson puts those objects on axes. Solving produced one number; graphing shows the whole solution set at once. Three things follow. **Intercepts and slope** are geometry rather than new algebra: an intercept is what you get by setting the *other* variable to zero, and slope is a ratio of differences. **Slope-intercept form, point-slope form, and standard form** are three encodings of one line, chosen by what you are given and what you want to read off. And **parallel and perpendicular lines** are conditions on slope alone — everything else in the equations is decoration.

**Key concepts**

- **An intercept is where a graph crosses an axis, and you find it by setting the other coordinate to zero.** The x-axis is the set of points with $y = 0$, so the **x-intercept** comes from putting $y = 0$; the y-axis is $x = 0$, so the **y-intercept** comes from putting $x = 0$. Worked: for $y = 3x - 2$, setting $x = 0$ gives $y = -2$, so the y-intercept is the **point** $(0, -2)$ — not the number $-2$, and emphatically not $(-2, 0)$. Setting $y = 0$ gives $3x - 2 = 0$, so $x = 2/3$ and the x-intercept is $(2/3, 0)$. That second step is the one-variable equation from last lesson: graphing consumes that skill rather than replacing it.
- **Slope is a ratio of differences — rise over run — and both differences must be taken in the same order.** For two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$, $m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$. Worked: through $(1, 2)$ and $(5, 10)$, $m = \dfrac{10 - 2}{5 - 1} = \dfrac{8}{4} = 2$. Reverse the points and both signs flip together, $(2-10)/(1-5) = 2$ again — the formula does not care which point you call first, only that you are consistent. Break that and you get $-2$; add the coordinates instead of subtracting and you get $12/6$, right here by coincidence and nonsense elsewhere; invert the fraction and you get $1/2$. Those three are the distractors. The mechanism: slope answers "how much does $y$ change per unit change in $x$", so $y$-differences go on top, "per unit" means divide, and the answer is the same $m$ you used as a rate.
- **Zero slope and undefined slope are opposite failures, one in each part of the fraction.** A **horizontal** line has $y_2 = y_1$, so the numerator is zero: through $(1, 4)$ and $(7, 4)$, $m = 0/6 = 0$, and the line $y = 4$ fits $y = mx + b$ perfectly with $m = 0$. A **vertical** line has $x_2 = x_1$, so the denominator is zero and the slope is **undefined** — not zero, not infinity: through $(3, 1)$ and $(3, 9)$ the line is $x = 3$, and it cannot be written as $y = mx + b$ at all.
- **Slope-intercept form $y = mx + b$ displays the slope and the y-intercept with zero rearrangement.** That is its entire job, and why it answers any question about which form shows those two most directly. Worked: $y = 3x - 2$ has $m = 3$ and y-intercept $(0, -2)$ on sight. It is also the graphing form: plot $(0, b)$, then step the slope — on $y = -\frac{2}{3}x + 4$, from $(0, 4)$ run $3$ right and rise $-2$ to $(3, 2)$.
- **Point-slope form $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$ is the slope formula with the denominator cleared, so writing a line costs no algebra at all.** Take $m = (y - y_1)/(x - x_1)$ for a general point $(x, y)$ on the line and clear the denominator. Worked: slope $3$ through $(2, -1)$ gives $y - (-1) = 3(x - 2)$, that is $y + 1 = 3(x - 2)$. Both signs are traps, because the form **subtracts** the coordinates: a negative $y_1$ becomes a plus, while $x_1 = 2$ stays a minus. The same digits rearranged into $y - 2 = 3(x + 1)$ describe the line through $(-1, 2)$ instead. Recognising the form is pattern-matching on shape: (y minus a number) $=$ (a number)(x minus a number), as in $y - 4 = 2(x - 1)$.
- **Converting point-slope to slope-intercept is one distribution and one addition.** Worked: $y - 3 = -2(x + 1)$ distributes to $y - 3 = -2x - 2$, and adding $3$ gives $y = -2x + 1$. Change one digit and the answer moves: $y - 4 = -2(x + 1)$ becomes $y - 4 = -2x - 2$, then $y = -2x + 2$. That is the parenthesis rule from the previous lesson, and the leading minus must reach **both** terms inside the bracket — where nearly every wrong option in this topic is manufactured.
- **Standard form $Ax + By = C$ keeps both variables on one side with integer coefficients, conventionally with $A > 0$.** From slope-intercept, move the $x$-term across: $y = -4x + 5$ becomes $4x + y = 5$, and adding $4x$ delivers $A > 0$ for free. Coming back means solving for $y$ and dividing **every** term. Worked: $2x + 3y = 12$ gives $3y = -2x + 12$, so $y = -\frac{2}{3}x + 4$. Again: $3x + 2y = 8$ gives $2y = -3x + 8$, so $y = -\frac{3}{2}x + 4$ — the constant is $8/2 = 4$, and leaving it as $8$ is the manufactured distractor. In general $m = -A/B$ and the y-intercept is $(0, C/B)$ when $B \neq 0$; $B = 0$ gives the vertical line $x = C/A$, the case slope-intercept form cannot express.
- **The three forms describe the same line, so choosing one is choosing what you want to read.** Slope-intercept for the slope and y-intercept, and for graphing. Point-slope when handed a point and a slope, because it costs nothing. Standard form for integer coefficients, fast intercepts, and vertical lines. Converting is pure rearrangement: the solution set never changes.
- **Two distinct non-vertical lines are parallel exactly when their slopes are equal and their y-intercepts differ.** Equal slopes mean they rise at the same rate and never close the gap; different intercepts mean the gap was never zero. Worked: parallel to $y = 3x + 1$ is $y = 3x - 5$. Both conditions carry weight — $y = 3x + 1$ shares its own slope *and* its own intercept, so it is the **same line**, not a parallel one. The near-misses are the other things you could do to a $3$: negate it ($y = -3x + 1$), invert it ($y = \frac{1}{3}x + 2$), or both. Only leaving it alone gives parallel.
- **Two lines are perpendicular exactly when their slopes are negative reciprocals: $m_2 = -1/m_1$, equivalently $m_1 m_2 = -1$.** Derive it rather than memorise it: a line of slope $m$ has direction vector $(1, m)$, and recall from linear algebra that perpendicular means a zero dot product, so $(1, m_1) \cdot (1, m_2) = 1 + m_1 m_2 = 0$. Worked: perpendicular to $y = -4x + 7$ needs slope $-1/(-4) = 1/4$, so $y = \frac{1}{4}x - 2$ qualifies — both changes happened, the sign flip and the reciprocal. Perpendicular to slope $2$ is slope $-\frac{1}{2}$. Half the transformation gives $-\frac{1}{4}$ or $4$, the two standard distractors, and the product test exposes both.
- **Vertical and horizontal lines are the edge cases of both conditions.** Two distinct vertical lines — $x = 2$ and $x = 5$ — both have undefined slope and never meet, so they are always **parallel**. A vertical and a horizontal line are perpendicular — the one such pair the product rule cannot express, because one slope does not exist. Settle those geometrically.

**Rules to remember**

- x-intercept: set $y = 0$. y-intercept: set $x = 0$. Both answers are points.
- Slope is $\Delta y / \Delta x$ — differences, taken in the same order, with $y$ on top.
- Horizontal line: $m = 0$. Vertical line: $m$ undefined. Not the same thing.
- $y = mx + b$ shows slope and y-intercept directly; $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$ needs a point and a slope; $Ax + By = C$ is standard form.
- From standard form, $m = -A/B$ and the y-intercept is $C/B$ when $B \neq 0$; $B = 0$ is the vertical line $x = C/A$. Divide every term, the constant included.
- Parallel: equal slopes, different intercepts. Equal slopes *and* equal intercepts is one line, not two.
- Perpendicular: $m_1 m_2 = -1$. Negate **and** invert.
- Two distinct vertical lines are parallel; a vertical and a horizontal line are perpendicular.

**Common pitfalls**

- Swapping the intercepts — answering $(-2, 0)$ for the y-intercept of $y = 3x - 2$ — or giving a number where a point is asked for.
- Writing the slope upside down, or as a quotient of sums. A sum of coordinates is never a slope.
- Calling a vertical line's slope zero, or a horizontal line's slope undefined.
- Leaving the second term of a bracket untouched, so $-2(x + 1)$ becomes $-2x + 1$ instead of $-2x - 2$.
- Dropping the division on the constant, turning $3x + 2y = 8$ into $y = -\frac{3}{2}x + 8$.
- Reading the slope's sign straight off standard form: $2x + 3y = 12$ has a **negative** slope, $-2/3$, though every printed coefficient is positive.
- Point-slope signs backwards, so slope $3$ through $(2, -1)$ comes out as $y - 2 = 3(x + 1)$.
- Accepting "same slope" as parallel without checking the two lines are distinct.
- Doing half the perpendicular transformation, or forcing $m_1 m_2 = -1$ onto a vertical line whose slope does not exist to be multiplied.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For an intercept question, ask which variable is zero, substitute, and solve. Write the answer as an ordered pair and check the zero sits in the correct slot.
2. For slope from two points, label $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ before any arithmetic, then compute the two differences separately. Sanity-check the sign: rising to the right is positive.
3. For "which form is this?", match the shape and ignore the values. An isolated $y$ on the left is slope-intercept; a subtracted $y$-value on the left with a factored $(x - x_1)$ on the right is point-slope; both variables on one side with the constant alone is standard.
4. To convert, name the destination first. Going to slope-intercept always means "solve for $y$"; going to standard form means "move the variable terms left, clear fractions, make $A$ positive".
5. For parallel and perpendicular, extract only the slopes — everything else is decoration — then test $m_1 = m_2$ or $m_1 m_2 = -1$ numerically; multiplying beats eyeballing reciprocals. Compute the required slope once and scan the options for it: the wrong three are almost always the negation, the reciprocal, and the original.
