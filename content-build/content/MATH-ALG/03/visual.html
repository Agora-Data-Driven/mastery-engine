1. Lines Become Functions

Every line you drew in Slope-intercept form was already a function: one x goes in, one y comes out. The test that says so is a vertical line — and it is the same test that throws a circle out. Pick a relation and drag the sweep line across it. Like a vending machine: one button must always drop exactly one item, or nobody trusts the machine.

Two outputs sharing one input is fatal; two inputs sharing one output is free. That asymmetry is the whole definition, and it is why the horizontal line test asks a completely different question — one this lesson only reaches in visual 5, about running the rule backwards.

2. The Function Machine

f(x) is not a product, it is an instruction: replace every x by whatever is handed in. Drag the input and watch the substitution happen, next to the three answers the distractors are built from. Like a coffee machine: it does not care whether you feed it beans or bricks, it only ever runs the same routine on what you put in.

Every wrong row above is a real computation of something — just not of the thing that was asked. That is why writing the substitution down with brackets, before simplifying, defends you: the distractor stops being a plausible alternative and becomes an answer to a different question.

3. Domain And Range

Two sets, two questions. The domain is what you are allowed to put in; the range is what can actually come out. Drag a candidate input along the top line and a candidate output along the bottom one, and the panel tests each against the real formula. Like a passport queue: one gate checks whether you may enter, the other checks whether anyone ever arrives there.

Only two things ever shrink a domain at this level: a denominator that hits zero, and an even root of a negative. Everything else is allowed in. And when the boundary value itself is legal — x = 2 for the square root, y = 0 for x² — the inequality is not strict, which is the entire difference between two of the answer choices you will be offered.

4. Sign Versus Direction

Two questions that sound alike and are unrelated: where is f below the axis (sign) and where is f climbing (direction). Drag the point and read both answers at once — they disagree far more often than they agree. Like a plane's altitude and its climb rate: you can be below sea level and rising, or high up and falling.

The intervals under the picture are not stored anywhere — they are found by sampling the real f and f' across the window, exactly the way you would find them by hand: factor for the sign intervals, differentiate for the direction ones. And note x³, whose derivative is zero at a single point and which is increasing everywhere anyway: one isolated flat instant does not interrupt a climb.

5. The Inverse Mirror

An inverse swaps the roles of the inputs and the outputs, which on a graph is one single move: reflect across the line y = x. Drag the point to watch (a, b) become (b, a), then drag the horizontal line to find out whether the reflection is still a function. Like a recipe read backwards: fine for "add 3 eggs", hopeless for "bake until brown".

Read the arrow backwards and most inverse questions are already answered: if f(3) = 8 then f-1(8) = 3, with no algebra at all. And keep the notation straight — f-1 is the reversing rule, never the reciprocal 1/f.
