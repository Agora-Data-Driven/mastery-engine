1. Expand And Undo

You already distribute, and you already know what an inverse pair looks like. Multiplying binomials is the distributive law applied twice; factoring is that same machine run backwards. Drag p and q and watch the box fill, then read the answer right to left. Like a zip: one pull closes it into a seam, the other pull opens it back into two sides.

The box is the honest version of FOIL. Firsts, Outers, Inners, Lasts is just a reading order for these four cells — and it only ever covers two terms times two terms. The grid keeps working when a bracket grows a third term, which is why it is the picture worth carrying forward.

2. The Middle Term

Three special products, one question each time: what happens to the two cross terms? They add, they subtract, or they cancel. Pick a product, drag a and b, and watch the area you would lose by forgetting them. Like measuring a room after knocking a wall through — the new floor is not just the two old floors, it is those plus the doorway.

Every wrong option you will ever be offered for these three is one of the same four slips: the middle term dropped, its factor of 2 dropped, its sign flipped, or a coefficient left unsquared. Naming which slip an option encodes is faster than expanding it — and safer, because the slip is what you are actually being tested on.

3. Spot The Pattern

Build any quadratic you like and watch the decision procedure run on it: greatest common factor first, then count terms, then match a pattern. The verdict and the factorization are computed from your coefficients, not looked up. Like triage at a door: one quick check, then the patient is sent down exactly one corridor.

Notice how much of the work the term count does. Two terms send you to a difference of squares, three terms to a perfect square or a factor-pair search, and a sum of squares such as x^2 + 9 is simply a dead end over the real numbers — no pair of real numbers multiplies to +9 and adds to 0. Getting the count and the GCF right first is most of the skill.

4. Group And Factor

Four terms have their own method, and it turns on one condition: both pairs must leave the same bracket. Drag the numbers, then switch the pairing to watch a legal-looking split walk straight into a dead end. Like pairing socks — the pairing only works if the two halves match, and if they do not you re-sort rather than force it.

Grouping is the same greatest-common-factor move applied three times: once to the whole polynomial, once inside each pair, and once more when the shared bracket itself becomes the common factor. Nothing new is being asked of you — only that you keep noticing what every term has in common.
