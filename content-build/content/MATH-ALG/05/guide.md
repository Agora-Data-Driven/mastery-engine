**The big idea**: **Factoring quadratics (intro, grouping, difference of squares, perfect squares)** taught you to turn $x^2 + 5x + 6$ into $(x+2)(x+3)$, and **Multiplying binomials and special products** taught you the road back. This lesson is what that algebra is *for*. A quadratic is a function, so **Recognizing functions from tables and graphs**, **Evaluating functions and function notation**, **Domain and range** and **Intervals (Positive/Negative, Increasing/Decreasing)** now describe a curve with a turning point instead of the straight line you met in **Intercepts and Slope** and **Slope-intercept form, Point-slope form, and Standard form** — and **Modeling with linear equations** becomes modeling something that peaks. Three ideas carry the lesson. First, **vertex form** stores the geometry inside the algebra: one glance gives the direction of opening, the turning point, the axis of symmetry and the extreme value. Second, the three solving methods are secretly one method — reach *(something)$^2$ = constant*, then take square roots; factoring and completing the square are two routes to that same place. Third, the Quadratic Formula is completing the square performed once in general, and the discriminant hiding inside it counts your solutions before you compute a single one.

**Key concepts**

- **Vertex form and features of quadratic graphs: $y = a(x-h)^2 + k$ hands you the entire picture.** The mechanism is that $(x-h)^2 \geq 0$ for every real $x$, and it is 0 only at $x = h$. So the term $a(x-h)^2$ can only push $y$ *away* from $k$, always in the direction of the sign of $a$. If $a > 0$ the parabola opens **upward** and $k$ is a minimum; if $a < 0$ it opens **downward** and $k$ is a maximum. The turning point — the **vertex** — is $(h,k)$, and the mirror line through it, the **axis of symmetry**, is the vertical line $x = h$. Worked: $y = (x-2)^2 + 3$ has $a=1$, $h=2$, $k=3$, so the vertex is $(2,3)$, the axis is $x=2$, the minimum value is 3. Worked with the sign trap: $y = -(x+1)^2 + 4$ has $a=-1$ and $h=-1$, because $x+1$ is $x-(-1)$; it opens **downward**, vertex $(-1,4)$, maximum value 4. Without the sign of $a$ you cannot say whether $k$ is a floor or a ceiling.
- **The extreme value is $k$ — the *y*-coordinate of the vertex. $h$ only says where it happens.** For $y = -(x+1)^2 + 4$ the maximum value is 4, attained at $x=-1$; answering $-1$ answers a different question. Neither the discriminant nor "the slope at the vertex" (0 for every parabola) gives that value.
- **Vertex form and standard form are the same function; completing the square is the bridge.** Expand: $a(x-h)^2 + k = ax^2 - 2ahx + (ah^2+k)$. Matching against $ax^2 + bx + c$ gives $b = -2ah$, so $h = -\frac{b}{2a}$ and $k$ is the function evaluated there. Worked: $y = x^2 + 6x + 5$ has $h = -6/2 = -3$ and $k = 9 - 18 + 5 = -4$, so $y = (x+3)^2 - 4$: vertex $(-3,-4)$, axis $x=-3$, opening upward.
- **Solving quadratics by square roots is the base case, and the $\pm$ is not optional.** Squaring destroys sign, so undoing it must restore both possibilities: $x^2 = d$ with $d>0$ has exactly the two solutions $\pm\sqrt{d}$. Worked: $x^2 = 49$ gives $x=-7$ or $x=7$; $x^2 - 16 = 0$ becomes $x^2 = 16$, so $x = \pm 4$; $x^2 - 9 = 0$ gives $x = \pm 3$. Note $\pm 16$ is the classic wrong answer to $x^2 = 16$ — take the root, do not keep the constant. Over the reals $x^2 + 4 = 0$ becomes $x^2 = -4$, which has **no real solution**, because the square of a real number is never negative. Reach for this method whenever there is no linear $x$ term, and on anything already shaped as $(x-h)^2 = c$.
- **Factoring solves through the zero-product property: a product is zero exactly when a factor is zero.** For a monic quadratic, find two numbers with product $c$ and sum $b$. Worked: $x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0$ needs product 6, sum 5 — that is 2 and 3 — so $(x+2)(x+3)=0$ and $x=-2$ or $x=-3$. The signs flip, which is why $x = 2, 3$ is the trap here. With negatives: $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$ needs product 6, sum $-5$, so $(x-2)(x-3)=0$ and $x=2$ or $x=3$; and $x^2 - 4x - 5 = 0$ needs product $-5$, sum $-4$, giving $(x-5)(x+1)=0$ and $x=5$ or $x=-1$.
- **Completing the square always works, which is exactly why it matters.** Mechanism, for leading coefficient 1: take half of $b$ and square it, $\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$, then add and subtract that number. Worked: for $x^2 + 6x + 5 = 0$, half of 6 is 3 and $3^2 = 9$, so **9** is the number added and subtracted. Then $x^2 + 6x + 9 - 9 + 5 = 0$, i.e. $(x+3)^2 - 4 = 0$, so $(x+3)^2 = 4$, so $x+3 = \pm 2$, giving $x=-1$ or $x=-5$. It never fails because it rewrites one side as a **perfect square plus a constant**, leaving a perfect square equal to a constant that square roots finish. Nothing has to factor over the integers, and irrational answers are welcome: $x^2 + 6x + 4 = 0$ runs identically and lands on $x = -3 \pm \sqrt{5}$.
- **The Quadratic Formula is that derivation done once, in general.** From $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \neq 0$: divide by $a$ to get $x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}$; add $\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2$ to both sides; the left becomes $\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2$ and the right becomes $\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}$; take square roots and subtract $\frac{b}{2a}$. The result is $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$. Distractors live in the details: the denominator is $2a$, not $2c$; the radicand is $b^2-4ac$, not $4ac-b^2$; the leading term is $-b$.
- **The discriminant $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ counts the solutions before you find them.** If $\Delta > 0$ the $\pm$ gives two distinct real solutions and the parabola crosses the $x$-axis twice. If $\Delta = 0$ the root term vanishes, leaving one repeated solution $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$: the vertex sits *on* the axis, so the graph **touches the x-axis once**. If $\Delta < 0$ there is no real square root, so there are **no real solutions** and the graph never meets the axis. Worked: $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$ has $a=1$, $b=-5$, $c=6$, so $\Delta = 25 - 24 = 1$ and $x = \frac{5 \pm 1}{2} = 3$ or $2$ — the pair factoring gave. $x^2 - 4x - 5 = 0$ has $\Delta = 16 + 20 = 36$ and $x = \frac{4 \pm 6}{2} = 5$ or $-1$. And $x^2 + 4 = 0$ has $b=0$, $\Delta = -16$: no real solution, agreeing with the square-root argument. And $k = -\frac{\Delta}{4a}$, which is why $\Delta = 0$ puts the vertex on the axis.
- **Interpreting quadratic models: the vertex is the optimum and the zeros are the "equals zero" moments.** In an applied quadratic the vertex is the **maximum or minimum output of interest** — not an asymptote, not the $y$-intercept — and the direction of opening decides which: a model that opens downward has the vertex as its **greatest** value, upward as its least. The **zeros** are where the modeled quantity equals 0. Worked projectile: $h(t) = -16t^2 + 32t + 48$ in feet and seconds. The leading coefficient $-16$ is negative, so the graph **opens downward** and a maximum exists. It peaks at $t = -\frac{b}{2a} = 1$ second, at $h(1) = -16+32+48 = 64$ feet, and reaches the ground where $-16t^2+32t+48 = 0$, i.e. $(t-3)(t+1)=0$, so $t=3$ or $t=-1$. In the general form $h(t) = -16t^2 + vt + s$, the $-16$ carries gravity, $s$ is the initial height and $v$ the initial upward velocity.
- **A model has a domain, so reject solutions that context forbids.** $t=-1$ above is algebraically flawless and physically meaningless: the answer is $t=3$ seconds and $t=-1$ is **rejected on context**. You do not negate it and you do not round it to 0. Worked revenue model: $R(p) = p(100-2p) = -2p^2 + 100p$ opens downward and peaks at $p = -\frac{100}{2(-2)} = 25$, giving $R(25) = 1250$. The maximum revenue is 1250 dollars; 25 is the price achieving it. Its zeros, $p=0$ and $p=50$, are the prices at which revenue is nothing.

**Rules to remember**

- $y = a(x-h)^2 + k$ is vertex form: vertex $(h,k)$, axis of symmetry $x=h$, opens upward when $a>0$ and downward when $a<0$.
- $(x+1)^2$ means $h=-1$. The form subtracts $h$, so a plus inside means a negative $h$.
- The maximum or minimum **value** is $k$; $h$ is only where it occurs.
- From standard form, $h = -\frac{b}{2a}$ and $k = f(h)$.
- $x^2 = d$ has two real solutions $\pm\sqrt{d}$ when $d>0$, one when $d=0$, none when $d<0$.
- To complete the square with leading coefficient 1, add and subtract $\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$ — for $b=6$ that is 9.
- $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$ for every $ax^2+bx+c=0$ with $a \neq 0$.
- $\Delta = b^2-4ac$: positive means two roots and two crossings; zero means one repeated root and a single touch; negative means no real roots and no crossing.
- In a model: vertex = optimum, zeros = where the quantity is 0, negative leading coefficient = opens downward.
- Test every solution against the context and discard the impossible ones.

**Common pitfalls**

- Reading the vertex of $y = -(x+1)^2 + 4$ as $(1,4)$. The sign inside the bracket flips.
- Quoting the $x$-coordinate when asked for the maximum or minimum value, or the reverse.
- Dropping the negative root: $x^2 = 49$ has two answers, and "$x=7$ only" is the most popular wrong one.
- Answering $x = \pm 16$ for $x^2 = 16$ — keeping the constant instead of taking its root.
- Reporting the factors' own numbers as roots: $(x+2)(x+3)=0$ gives $-2$ and $-3$, not 2 and 3.
- Adding $b^2$ or $\frac{b}{2}$ instead of $\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$ when completing the square — for $b=6$ that is 9, not 36 and not 3.
- Believing completing the square guarantees rational answers. It guarantees a perfect square, nothing more.
- Calling a negative discriminant "one solution". Zero gives one; negative gives none over the reals.
- Keeping a negative time, length or count because the algebra produced it.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Read the form before choosing a method. No linear term means square roots; obvious integer factors mean factoring; anything else means completing the square or the formula — and the formula never fails.
2. For vertex-form questions, name $a$, $h$ and $k$ out loud, flipping the sign of whatever is inside the bracket, then answer from those three numbers.
3. When a question asks for a maximum or minimum, check whether it wants the value ($k$) or its location ($h$). Distractors pair the two.
4. When a question asks *how many* solutions, or how the graph meets the $x$-axis, compute $\Delta = b^2-4ac$ and stop. You rarely need the roots themselves.
5. Verify by substitution — putting $x=-1$ and $x=-5$ back into $x^2+6x+5$ kills every sign slip.
6. In modeling questions, translate first: which quantity is input, which is output, what does zero output mean, and does the graph open upward or downward? Then check each candidate against the real-world domain.

**Where this leads**: every "no real solution" here was a promise, not a dead end. The next lesson, **06 Complex numbers**, supplies the missing numbers: the imaginary unit $i$ with $i^2 = -1$ turns $x^2 + 4 = 0$ into $x = \pm 2i$, and every negative discriminant into a conjugate pair. You will add, subtract and multiply complex numbers, then use complex conjugates to divide them — and the formula you derived here becomes one that always returns two solutions, with no exceptions at all.
