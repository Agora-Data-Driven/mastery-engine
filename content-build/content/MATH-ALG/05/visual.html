1. From Factors To Vertex

You already turn x^2 + 5x + 6 into (x + 2)(x + 3). Those factors hand you the roots — and the roots hand you the vertex for free, because a parabola is symmetric about the point halfway between them. Drag each root and watch the factored, expanded and vertex forms move as one object. Like a hammock slung between two trees: the lowest point is always exactly halfway across.

already taught — factoring
x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3) roots x = -2 and x = -3
this lesson — vertex form
h = midpoint of the roots k = the value of y there y = (x - h)^2 + k

Watch the sign flip as you drag: the factor (x + 2) contributes the root -2, never +2. That single reversal is what turns x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0 into x = -2, -3 rather than the tempting 2, 3 — and it is the difference between two answers that both appear on the page.

2. Reading Vertex Form

y = a(x - h)^2 + k is a graph written as a sentence: a says which way it opens, h says where the turn happens, k says how high. Drag all three and read the four features off the picture. Like a thermostat: one dial for the setting, one for how fast it reacts, one for which way it pushes.

Two habits pay for themselves here. Read the bracket as x - h, so a plus sign inside means a negative h. And when a question asks for the maximum or minimum value, answer with k — the height — not with h, which only says where it happens. Every distractor set on this topic offers you both.

3. Completing The Square

Why is the magic number (b/2)^2? Because x^2 + bx really is a square with a corner missing. Drag b and watch the corner you have to add — then watch the badge tell you which of the three methods is quickest for the equation you have built. Like a jigsaw with one piece gone: you can only see the picture once you supply the missing bit.

The picture uses the size of b, so a negative b draws the same square — the algebra is unchanged because (b/2)^2 squares away the sign. That is also why this method never cares whether anything factors: it manufactures a perfect square by force, and square roots do the rest.

4. The Discriminant Dial

One number under the square root sign decides everything: b^2 - 4ac. Drag a, b and c and watch the quadratic formula fill itself in while the parabola crosses the axis twice, grazes it once, then misses it entirely. Like checking the weather before packing: you learn how many answers exist before you go and find them.

Notice what the dial does not change: the formula itself. x = (-b +/- sqrt(b^2 - 4ac)) / (2a) is completing the square carried out once, in general — the denominator is 2a, never 2c, and the radicand is b^2 - 4ac, never 4ac - b^2. Both wrong versions appear in answer lists precisely because they look right at a glance.

5. Reject The Root

A model is a parabola with a job. The vertex is the best or worst outcome, the zeros are where the quantity hits zero — and one of those zeros is often a lie the algebra tells. Switch the story, drag the numbers, and watch the impossible answer appear and get thrown out. Like a satnav offering a route through a river: perfectly computed, obviously not taken.

The same parabola answers three different questions, so read what is being asked. "When does it land?" wants a zero. "How high does it get?" wants the vertex height k, not the time h at which it happens. "Is that answer possible?" wants the context — and a negative time, a negative price or a negative count is rejected, never negated and never rounded to zero.
