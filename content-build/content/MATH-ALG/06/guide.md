**The big idea**: **The Quadratic Formula** hands every quadratic the same answer, $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}}{2a}$, and **Vertex form and features of quadratic graphs** told you what it means when a parabola sits entirely above the $x$-axis — nothing crosses, so there is nothing to find. **Number of solutions to equations** let you call that "no real solution", and **Solving quadratics by square roots, factoring, and completing the square** kept hitting the same wall from the other side: $x^{2} = -9$ has no real answer, because squaring a real number never produces a negative. This lesson does not repair the formula. It enlarges the number system underneath it by adjoining exactly one new symbol, the imaginary unit $i$, with exactly one defining property, $i^{2} = -1$. Everything after that is algebra you already own — **Multiplying binomials and special products** does the multiplying, and **Factoring quadratics (intro, grouping, difference of squares, perfect squares)** turns out to be the engine of the dividing. The only genuinely new instruction is: whenever an $i^{2}$ appears, fold it back into $-1$.

**Key concepts**

- **The imaginary unit is defined by $i^{2} = -1$, and by nothing else.** It is an adjoined symbol, not a computed value: you are not told what $i$ *is*, only what it *does* when squared. That distinction matters because the plausible-looking alternatives are all false — $i \neq -1$ (if it were, $i^{2}$ would be $+1$), and $i \neq \sqrt{1}$ (that is just 1). From the one rule you immediately get square roots of negatives: split off the $-1$ and take the ordinary root of what is left. $\sqrt{-9} = \sqrt{9 \cdot (-1)} = \sqrt{9}\,\sqrt{-1} = 3i$. Verify it the only way that counts: $(3i)^{2} = 3^{2} i^{2} = 9(-1) = -9$. In general $\sqrt{-k} = i\sqrt{k}$ for $k > 0$, so $\sqrt{-36} = 6i$ and $\sqrt{-5} = i\sqrt{5}$.
- **Powers of the imaginary unit cycle with period 4.** Build the cycle rather than memorising it: $i^{1} = i$; $i^{2} = -1$ by definition; $i^{3} = i^{2} \cdot i = -1 \cdot i = -i$; $i^{4} = (i^{2})^{2} = (-1)^{2} = 1$. Because $i^{4} = 1$, multiplying by it changes nothing, so the list $i, -1, -i, 1$ repeats forever — the cycle length is 4, not 2, even though the *sign* flips every 2. Why it matters: without it, $i^{23}$ is a 23-step multiplication. With it, divide the exponent by 4 and keep the remainder. $23 = 4 \cdot 5 + 3$, so $i^{23} = (i^{4})^{5} \cdot i^{3} = 1 \cdot (-i) = -i$. Remainder 0 gives 1, remainder 1 gives $i$, remainder 2 gives $-1$, remainder 3 gives $-i$.
- **Standard form is $a + bi$: two independent real coordinates, not one number.** $a$ is the real part and $b$ is the imaginary part — and $b$ is a *real* number, the coefficient only. The imaginary part of $3 - 7i$ is $-7$, not $-7i$. Because $i$ is not a real number, $a$ and $bi$ can never be merged, exactly as $3 + 2\sqrt{2}$ cannot be collapsed into one rational number. Real numbers are the case $b = 0$; pure imaginary numbers are the case $a = 0$. Two complex numbers are equal only when both coordinates match, which is why every problem here ends by reporting a pair.
- **Adding and subtracting is combining like terms: real with real, imaginary with imaginary.** Treat $i$ as an algebraic symbol and the rule is the one you already use on $3x + 2$: $(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i$. Worked: $(3 + 2i) + (1 - 5i) = (3 + 1) + (2 - 5)i = 4 - 3i$. Subtracting means distributing the minus sign across **both** parts of the second number: $(5 + i) - (2 + 4i) = (5 - 2) + (1 - 4)i = 3 - 3i$. Drop the minus on the imaginary part and you get $3 + 5i$ — a standing distractor. Nothing here can mix the four coefficients into a single real term; the two axes never talk to each other under addition.
- **Multiplying is FOIL plus one fold.** Recall **Multiplying binomials and special products** and expand: $(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\,i^{2}$. The last term is the only new event — $i^{2} = -1$ turns $bd\,i^{2}$ into $-bd$, which is *real*, so it migrates into the real part and arrives with its sign flipped. The general result is $(ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i$. Worked: $(2 + i)(3 - i) = 6 - 2i + 3i - i^{2} = 6 + i - (-1) = 7 + i$. Watch where the $+1$ came from: $-i^{2} = -(-1) = +1$. This is why multiplication, unlike addition, does move information between the real and imaginary parts.
- **The complex conjugate of $a + bi$ is $a - bi$** — same real part, sign flipped on the imaginary part only. Not $-a + bi$, not $-a - bi$, not $b + ai$. Its purpose is what it does under multiplication: $(a + bi)(a - bi)$ is the **difference of squares** you already factor, $a^{2} - (bi)^{2} = a^{2} - b^{2}i^{2} = a^{2} + b^{2}$. The cross terms $+abi$ and $-abi$ cancel exactly, so the answer is always real and never negative. Worked: $(3 + 2i)(3 - 2i) = 9 - 4i^{2} = 9 + 4 = 13$. Note the two traps in that one line — it is not $9 - 4 = 5$ (that would need $i^{2} = +1$) and it is not $13i$ (no $i$ survives at all).
- **Dividing complex numbers means multiplying the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator, in order to make the denominator real.** The denominator is the whole problem: $\frac{1}{1 - i}$ is not in standard form because you cannot read off $a$ and $b$ while an $i$ sits downstairs. Since $\frac{a - bi}{a - bi} = 1$, multiplying by it changes the form and not the value, and the previous bullet guarantees the new denominator is the real number $c^{2} + d^{2}$. Then split the fraction and you are done. Worked: $\frac{1 + i}{1 - i} = \frac{(1 + i)(1 + i)}{(1 - i)(1 + i)} = \frac{1 + 2i + i^{2}}{1^{2} + 1^{2}} = \frac{1 + 2i - 1}{2} = \frac{2i}{2} = i$. A second, messier one: $\frac{4 + 7i}{2 - 3i}$ has conjugate $2 + 3i$, giving numerator $8 + 12i + 14i + 21i^{2} = -13 + 26i$ and denominator $2^{2} + 3^{2} = 13$, so the answer is $-1 + 2i$.
- **This closes the quadratic story you started with.** Solve $x^{2} - 4x + 13 = 0$: the discriminant is $16 - 52 = -36$, so $x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm 6i}{2} = 2 \pm 3i$. The two roots are conjugates of each other — inevitable, since the only thing distinguishing them is the $\pm$ in front of the root. Check by rebuilding the quadratic: $(x - 2 - 3i)(x - 2 + 3i) = (x-2)^{2} - (3i)^{2} = x^{2} - 4x + 4 + 9$, which is the original. The parabola still never touches the $x$-axis; what changed is that "no solution" has become "no *real* solution", and there are exactly two.

**Rules to remember**

- $i^{2} = -1$ is the definition. Everything else in the lesson is derived from it.
- $\sqrt{-k} = i\sqrt{k}$ for $k > 0$. Convert to $i$ form *first*, then do any other arithmetic.
- Powers of $i$ repeat every 4: remainders 0, 1, 2, 3 give $1$, $i$, $-1$, $-i$.
- Standard form is $a + bi$ with $a$ and $b$ both real; the imaginary part is $b$, without the $i$.
- Adding and subtracting: real with real, imaginary with imaginary. The minus sign hits both parts.
- Multiplying: FOIL, then replace $i^{2}$ with $-1$ and move that term into the real part.
- Conjugate of $a + bi$ is $a - bi$, and $(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^{2} + b^{2}$, always real.
- Dividing: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the **denominator**, then split into $a + bi$.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Forgetting that $i^{2}$ equals $-1$ part-way through a calculation.** This is the single most common error when simplifying a fraction with conjugates: the FOIL is right, the last term is left as $i^{2}$ or silently treated as $+1$, and both the sign and the part it belongs to come out wrong.
- **Reading $i^{2} = -1$ as $i = -1$.** Substituting $-1$ for $i$ collapses every complex number to a real one and makes $i^{2}$ come out $+1$ — a contradiction one line later.
- **Replacing $i$ with the value 1.** Same failure with a different sign; square your replacement and see whether you get $-1$.
- **Distributing a subtraction over only the first part.** $(5 + i) - (2 + 4i)$ is $3 - 3i$; writing $3 + 5i$ means the minus never reached the $4i$.
- **Using $\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ when both are negative.** $\sqrt{-4}\,\sqrt{-9} = (2i)(3i) = 6i^{2} = -6$, not $\sqrt{36} = 6$. The rule needs a non-negative radicand, which is precisely what this lesson stepped outside of.
- **Thinking the powers of $i$ cycle every 2 because the sign alternates.** $i^{2} = -1$ and $i^{4} = 1$ are different values, so the period is 4.
- **Multiplying by the conjugate of the numerator.** It clears nothing: the $i$ you needed to remove was downstairs.
- **Getting the conjugate's sign pattern wrong.** Only the imaginary part flips. $-a - bi$ is the negative, not the conjugate.

**How to approach the questions**

1. On any power of $i$, divide the exponent by 4 and answer from the remainder. Do not compute the power; the four possible answers are always $1$, $i$, $-1$, $-i$.
2. On a definition question, test each option by squaring it. Only $i^{2} = -1$ survives, and that eliminates $i = -1$ and $i = \sqrt{1}$ instantly.
3. For square roots of negatives, pull out $\sqrt{-1} = i$ before anything else, then take the ordinary root. $\sqrt{-9} = 3i$; the distractors are the unsimplified $9i$ and sign flips.
4. For adding and subtracting, work the real coordinate and the imaginary coordinate separately and compare both against the options — the wrong answers usually get one of the two right, so checking a single coordinate will not separate them.
5. For any multiplication, expect the $i^{2}$ term and write the fold explicitly. In $(2 + i)(3 - i)$, the distractor $6 - i$ is what you get by dropping that term and the distractor $5 + i$ is what you get by taking $i^{2} = +1$.
6. When a conjugate product appears, jump straight to $a^{2} + b^{2}$: $(3 + 2i)(3 - 2i) = 9 + 4 = 13$. A purely real answer is the point of the operation, so any option carrying an $i$ is wrong by construction.
7. For a division, state the goal before the algebra — make the denominator real — then confirm by multiplying your answer back by the denominator.
