1. Roots That Vanish

The Quadratic Formula and Vertex form and features of quadratic graphs have always agreed with each other: lift a parabola off the x-axis and the crossings disappear, and at exactly that moment the formula asks for the square root of a negative number. Number of solutions to equations called that "no solution". Drag the constant term through the moment it happens. Like a thermometer reading below zero: the temperature did not stop existing — you needed a number line that goes further.

Nothing about the parabola changed when the roots went complex — it is the same smooth curve, and it still has a vertex, an axis of symmetry and a y-intercept. What changed is the number system you are allowed to answer in. Complex numbers were not invented to describe a new picture; they were invented so that one old formula never has to say "no answer".

2. The Powers Cycle

One rule — i^2 = -1 — forces every other power, and the answers walk round a four-stop loop forever. Slide the exponent and watch where it lands. Like the hours on a clock face: hour 15 is hour 3, because only the remainder ever matters.

The period is 4, not 2. It is tempting to say "the sign just alternates", but the four stops are four different values — 1, i, -1, -i — and only every fourth step returns you to where you started. Two of them are real and two are imaginary, which is the quiet reason multiplying complex numbers can move a quantity from the imaginary part into the real part at all.

3. Parts Stay Apart

Adding and subtracting complex numbers is combining like terms and nothing else: real with real, imaginary with imaginary. Move the four coefficients and watch the two columns stay sealed off from one another. Like adding up a shopping list of apples and oranges: you get a count of each, never a single number of fruit.

Because the two columns never interact, addition and subtraction of complex numbers is exactly vector addition on the plane you can see on the right — the arrows go tip to tail. That is the last operation for which this is true. The moment you multiply, the two columns start feeding each other, which is the whole subject of the next visual.

4. Foil Then Fold

Multiplying complex numbers is the binomial expansion you already do, plus exactly one extra move: the i^2 term folds into the real part with its sign flipped. Toggle the fold on and off and watch which digit changes. Like a receipt where one line is in a foreign currency: three lines add up normally, and the fourth has to be converted before it can join them.

Read the general result off the grid: (a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i. The minus sign in ac - bd is not a convention to memorise — it is i^2 arriving from the fourth cell. Every distractor on a multiplication question is one of two things: that cell ignored, or that cell added instead of subtracted.

5. Conjugate Clears i

A fraction is not in standard form while an i sits downstairs. Multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator is the one move that clears it — try the other conjugate and watch it fail. Like rationalising a denominator: same trick, same reason, one new sign.

Two facts carry this whole visual, and both come from the difference of squares you already factor. (a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2 is always real and never negative, and (a - bi)/(a - bi) = 1, so multiplying by it changes the form and not the value. Every division question is those two facts applied in that order.
