**The big idea**: **Multiplying binomials and special products** had you writing $x \cdot x = x^2$ on every line; **The imaginary unit i** had you evaluating $i^2 = -1$ and watching $i^3 = -i$, $i^4 = 1$ cycle; and **Solving quadratics by square roots, factoring, and completing the square** handed back answers such as $x = \pm\sqrt{50}$ that nobody ever tidied up. This lesson turns those habits into stated rules, then does something new with them: it moves the variable out of the base and into the exponent. Three ideas carry all of it. First, an integer exponent is a *count of repeated factors*, so every exponent property is bookkeeping on that count — multiply and the counts add, nest a power inside a power and they multiply, divide and they subtract. Second, a square root is simplified by factoring the radicand until a perfect square falls out, which is the factoring you already do, performed under a radical sign. Third, $y = b^x$ changes by a constant **ratio** per unit step where a line changes by a constant difference, and the single number $b$ decides the entire shape of the graph.

**Key concepts**

- **Exponent properties (integer exponents) start with the product rule, $x^m \cdot x^n = x^{m+n}$.** Mechanism: $x^3 \cdot x^2$ is $(x \cdot x \cdot x)(x \cdot x)$ — five factors of $x$ in one bag, so the counts *add*. Why it matters: the plausible-looking alternative $x^{mn}$ is a different number, and one numeric test settles it. At $x = 2$: $2^3 \cdot 2^2 = 8 \cdot 4 = 32 = 2^5$, while $2^{3 \cdot 2} = 2^6 = 64$.
- **Power of a power: $(x^m)^n = x^{mn}$.** Mechanism: $(x^3)^2 = x^3 \cdot x^3$, which is $n$ copies of a block of $m$ factors, so the total count is the product. Numbers: $(2^3)^2 = 8^2 = 64 = 2^6$, and $6 = 3 \times 2$. Hold this against the previous rule: the same two exponents, one adding and the other multiplying. The difference is only whether the blocks sit side by side (a product) or nested in a bracket (a power of a power).
- **Quotient rule: $\dfrac{x^m}{x^n} = x^{m-n}$ for $x \neq 0$.** Mechanism is cancellation, not a new idea: $\dfrac{x^5}{x^2}$ has five factors on top and two below, two pairs cancel, three survive. Numbers: $2^5 / 2^2 = 32/4 = 8 = 2^3$. The order of subtraction is the trap — $x^{n-m}$ is the *reciprocal* of the right answer, so $2^{2-5} = 1/8$, not $8$.
- **$x^0 = 1$ for every nonzero $x$ — derived, not decreed.** Put $m = n$ in the quotient rule: $\dfrac{x^3}{x^3} = x^{3-3} = x^0$. But that same expression is a nonzero number divided by itself, which is $1$. So $x^0 = 1$: $7^0 = 1$, $(-3)^0 = 1$, $(0.5)^0 = 1$. The exclusion $x \neq 0$ does real work: the derivation needed $x^3/x^3$, and at $x = 0$ that is $0/0$. Note what $x^0$ is *not* — neither $0$ nor undefined for a nonzero base.
- **Negative exponents are reciprocals, and they fall out of the same rule.** $\dfrac{x^0}{x^n} = x^{0-n} = x^{-n}$, and the left side is plainly $\dfrac{1}{x^n}$. Hence $x^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$, which at $x = 2$ is $1/4$. A negative exponent never makes the value negative — $-x^2$ at $x = 2$ is $-4$ — and it is neither $x/2$ nor $2/x$. Walk the ladder and it becomes obvious: $2^3 = 8$, $2^2 = 4$, $2^1 = 2$, $2^0 = 1$, $2^{-1} = 1/2$, $2^{-2} = 1/4$, every step down dividing by the base.
- **The properties compose, which is the whole point of having them.** Simplify $\dfrac{(x^2)^3 \cdot x^{-4}}{x^3}$: the power rule gives $x^6$, the product rule folds in $x^{-4}$ to leave $x^2$, and the quotient rule subtracts $3$, giving $x^{-1} = 1/x$. One more: a product raised to a power distributes, $(xy)^n = x^n y^n$, so $(2x^3)^2 = 4x^6$ — the coefficient gets the exponent too, the commonest slip in a multi-step simplification.
- **Simplifying square-root expressions means factoring the radicand so you can pull out a perfect square.** Mechanism: $\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{25}\,\sqrt{2} = 5\sqrt{2}$. Only a *squared* factor can escape the radical, because $\sqrt{k^2} = k$ for $k \ge 0$; pulling out a single prime or an odd factor achieves nothing, since $\sqrt{2}$ has no square inside it. Check numerically: $5 \times 1.41421 = 7.07107$, and $7.07107^2 = 50$. The same move gives $\sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \cdot 2} = 3\sqrt{2}$, and its near neighbours are worth recognising on sight: $\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{24} = 2\sqrt{6}$, $\sqrt{6}$ already simplest. Take the *largest* square factor, or repeat until none is left: $\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{4 \cdot 18} = 2\sqrt{18} = 6\sqrt{2}$, which $\sqrt{36 \cdot 2}$ reaches in one step.
- **The splitting rule carries a domain condition: $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}$ is guaranteed precisely when $a$ and $b$ are both nonnegative.** Why the condition exists — watch it fail. $\sqrt{-4} \cdot \sqrt{-9} = (2i)(3i) = 6i^2 = -6$, while $\sqrt{(-4)(-9)} = \sqrt{36} = +6$. Recall **The imaginary unit i**: the two sides differ by exactly the factor $i^2$, so the rule that all radical simplification rests on is simply false once both radicands are negative. Related: over the reals $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$, not $x$, because the principal square root is the nonnegative one — $\sqrt{(-5)^2} = 5$, the same nonnegativity you used in **Absolute value equations**.
- **Like radicals add like terms; the radicand is a label, not a quantity to combine.** $2\sqrt{3} + 5\sqrt{3} = (2+5)\sqrt{3} = 7\sqrt{3}$ — the distributive law with $\sqrt{3}$ as the common factor, exactly as $2u + 5u = 7u$. Decimals confirm it: $2(1.732) + 5(1.732) = 3.464 + 8.660 = 12.124 = 7(1.732)$. The two wrong answers come from combining the wrong thing — $7\sqrt{6}$ adds the radicands, $10\sqrt{3}$ multiplies the coefficients. Unlike radicals such as $2\sqrt{3} + 5\sqrt{2}$ do not combine; but simplify before you judge, since $\sqrt{8} + \sqrt{18} = 2\sqrt{2} + 3\sqrt{2} = 5\sqrt{2}$ only looked unlike until tidied.
- **Graphing exponential growth and decay: the variable moves into the exponent, and the change becomes multiplicative.** In $y = b^x$ with $b > 0$, each unit step in $x$ multiplies $y$ by $b$. Compare $y = 2^x$ at $x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$ — $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$, a constant *ratio* of $2$ — against the line $y = 3x + 1$, which gives $1, 4, 7, 10, 13$, a constant *difference* of $3$. Every such graph passes through $(0, 1)$, because the y-intercept is $b^0 = 1$ for any positive base: the zero-exponent rule again, now visible as a point all these curves share. The domain is all real numbers and the range is $y > 0$ (recall **Domain and range**), with the x-axis as a horizontal asymptote the curve approaches and never reaches.
- **$b > 1$ is growth, $0 < b < 1$ is decay, $b = 1$ is neither.** With $b > 1$ the multiplier exceeds one, so each step is larger than the last and the curve rises ever more steeply — $y = 2^x$ reaches $1024$ at $x = 10$ and $1{,}048{,}576$ at $x = 20$. With $0 < b < 1$ the multiplier shrinks the value: $y = (1/3)^x$ gives $1, 1/3, 1/9, 1/27$, falling toward zero without ever touching it. The two families are one seen from two sides, since $(1/3)^x = 3^{-x}$: decay is growth reflected across the y-axis. And $b = 1$ gives $1^x = 1$, a horizontal line — excluded from both cases rather than counted as slow growth.
- **Exponential is not polynomial, and the giveaway is where the variable sits.** $y = x^3$ has the variable in the *base* and a fixed exponent, so it is a power function; $y = 3x + 1$ is linear; only $y = 2^x$ and $y = (1/3)^x$ are exponential. The distinction is not cosmetic: at $x = 5$, $x^3 = 125$ still beats $2^x = 32$; at $x = 10$ they are $1000$ and $1024$; by $x = 20$ it is $8000$ against over a million. Exponential growth overtakes every fixed power eventually, which is the whole content of the phrase "this algorithm is $O(2^n)$".

**Rules to remember**

- Multiply: add the exponents. Nest: multiply them. Divide: subtract them, top minus bottom.
- $x^0 = 1$ for every $x \neq 0$. $x^{-n} = 1/x^n$, and the value stays positive when $x$ is positive.
- $(xy)^n = x^n y^n$: the coefficient is raised too, so $(2x^3)^2 = 4x^6$.
- To simplify a square root, factor the radicand and extract the largest perfect square.
- $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}$ is guaranteed only for $a, b \ge 0$; over the reals $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$.
- Add radicals only when the radicands match, and add only the coefficients: $2\sqrt{3} + 5\sqrt{3} = 7\sqrt{3}$.
- For $y = b^x$ with $b > 0$: the y-intercept is always $1$, the range is always $y > 0$.
- $b > 1$ grows and steepens; $0 < b < 1$ decays toward the asymptote; $b = 1$ is a flat line.

**Common pitfalls**

- Multiplying exponents in a product ($x^m x^n = x^{mn}$) or adding them in a power of a power. Test with $x = 2$ and the confusion dies.
- Subtracting the wrong way round in $x^m/x^n$, which silently reports the reciprocal.
- Reading $x^0$ as $0$, or as undefined for a perfectly good nonzero base.
- Reading $x^{-2}$ as $-x^2$, $x/2$ or $2/x$. A negative exponent inverts; it does not negate and it does not halve.
- Pulling a non-square factor out of a radical — $\sqrt{50} = 25\sqrt{2}$ comes from "pulling out" $25$ without taking its root, and $10\sqrt{5}$ from factors that do not multiply to $50$.
- Stopping at a partial simplification: $\sqrt{72} = 2\sqrt{18}$ is correct but not finished, because $18$ still hides a $9$.
- Splitting $\sqrt{ab}$ when a radicand is negative. That is exactly where the rule fails, and where the imaginary unit takes over.
- Adding radicands ($7\sqrt{6}$) or multiplying coefficients ($10\sqrt{3}$) when combining like radicals.
- Calling $y = x^3$ exponential decay or growth. Check which position the variable occupies before anything else.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For any exponent identity, substitute $x = 2$ with small exponents and evaluate both sides. Ten seconds of arithmetic beats recalling which rule adds and which multiplies.
2. When the exponent is $0$ or negative, rewrite it through the quotient rule rather than reaching for a memorised fact — the derivation is two lines and it never misfires.
3. For "simplest radical form", find the largest perfect square dividing the radicand, take its root outside, and leave the rest inside. Then square your answer mentally to confirm you land back on the original.
4. For a "which expression equals $k\sqrt{m}$" question, work backwards instead: $3\sqrt{2}$ squares to $9 \cdot 2 = 18$, so the answer is $\sqrt{18}$ with no searching at all.
5. For a rule stated with conditions, ask what the condition prevents. The nonnegativity condition on $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$ exists because two negative radicands produce a factor of $i^2$.
6. For graph questions, read the base first and place it against the landmarks $b = 1$ and $b > 1$; then check $(0, 1)$, which every $y = b^x$ must pass through.
