1. Counting The Factors

You have been using these rules for two lessons without naming them: squaring a binomial needs x·x = x^2, and the powers of i cycle because i^2·i^2 = i^4. Every exponent property is one thing — counting how many copies of the base are in the bag. Pick an operation and drag the counts. Like piling up coins: two stacks poured together, or one stack copied several times.

2. Zero And Negative Powers

Nobody decreed that x^0 = 1. Walk the ladder down and each step divides by the base — keep walking past zero and you are forced into reciprocals. Drag the exponent through 0 and read the derivation as it changes. Like a thermometer: nothing special happens at zero, the scale just keeps going.

The ladder is also the reason a negative exponent cannot produce a negative number. Each step down divides by a positive base, and dividing a positive by a positive stays positive forever — the values shrink towards zero and never cross it. That is the same fact you will see again in visual 5 as the horizontal asymptote.

3. Pull Out Squares

Simplifying a square root is one move: find the largest perfect square hiding inside the radicand and walk it out through the sign. Drag the radicand and watch which squares divide it. Like unpacking a suitcase: only the pairs of socks come out matched.

Only a squared factor can leave the radical, because √(k²) = k for k ≥ 0. That is why "pull out a single prime" or "pull out an odd factor" is not a method — √2 has no square inside it, so nothing comes out. And over the reals √(x²) = |x|, never plain x: the principal root is the nonnegative one, so √((-5)²) = 5.

4. When Radicals Combine

Two rules that look alike and are not. Adding radicals needs the radicands to match; splitting a product needs them to be nonnegative. Switch between them and push each one until it breaks. Like currency: you can add euros to euros, and the conversion table only works inside the countries it was printed for.

Simplify before you judge whether radicals are alike. √8 + √18 looks like two unlike terms and is really 2√2 + 3√2 = 5√2 — visual 3 is the step that reveals it. The habit is: simplify every radical first, then collect like terms, exactly as you would with x-terms.

5. Growth Or Decay

Move the variable into the exponent and change stops being additive: each unit step multiplies. Drag the base across 1 and watch the curve turn over — and watch the point (0, 1) never move. Like interest on a balance: the same rate on a bigger balance is a bigger payment every year.

Read the base against two landmarks and every graph question answers itself: b = 1 is the flat line, and b > 1 versus 0 < b < 1 is growth versus decay. A negative base is not allowed at all — b^x has to be defined for every real x, and a negative base fails that between the integers.
