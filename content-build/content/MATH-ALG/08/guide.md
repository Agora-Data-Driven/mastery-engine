**The big idea**: **Factoring quadratics (intro, grouping, difference of squares, perfect squares)** taught you to turn a sum of terms into a product of factors; **Domain and range** taught you that a formula is only a function where it is actually defined; and **Equations with parentheses, decimals, and fractions** taught you to clear denominators by multiplying both sides by the LCD. This lesson welds those three together. A rational expression is a ratio of polynomials $\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$, and every rule you already trust for $\frac{2}{3}$ transfers unchanged: reduce by dividing out common factors, multiply straight across, divide by flipping the second fraction, and add only over a common denominator. Exactly one thing is new, and the whole lesson turns on it — the denominator now contains a variable, so it is zero at particular inputs, and those inputs are forbidden. Arithmetic that ignores them stays *symbolically* correct while silently answering a different question. That is why "simplify" here means simplify **and state the restrictions**, and why solving a rational equation ends with a check rather than with an answer.

**Key concepts**

- **A rational expression is a fraction of polynomials, and its denominator is a list of forbidden inputs.** $\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ means "$P(x)$ divided by $Q(x)$", so it exists exactly where $Q(x) \neq 0$. Factor the denominator and you can read the excluded values straight off: in $\frac{x^2-16}{x^2-4x}$ the denominator factors as $x(x-4)$, zero at $x = 0$ and $x = 4$, so the domain is every real except those two. Skip this step and every later step inherits a hole you cannot see.
- **Reducing rational expressions: factor both parts completely, then cancel common *factors*.** The mechanism is that canceling is division, not deletion: $\frac{ac}{bc} = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{c} = \frac{a}{b} \cdot 1$. Only something multiplying the *whole* numerator and the *whole* denominator can be split off as that 1. So $\frac{x^2-16}{x^2-4x} = \frac{(x-4)(x+4)}{x(x-4)} = \frac{x+4}{x}$. Check with numbers at $x = 2$: the original is $\frac{4-16}{4-8} = \frac{-12}{-4} = 3$, and $\frac{2+4}{2} = 3$. ✓
- **A term is not a factor, and this is the single commonest error in the topic.** In $\frac{x+4}{x}$ the $x$ upstairs is *added* to 4, so it is not a factor of the numerator and cannot be canceled. Test it: at $x = 1$ the true value is $\frac{5}{1} = 5$, while the illegal cancellation claims 4. Whenever you feel the urge to cancel, ask whether the thing is joined to its neighbours by $\times$ or by $+$.
- **Restrictions come from the ORIGINAL denominator, never from the simplified one.** $\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = x+3$, but only with $x \neq 3$, because canceling $(x-3)$ is exactly what removed the factor that made the original denominator zero. At $x = 3$ the original reads $\frac{0}{0}$ and is undefined, while $x+3$ cheerfully returns 6. Recall from calculus: this is a removable discontinuity, and $\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = 6$ is precisely the value the simplified form supplies at the hole. The two expressions agree everywhere except at one point, and stating $x \neq 3$ is how you keep that point honest.
- **Multiplying: cancel across the whole product first, then multiply straight across.** Numerators multiply, denominators multiply, and any factor upstairs may cancel any equal factor downstairs — including across the two fractions. $\frac{2x}{3} \times \frac{9}{x^2}$: the 3 divides into the 9 leaving 3, and one $x$ divides into $x^2$ leaving $x$, giving $\frac{2 \cdot 3}{x} = \frac{6}{x}$, with $x \neq 0$. Numeric check at $x = 3$: $\frac{6}{3} \times \frac{9}{9} = 2 \times 1 = 2$, and $\frac{6}{3} = 2$. ✓ Cancelling before multiplying keeps the polynomials small, which matters once the degree climbs.
- **Dividing: multiply by the reciprocal — and the divisor's numerator becomes a new restriction.** $\frac{x^2-1}{x+2} \div \frac{x-1}{x+2} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x+2} \times \frac{x+2}{x-1} = x+1$. Both $(x+2)$ and $(x-1)$ cancel, leaving $x \neq -2$ from the original denominators *and* $x \neq 1$, because dividing by $\frac{x-1}{x+2}$ is only legal when that fraction is nonzero. Check at $x = 3$: $\frac{8}{5} \div \frac{2}{5} = 4 = 3+1$. ✓ Structurally this is the move you made in **Complex conjugates and dividing complex numbers** — rearrange a division into a multiplication you already know how to do.
- **Adding and subtracting rational expressions requires a common denominator, because the denominator names the unit.** You cannot add thirds to fifths until both are fifteenths, and you cannot add $\frac{1}{x+1}$ to $\frac{1}{x-1}$ until both sit over $(x+1)(x-1) = x^2-1$: $\frac{(x-1) + (x+1)}{x^2-1} = \frac{2x}{x^2-1}$. The tempting $\frac{2}{2x}$ — add the tops, add the bottoms — is not addition at all. At $x = 3$ the true value is $\frac{6}{8} = 0.75$ while the fake gives $\frac{1}{3} \approx 0.333$.
- **Like denominators: combine the numerators and leave the denominator alone.** $\frac{1}{x} + \frac{2}{x} = \frac{3}{x}$ — not $\frac{3}{2x}$ and not $\frac{2}{x^2}$, because you are counting three of the same unit. Likewise $\frac{3}{x+1} - \frac{1}{x+1} = \frac{2}{x+1}$. With subtraction, bracket the whole numerator you are subtracting: $\frac{x+3}{x-1} - \frac{2x}{x-1} = \frac{(x+3) - 2x}{x-1} = \frac{3-x}{x-1}$.
- **Unlike denominators: build the LCD from the factored denominators, then rescale each fraction.** The LCD is the least common *multiple*, so shared factors are used once. For $\frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{2}$ it is $2x$: rewrite as $\frac{2}{2x} + \frac{x}{2x} = \frac{x+2}{2x}$, and at $x = 2$ both sides give $0.5 + 0.5 = 1$. ✓ For $\frac{1}{x^2-1} + \frac{1}{x+1}$, factoring first shows the LCD is $(x-1)(x+1)$, not the product $(x^2-1)(x+1)$. Multiplying each fraction by a disguised 1 such as $\frac{x}{x}$ changes its appearance, never its value.
- **Solving rational equations: multiply *both sides* by the LCD to clear denominators, then solve the polynomial equation that survives.** Multiplying by a nonzero quantity preserves equivalence, so $\frac{1}{x} = 3$ becomes $1 = 3x$, giving $x = \frac{1}{3}$ — the reciprocal, not 3. Similarly $\frac{1}{x-2} = \frac{1}{3}$ cross-multiplies to $3 = x-2$, so $x = 5$; the denominator $5-2 = 3$ is nonzero, so it stands. The catch is that the LCD is only nonzero *away* from the restrictions, so the cleared equation can carry solutions the original never had.
- **Extraneous solutions, and the check that catches them.** Solve $\frac{1}{x-1} + \frac{1}{x+1} = \frac{2}{x^2-1}$: multiplying by $(x-1)(x+1)$ gives $(x+1) + (x-1) = 2$, so $2x = 2$ and $x = 1$. But $x = 1$ makes two original denominators zero, so it is rejected and the equation has **no solution** — a case of **Number of solutions to equations** where the algebra is flawless and the solution set is still empty. Change that right-hand 2 to a 4 and the same steps give $x = 2$, which is legal and genuinely solves it. The rule is mechanical: every candidate goes back into the *original* denominators.
- **Modeling with rational equations: the unknown lands in a denominator whenever a rate is unknown.** Time equals distance over speed, so a 120-mile trip at constant speed $r$ taking 3 hours is $\frac{120}{r} = 3$, giving $r = 40$ mph. The units settle which model is right — miles ÷ (miles per hour) = hours — and they immediately kill $120 + r = 3$, $120r = 3$ and $\frac{r}{120} = 3$. Combined-work problems have the same shape: a pump filling a tank in 4 hours and another in 6 contribute $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{T}$, so $\frac{5}{12} = \frac{1}{T}$ and $T = 2.4$ hours — faster than either alone, which is the sanity check. Models add a second layer of restriction on top of the algebraic one: $r = 0$ is excluded by the denominator, and $r < 0$ by the physics.

**Rules to remember**

- Factor first. Nothing in this lesson — reducing, multiplying, dividing, adding, solving — works reliably on unfactored polynomials.
- Cancel factors, never terms. Canceling is division, so it only removes what multiplies the entire numerator and the entire denominator.
- State restrictions from the original denominator, before any cancellation.
- Multiply: straight across, cancelling across both fractions first. Divide: multiply by the reciprocal, and exclude the values that make the divisor zero.
- Add or subtract only over a common denominator. Never add denominators.
- Same denominator ⇒ combine numerators only, and bracket a subtracted numerator.
- The LCD is a least common multiple of the factored denominators, so a shared factor appears once.
- Clearing denominators can create extraneous roots; substituting back into the original is not optional.
- A candidate that makes any original denominator zero is rejected. If every candidate is rejected, the answer is "no solution".

**Common pitfalls**

- Cancelling a term: reading $\frac{x+4}{x}$ as 4, or $\frac{x^2+1}{x^2}$ as 1. Plug in $x = 1$ and watch it fail.
- Stating the restrictions from the simplified expression. $\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = x+3$ still needs $x \neq 3$; nothing in $x+3$ remembers that.
- Adding numerators and denominators: $\frac{1}{x+1} + \frac{1}{x-1} = \frac{2}{2x}$. That is not addition, and one numeric test exposes it.
- Multiplying the denominator too when combining like denominators — writing $\frac{1}{x} + \frac{2}{x}$ as $\frac{3}{2x}$.
- Dropping the bracket on a subtracted numerator, which flips the sign of every term after the first.
- Forgetting to exclude the zeros of the divisor's numerator when dividing.
- Inverting the wrong fraction, or inverting both, when dividing.
- Solving $\frac{1}{x} = 3$ as $x = 3$. Reciprocal, not copy.
- Accepting a solved value without checking it against the original denominators — the specific way a rational equation punishes correct algebra.
- Building an LCD by multiplying the denominators when they share a factor. Harmless but slower, and it hides cancellations you will need.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Factor every numerator and denominator before deciding anything. Most of these items become one line of cancellation once factored.
2. When a question asks which restrictions survive simplification, look only at the original denominator. "Only those visible in the simplified expression" is the standard distractor.
3. For a cancellation question, check whether the candidate is joined by multiplication or by addition. If it is a term, no cancellation is legal, whatever it looks like.
4. For addition and subtraction, the first move is always the common denominator — before any simplifying, factoring of numerators, or arithmetic.
5. Test with a number. Pick a legal value such as $x = 2$ or $x = 3$, evaluate the original and the candidate answer, and any mismatch settles the question in seconds.
6. For a solve, clear the denominators, solve, and then run every candidate through the original denominators. If the question asks what must "always be checked", it is that.
7. For a modeling item, write the defining relation in words first — time = distance ÷ speed, rate = job ÷ time — and let the units pick the equation. The distractors are the same numbers combined by the wrong operation.
