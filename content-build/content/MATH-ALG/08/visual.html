1. Factor Then Cancel

You already factor a difference of squares without thinking, and you already ask which inputs a formula is allowed to take. Reducing a rational expression is those two habits in one move — and the second one is where the marks are lost. Drag a and watch the hole appear. Like cancelling a shared ingredient from both sides of a recipe: the dish is unchanged, but it was still on the shopping list.

Read the restrictions off the original denominator, and read them before you cancel anything. Nothing in the simplified expression remembers the factor you divided out — which is exactly why the statement "x != a" has to be written by hand and carried forward.

2. Multiply And Divide

Multiplying rational expressions is "straight across, cancelling anything shared"; dividing is "flip the second one, then do that". Drag the numbers, then drag the test point onto a forbidden value and watch the expression stop existing. Like flipping a recipe from "per serving" to "servings per": you turn it upside down and multiply.

Cancel before you multiply out. Nothing forces you to expand (x - 1)(x + 1)(x + 2) and then hunt for the factors again — and on bigger problems the expanded form is where the sign errors live. The restrictions, though, are read from the expressions you started with.

3. Common Denominator

Adding and subtracting rational expressions is the one place where the rules of numeric fractions bite hardest: you cannot combine two fractions until they are counted in the same unit. Drag the denominators together and apart, and watch the naive "add the tops, add the bottoms" answer be wrong by a measurable amount. Like adding thirds to fifths: nothing happens until both are fifteenths.

When the denominators share a factor the LCD is the least common multiple, not the product: 1/(x^2 - 1) and 1/(x + 1) live over (x - 1)(x + 1), not over (x^2 - 1)(x + 1). Multiplying the denominators always works and is sometimes twice the algebra — factor first and you see which.

4. Clear The Denominators

Multiplying both sides by the LCD turns a rational equation into an ordinary one — and quietly hands you solutions the original equation never had. Drag until the answer lands on a forbidden value and watch a perfectly correct calculation produce nothing at all. Like a key that opens the door but snaps in the lock: the step worked, the answer is still no good.

The check is not politeness, it is part of the method. Multiplying by the LCD is only an equivalence away from the excluded values, so every candidate has to be pushed back through the original denominators before it may be called a solution — and when the last candidate falls, the honest answer is "no solution".

5. Miles Per Hour

Modeling puts the unknown in the denominator whenever the thing you do not know is a rate. Drag the distance and the time, and watch three plausible-looking equations fail the same test the right one passes. Like reading a recipe's "serves 4": the number you want is hiding underneath the division bar.

Two layers of restriction live in every model. The algebra forbids r = 0 because it is a denominator; the situation forbids r < 0 because nothing drives at a negative speed. A candidate that clears the first test can still fail the second, and only reading the words tells you so.
