**The big idea**: **Intercepts and Slope** taught you to turn two points into two differences, $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ and $\Delta y = y_2 - y_1$. **Multiplying binomials and special products** taught you that $(x - h)^2 = x^2 - 2hx + h^2$. **Solving quadratics by square roots, factoring, and completing the square** taught you how to force a scattered quadratic into a perfect square, and **Vertex form and features of quadratic graphs** taught you why that is worth the trouble: once a quadratic is a square plus a constant, its features are readable by inspection. This lesson spends those four skills on geometry and introduces almost nothing new. Two ideas carry all of it. First, every measurement you can make from two points is built from the same $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ — divide them and you have slope, square them and add and take a root and you have **distance**, average the raw coordinates instead and you have the **midpoint**. Second, a circle is a distance statement: "every point exactly $r$ away from $(h, k)$". So the standard equation of a circle is the distance formula with the square root squared away, and completing the square is the only tool you need to recover $(h, k)$ and $r$ when the equation arrives expanded.

**Key concepts**

- **The distance formula is the Pythagorean theorem with the legs written as coordinate differences.** Drop a horizontal segment from $(x_1, y_1)$ and a vertical one to $(x_2, y_2)$; they meet at the corner $(x_2, y_1)$ at a right angle. The legs have lengths $|\Delta x|$ and $|\Delta y|$, so $c^2 = (\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2$ and $d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$. From $(0,0)$ to $(3,4)$: $\Delta x = 3$, $\Delta y = 4$, $9 + 16 = 25$, $d = \sqrt{25} = 5$. **The square root is there because it inverts the Pythagorean theorem** — Pythagoras hands you $c^2$, and you wanted $c$. Stop at 25 and you have reported an *area* where a *length* was asked for.
- **Squaring destroys sign, so order never matters.** $(x_2 - x_1)^2 = (x_1 - x_2)^2$, which is why you may subtract in either direction and why the formula needs no absolute value bars. From $(-1, 3)$ to $(5, -1)$: $\Delta x = 6$, $\Delta y = -4$, giving $36 + 16 = 52$ and $d = \sqrt{52} = 2\sqrt{13} \approx 7.21$ — exactly the kind of tidy-up you practised in **Simplifying square-root expressions**. Reversing the points gives the same 52.
- **The midpoint is an average of coordinates, not a difference.** $M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$: average the $x$'s, average the $y$'s, and you have **the point halfway between the endpoints** — the unique point on the segment whose distance to each endpoint is $d/2$. For $(2,6)$ and $(8,10)$: $\frac{2+8}{2} = 5$ and $\frac{6+10}{2} = 8$, so $M = (5, 8)$. Verify with the other formula: $(2,6) \to (5,8)$ is $\sqrt{9 + 4} = \sqrt{13}$, and $(5,8) \to (8,10)$ is $\sqrt{9 + 4} = \sqrt{13}$. Equal halves. ✓ Negatives are handled by the arithmetic, not by a special rule: $(-1, 3)$ and $(5, -1)$ give $\left(\frac{-1+5}{2}, \frac{3 + (-1)}{2}\right) = (2, 1)$.
- **Three different objects come out of the same two points, and confusing their types is what the distractors are made of.** Slope $\Delta y / \Delta x$ is a *ratio* (undefined when $\Delta x = 0$); distance is a single nonnegative *number*; the midpoint is an *ordered pair*. A midpoint is not the length of the segment, not the slope between the endpoints, and not an intercept — it is a location. Forget to halve the sums for $(2,6)$ and $(8,10)$ and you get $(10, 16)$, a point nowhere near the segment.
- **A circle is the locus of points at a fixed distance from a fixed point — write that sentence with the distance formula and square both sides.** "$(x, y)$ is $r$ away from $(h, k)$" is $\sqrt{(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2} = r$. Both sides are nonnegative, so squaring is safe and loses nothing: $(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2$. That is the **standard** form, and it is not a new formula — it is the distance formula with the root cleared.
- **Standard form hands you the center and the radius by inspection, with two sign traps.** The form subtracts the center coordinates, so a plus sign in the equation means a negative coordinate: $(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 16$ reads $(y + 2) = (y - (-2))$, giving center $(3, -2)$ — not $(-3, 2)$. And the right-hand side is $r^2$, not $r$, because it was built by squaring a length: $(x + 1)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 25$ has center $(-1, 4)$ and radius $\sqrt{25} = 5$, not 25. What you read off is the center and the radius; intercepts, area and circumference all require further work.
- **The two topics combine on a diameter.** If $(2,6)$ and $(8,10)$ are the endpoints of a diameter, the center is their midpoint $(5,8)$ and the radius is half their distance, $\sqrt{52}/2 = \sqrt{13}$, so the circle is $(x-5)^2 + (y-8)^2 = 13$. Note that $r^2 = 13$ is an integer even though $r$ is irrational — another reason standard form stores the square.
- **Expanded (general) form is what you get by multiplying out and collecting on one side.** Expand $(x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 = 9$ with the special products you already know: $x^2 - 4x + 4$ and $y^2 + 2y + 1$. Adding gives $x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y + 5 = 9$, and moving the 9 across gives $x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y - 4 = 0$. General form is $x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$ — everything on the left, zero on the right — so stopping at "$\dots - 4 = 9$" is not yet an answer. The cost of expanding is that the center and radius are now invisible.
- **Completing the square is the return trip, run once per variable.** Take $x^2 + y^2 + 6x - 8y + 9 = 0$. Group and move the constant: $(x^2 + 6x) + (y^2 - 8y) = -9$. Half of 6 is 3 and $3^2 = 9$; half of $-8$ is $-4$ and $(-4)^2 = 16$; add both to **both sides**: $(x+3)^2 + (y-4)^2 = -9 + 9 + 16 = 16$. Center $(-3, 4)$, radius 4. The original equation was a perfectly good circle all along — the only thing wrong with it was that its $x$ and $y$ terms had not yet been completed into squares.
- **Everything degenerate hides in the right-hand side after you complete.** That number is $r^2$. If it is positive you have a circle. If it is exactly 0 you have a single point: $(x-3)^2 + (y-1)^2 = 0$ forces both squares to vanish, so the graph is just $(3,1)$. If it is negative there are no real points at all, because a sum of two real squares cannot be negative — this is where **The imaginary unit i** would appear if you insisted on a radius. Complete the square *first*, then decide what you are looking at.
- **Recognising a circle in general form takes three checks.** The $x^2$ and $y^2$ coefficients must be equal, nonzero and the same sign, and there must be no $xy$ term. If the equal coefficients are not 1, divide the whole equation through before completing: $2x^2 + 2y^2 - 8x = 0$ becomes $x^2 + y^2 - 4x = 0$, then $(x-2)^2 + y^2 = 4$.

**Rules to remember**

- $d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$ — Pythagoras on the legs $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$. The root is there to invert the squaring.
- $M = \left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$ — **add and halve**. Distance subtracts; the midpoint averages.
- Distance is a number, the midpoint is a point, slope is a ratio. Never answer one with another.
- Subtraction order is free in the distance formula and irrelevant in the midpoint formula; both are symmetric in the two points.
- Standard form: $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$. Center $(h, k)$, radius $r$, and the signs in the equation are the *opposite* of the center's coordinates.
- The constant on the right is $r^2$. Take its square root before calling it a radius.
- Expanded form is $x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$ — all terms on one side, equal to zero.
- To un-expand: group $x$ terms and $y$ terms, take half of each linear coefficient, square it, and add it to both sides.
- After completing, $r^2 > 0$ is a circle, $r^2 = 0$ is one point, $r^2 < 0$ is no real graph.

**Common pitfalls**

- Reporting $d^2$ as the distance. For $(0,0)$ and $(3,4)$ the answer is 5; 25 is the square, and 7 is the two legs added as if you could walk diagonally by walking two sides.
- Subtracting to find a midpoint, or adding without halving. $(2,6)$ and $(8,10)$ give $(5,8)$, never $(10,16)$ and never $(6,4)$.
- Mishandling negatives inside the averages. $\frac{3 + (-1)}{2} = 1$, not 2 — write the sum out before dividing.
- Flipping the sign wrongly when reading a center. $(x - 3)$ means $h = +3$; $(y + 2)$ means $k = -2$.
- Calling the right-hand side the radius. In $(x+1)^2 + (y-4)^2 = 25$ the radius is 5.
- Expanding but leaving the constant on the right, or forgetting a term of $(x-h)^2 = x^2 - 2hx + h^2$ — the middle term is $-2h$, not $-h$.
- Adding the completing constant to only one side of the equation. Whatever you add on the left must be added on the right, or you have changed the circle.
- Declaring an equation "not a circle" because it is expanded. Only the completed right-hand side can tell you that.

**How to approach the questions**

1. If a question gives you two points, decide first *what type* the answer is. A point means midpoint (add and halve); a single number means distance (subtract, square, add, root); "how steep" means slope.
2. For "why does the distance formula have a square root", answer from the derivation: the formula is the Pythagorean theorem solved for the hypotenuse, so the root undoes the squares. Nothing about averaging or slope is involved.
3. For a conceptual midpoint question, say what it *is*: the point halfway between the endpoints. Options naming the segment's length, its slope, or an intercept are describing other objects entirely.
4. Given standard form, read $h$, $k$ and $r^2$ straight off, then flip both signs and take one square root. Two of the four options are usually the unflipped signs and the un-rooted radius.
5. To expand, square each binomial in full, add, collect, and drive the whole thing to zero. Then check one coefficient against the options — the linear coefficients $-2h$ and $-2k$ separate them fastest.
6. If an equation is in general form, complete the square before answering anything about center, radius, or even whether it is a circle. If a question asks only *why* it is not in standard form, the answer is that its $x$ and $y$ terms are not yet completed into squares.
