1. Same Two Differences

Intercepts and Slope already had you build dx and dy from two points and divide them. Keep the same two numbers and do something else with them: square-add-root for distance, or average the raw coordinates for the midpoint. Drag the points and watch all three answers move together. Like two street corners on a map: how steep the road is, how far apart they are, and where you would meet in the middle are three different questions about one pair of pins.

already taught
dx = x2 - x1 dy = y2 - y1 slope = dy / dx (a ratio)
this lesson
distance = sqrt(dx^2 + dy^2) (a length) midpoint = ((x1+x2)/2, (y1+y2)/2) (a point)

Watch the types, because that is what the distractors trade on. The midpoint is a location, the distance is a single nonnegative number, and the slope is a ratio that can fail to exist. A question asking "what does the midpoint represent" is answered by the point halfway between the endpoints — never by the segment's length and never by its slope.

2. Undoing Pythagoras

Pythagoras is a statement about areas: the two small squares fill the big one. But the question asked for a length, so one step is left over. Drag the legs and watch the three tempting numbers stay wrong while only the root comes out right. Like being told a room is 25 square metres when you asked how wide it is.

The formula never needs absolute value bars, and the reason is on screen: the squares are built from dx and dy, and a square is blind to sign. That is also why you may subtract the points in either order. The only thing the square root can ever return here is a nonnegative number — a distance of −5 is not a wrong answer, it is not an answer.

3. Center And Radius

A circle is one sentence: "every point exactly r from (h, k)". Square both sides of that distance statement and you have the standard equation. Drag the center, the radius and a test point, and watch the equation and the in/on/out verdict follow. Like a compass: pin the point, set the span, and the equation is just a written record of both.

Two traps live in this one line, and both are sign-or-power slips rather than conceptual ones. The form subtracts the center, so a visible plus means a negative coordinate; and the right-hand side is r², not r, because it was manufactured by squaring a length. What standard form gives you directly is the center and the radius — intercepts, area and circumference are all further work.

4. Complete The Square

Expanded form hides the center and the radius; completing the square is how you get them back, once per variable. Set the coefficients of the general form, then run the trip in either direction and check that you land where you started. Like a packed suitcase: nothing has been lost, but you cannot see what is in there until you unpack it.

Notice where the judgment lives: not in the equation's appearance but in the number the right-hand side becomes after you complete. Positive is a circle, zero is a single point, negative is no real graph at all. An expanded equation is never "not a circle" merely because it is expanded — you cannot know until you have finished the square.
