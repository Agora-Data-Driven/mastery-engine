**The big idea**: **Factoring quadratics (intro, grouping, difference of squares, perfect squares)** taught you to run multiplication backwards, and **Multiplying binomials and special products** gave you the forward patterns that make that possible. **Solving quadratics by square roots, factoring, and completing the square** then spent the skill on a purpose: a product set equal to zero hands you the roots for free. This lesson raises the degree and changes remarkably little. Adding and subtracting higher-degree polynomials is like-term bookkeeping that never touches an exponent; factoring higher-degree polynomials is the same reverse multiplication with two new patterns — the difference and the sum of cubes — plus one standing instruction: *keep going until nothing factors further*. The second half borrows a different old idea. **Intercepts and Slope** built slope as $\Delta y / \Delta x$ between two points on a line. Apply that identical quotient to two points on a *curve* and you have the **average rate of change** of a polynomial: the slope of the secant line joining them, and the only honest single number for "how fast did this thing change across this interval".

**Key concepts**

- **Adding and subtracting combine coefficients within a degree; an exponent is an address, not a quantity.** $ax^n + bx^n = (a+b)x^n$ is the distributive property read right to left, so only matching powers may merge. Worked: $(3x^3 + 2x - 1) + (x^3 - 5x + 4)$ has a degree-3 column ($3 + 1 = 4$), a degree-1 column ($2 + (-5) = -3$) and a constant column ($-1 + 4 = 3$), giving $4x^3 - 3x + 3$. The tempting $4x^6$ comes from adding the exponents, which is what **Exponent properties (integer exponents)** reserves for *multiplication*: $x^3 \cdot x^3 = x^6$, while $x^3 + x^3 = 2x^3$.
- **Subtraction distributes the minus sign across every term of the second polynomial, not just the first.** $(3x^3 + 2x - 1) - (x^3 - 5x + 4) = 3x^3 + 2x - 1 - x^3 + 5x - 4 = 2x^3 + 7x - 5$. Two of those three signs flip, and missing them is the most common arithmetic failure at any degree. Note also that the degree can *drop*: $(x^3 + 2x) - (x^3 - 5)$ is $2x + 5$, degree 1, because the leading terms annihilated. Adding or subtracting never raises the degree above the larger input, and sometimes lowers it.
- **Factoring is worth doing before solving because of the zero-product property.** If a product of real numbers is zero, then at least one factor is zero — a conclusion that is only available once the equation reads "something $= 0$". So $x^3 - 4x = 0$ becomes $x(x-2)(x+2) = 0$ and splits into three one-step equations: $x = 0$, $x = 2$, $x = -2$. Factoring does not reduce the degree to 1, eliminate constants, or guarantee positive roots; it converts one hard equation into several trivial ones. And it needs the zero — from $(x-2)(x+2) = 5$ you may conclude nothing about either factor.
- **The difference and sum of cubes are the two new patterns, and one expansion proves both.** $a^3 - b^3 = (a-b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)$ and $a^3 + b^3 = (a+b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)$. Derive the first rather than memorising it: $(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2) = a^3 + a^2b + ab^2 - a^2b - ab^2 - b^3$, and the four middle terms cancel in two pairs, leaving $a^3 - b^3$. That cancellation is the entire reason the trinomial's middle sign is the *opposite* of the binomial's — and the reason the trinomial itself never factors further.
- **Reading a cube pattern off a real expression.** $x^3 - 8$ is $a = x$, $b = 2$, so it is a difference of cubes: not a difference of squares (the exponents are odd), not a perfect-square trinomial (it has two terms), and not a quadratic-formula problem (degree 3). Likewise $x^3 - 27$ has $b = 3$ and factors as $(x-3)(x^2 + 3x + 9)$. Several traps sit inside that one answer: the binomial takes the minus, the trinomial is all plus, its constant is $b^2 = 9$ rather than $3$, and its middle coefficient is $ab = 3$. Multiply back to confirm — $x^3 + 3x^2 + 9x - 3x^2 - 9x - 27 = x^3 - 27$.
- **"Factor completely" means keep applying patterns until every factor is irreducible.** $x^4 - 16 = (x^2)^2 - 4^2 = (x^2 - 4)(x^2 + 4)$ by difference of squares — and $x^2 - 4$ is *itself* a difference of squares, so the finished answer is $(x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)$. Stopping one step early yields a true factorization that is not the complete one, which is exactly how that distractor is built.
- **A sum of squares does not factor over the reals, and that is where the process stops.** $x^2 + 4$ would need two real numbers multiplying to 4 and adding to 0, which is impossible; over the complex numbers **The imaginary unit i** makes it $(x-2i)(x+2i)$, but "completely" here means over the reals. Sanity-check with degrees: the factors' degrees must sum to the original degree, and $1 + 1 + 2 = 4$. Degree accounting catches omissions but not errors — $(x-2)^2(x+2)^2$ also sums to 4, yet it expands to $x^4 - 8x^2 + 16$, so multiply back when unsure.
- **The average rate of change of $f$ on $[a,b]$ is $\dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$: the slope of the secant line through $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$.** It is $\Delta y / \Delta x$ from **Intercepts and Slope** verbatim; the only difference is that the two points now sit on a curve, so the answer depends on which interval you picked. Dividing is what turns a total into a rate: $f(b) - f(a)$ on its own is net change measured in output units, and a rise of 8 across a width of 2 is not the same rate as a rise of 8 across a width of 20.
- **Evaluate both endpoints first, then subtract — the negatives are where answers go wrong.** For $f(x) = x^3 - 1$ on $[0,2]$: $f(2) = 8 - 1 = 7$ and $f(0) = 0 - 1 = -1$, so the rate is $\frac{7 - (-1)}{2 - 0} = \frac{8}{2} = 4$. Drop the double negative and you get $6/2 = 3$, which is one of the offered options. For $f(x) = x^2$ on $[1,3]$: $\frac{9 - 1}{3 - 1} = 4$. For $f(x) = 2x^2 + x$ on $[-1,1]$: $f(1) = 3$ and $f(-1) = 2(-1)^2 + (-1) = 1$, so $\frac{3 - 1}{1 - (-1)} = \frac{2}{2} = 1$ — the denominator is $b - a = 2$, never $a + b = 0$, which would not even be defined.
- **A secant slope is not a tangent slope, not a maximum, and not a midpoint output.** Recall from calculus that $f'(a)$ is the instantaneous rate at a single point — the limit of this very quotient as $b \to a$. On $f(x) = x^2$ over $[1,3]$ the average rate of change is 4, while $f'(1) = 2$ and $f'(3) = 6$: it lies between them and equals neither. It is likewise not the largest output the function reaches, and not $f$ evaluated at the interval's midpoint. (For a quadratic it does coincide with $f'$ at the midpoint — here $f'(2) = 4$ — but that is a property of parabolas, not the definition.)
- **Sign and vanishing.** A negative average rate of change means a net decrease across the interval; zero means the two endpoint outputs are equal, not that the polynomial stood still. On $f(x) = x^2$ over $[-2,2]$ the value is $\frac{4-4}{4} = 0$ while the curve falls to the origin and climbs back. A secant reports only the net effect of its two endpoints, which is why higher-degree polynomials have to be measured interval by interval.

**Rules to remember**

- Combine like terms only: same variable, same exponent. Adding never changes an exponent.
- Subtracting a polynomial means distributing $-1$ over all of its terms, not just the leading one.
- The zero-product property needs a product on one side and a zero on the other.
- $a^3 - b^3 = (a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)$ and $a^3+b^3 = (a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)$: opposite middle sign, constant $b^2$.
- A difference of squares factors; a sum of squares does not, over the reals.
- Keep factoring until every factor is irreducible, then check that the factors' degrees sum to the original degree.
- Average rate of change $= \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$: subtract the outputs, subtract the inputs, divide. Never add either pair.
- That quotient is a secant slope. The tangent slope $f'(a)$ is its limit as $b \to a$, and not the same number.

**Common pitfalls**

- Adding exponents while adding terms: $3x^3 + x^3 = 4x^3$, never $4x^6$.
- Dropping the sign flip on every term after the first inside a subtracted polynomial.
- Writing $(x-3)(x^2 - 3x + 9)$ or $(x+3)(x^2 - 3x + 9)$ for $x^3 - 27$. The binomial carries the minus and the trinomial is all plus.
- Ending $x^4 - 16$ at $(x^2-4)(x^2+4)$ and calling it complete — or, worse, "factoring" $x^2 + 4$ into $(x+2)^2$.
- Believing that factoring lowers the degree or forces the roots positive. It rearranges; the degree and the roots were always there.
- Reporting $f(b) - f(a)$ as the rate. That is total change, and it carries the wrong units.
- Dividing by $a + b$, or by $a - b$ after subtracting the outputs as $f(b) - f(a)$ — that flips the sign of a correct magnitude.
- Naming the average rate of change as the tangent slope at $x = a$, the function's maximum, or its midpoint value. Those are the three wrong options on the conceptual question.

**How to approach the questions**

1. On any add-or-subtract question, stack the polynomials by degree and work one column at a time. Then scan the exponents in the options — a wrong exponent is the fastest distractor to eliminate.
2. For "which strategy fits", count the terms and read the exponents before anything else. Two terms with odd exponents and a minus sign is a difference of cubes; two terms with even exponents and a minus is a difference of squares; three terms may be a perfect square; any degree above 2 rules the quadratic formula out at once.
3. Having factored a cube, check the trinomial's middle sign and its constant against $ab$ and $b^2$ before reading the options. Two of the four choices usually differ from the truth in exactly one of those two places.
4. When a question says "completely", attempt to factor each factor you produced, and stop only at linear factors and irreducible quadratics such as a sum of squares.
5. For an average rate of change, write $f(b)$ and $f(a)$ as plain numbers on their own lines before combining, and parenthesise every negative. $7 - (-1)$ and $1 - (-1)$ are where the wrong options are manufactured.
6. For a conceptual rate question, state what the object *is* — the slope of the secant line through the two endpoints — and reject each option describing a tangent, a maximum, or a single output value.

**Where this leads**: adding, subtracting and factoring higher-degree polynomials all treated a polynomial as an expression to be rearranged, while the average rate of change treated it as a function sampled at two points. The next lesson, **11 Advanced function types**, leans entirely on that second view: absolute value and piecewise functions, composite functions, and verifying inverse functions. It builds new functions out of the ones you have rather than new expressions, and the habit of evaluating $f$ cleanly at a named input does most of the work.
