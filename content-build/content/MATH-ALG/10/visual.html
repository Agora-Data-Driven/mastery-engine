1. Stack By Degree

Adding and subtracting quadratics was already column bookkeeping: like terms merge and nothing else moves. Raise the degree and that rule does not change at all, because an exponent is only the name of the column an amount is filed under. Slide the six coefficients and watch the columns total. Like a receipt that keeps euros and yen on separate lines: you can add within a line forever, but no amount of adding ever turns a euro into a yen.

already taught — degree 2
(2x^2 + 3x) + (5x^2 - x) = 7x^2 + 2x
this lesson — any degree
(3x^3 + 2x - 1) + (x^3 - 5x + 4) = 4x^3 - 3x + 3

Notice the x^2 column, which never leaves zero here. A missing term is not a missing column — it is a coefficient of 0, and writing it in is what keeps the arithmetic in line. That habit pays later: the moment you divide polynomials or read off a leading coefficient, the invisible zeros are exactly the ones that get miscounted.

2. Why Cubes Cancel

The cube patterns are not two more formulas to memorise — they are one multiplication whose middle four terms destroy each other in pairs. Change the number being cubed, flip the sign, and watch which candidate factorization survives being multiplied back out. Like a tug-of-war where the two inner teams are exactly matched: whatever they pull, only the outer two ever move the rope.

The trinomial that comes out of a cube pattern never factors again, and you can see why without trying: its discriminant is b^2 - 4b^2 = -3b^2, negative for every b except 0. So a correctly factored cube is already completely factored — which is exactly the judgment visual 3 is about.

3. Factor Until It Stops

"Factor completely" is an instruction with a stopping rule, and most lost marks are one step short of it. Pick an expression and keep pressing the button: every press applies one real pattern to one factor, and the roots appear by the zero-product property as the linear factors fall out. Like splitting logs — you keep swinging until every piece is too small to split, not until the first piece comes off.

The zero-product property is the reason any of this is worth doing, and it has a precondition people skip: the equation must read something = 0. From (x - 2)(x + 2) = 5 you may conclude nothing at all about either factor, because plenty of pairs of numbers multiply to 5. Set it to zero first, then factor, then read the roots off one bracket at a time.

4. Slide The Secant

Average rate of change is the slope you already know, measured between two points that happen to sit on a curve instead of a line. Drag the endpoints and watch the secant tilt, the arithmetic rewrite itself, and the three tempting near-misses stay stubbornly different numbers. Like a road trip: your average speed is the whole distance over the whole time, and it says nothing about the speedometer at any single moment.

Push b towards a and watch the secant lie down on the tangent: the average rate of change over a shrinking interval is what the instantaneous rate is defined as the limit of. They are the same construction at two scales — which is why answering a secant question with f'(a) is not a random error but the right idea one limit too early.
