**The big idea**: **Exponent properties (integer exponents)** gave you a count of repeated factors and three rules for bookkeeping on that count; **Simplifying square-root expressions** had you dragging perfect squares out from under a radical; **Solving rational equations and modeling** taught you that a legal step can manufacture an answer the original equation never had; and **Graphing exponential growth and decay** moved the variable up into the exponent. This lesson fuses all four, and it has exactly two moves. First, a fractional exponent is not new notation to memorise — it is the *only* meaning that keeps $(x^m)^n = x^{mn}$ true when the exponents stop being whole numbers, and once you accept it, every property you already own extends verbatim and radicals simply become exponents. Second, once the unknown sits under a radical or up in an exponent, solving means undoing an operation that may not be reversible: squaring both sides is a one-way street that can invent roots, while matching bases is safe because $b^x$ never repeats a value.

**Key concepts**

- **$x^{1/2} = \sqrt{x}$ is forced, not decreed.** Mechanism: insist that the power-of-a-power rule keeps working for fractions. Then $(x^{1/2})^2 = x^{(1/2)\cdot 2} = x^1 = x$, so $x^{1/2}$ is a number whose square is $x$ — and for $x \ge 0$ the principal (nonnegative) one is $\sqrt{x}$. Numbers: $9^{1/2} = 3$, because $3^2 = 9$. Note what it is *not*, all evaluated at $x = 9$: $2x = 18$, $x^2 = 81$, $\frac{1}{2x} = \frac{1}{18}$. Only one of those four squares back to $9$, and that decides the question without any manipulation.
- **The general rule: $x^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{x}\right)^{m} = \sqrt[n]{x^{m}}$ — the denominator is the root index, the numerator is the power.** Mechanism: $x^{m/n} = (x^{1/n})^m$ by that same rule, and $x^{1/n}$ is the $n$-th root because raising it to the $n$-th power returns $x$. Why it matters: it retires the separate rulebook radicals used to need. Numbers: $8^{2/3} = (\sqrt[3]{8})^2 = 2^2 = 4$, and the other order $\sqrt[3]{8^2} = \sqrt[3]{64} = 4$ agrees — take the root first and the arithmetic stays small. Likewise $16^{3/4} = 2^3 = 8$ and $32^{1/5} = 2$.
- **Rational exponents and properties: every integer-exponent rule survives unchanged.** Product: $x^{1/2} \cdot x^{3/2} = x^{1/2 + 3/2} = x^{2}$. Verify at $x = 4$: $4^{1/2} = 2$ and $4^{3/2} = 8$, and $2 \cdot 8 = 16 = 4^2$. Power of a power: $(x^{2/3})^{3} = x^{(2/3) \cdot 3} = x^{2}$ — the exponents multiply, and adding them instead is where the wrong answer $x^{5/3}$ comes from. Quotient: $x^{7/2} / x^{1/2} = x^{3}$. Product of bases: $(ab)^r = a^r b^r$, so $(4 \cdot 9)^{1/2} = 36^{1/2} = 6 = 2 \cdot 3$.
- **Know which stated rule is real, because three convincing fakes travel beside them.** $(a^m)^n = a^{m+n}$ is false: at $a = 2$, $m = 3$, $n = 2$ the left side is $64$ and the right is $32$. $a^{m+n} = a^m + a^n$ is false: $2^{1+2} = 8$ while $2^1 + 2^2 = 6$ — exponents never distribute over a sum. $\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{mn}$ is false: $2^5/2^2 = 8 = 2^3$, not $2^{10}$. The one that holds for all positive bases $a$, $b$ and all real exponents $r$ is $(ab)^r = a^r b^r$.
- **A negative rational exponent inverts, then roots.** $x^{-m/n} = \dfrac{1}{x^{m/n}}$, so $\dfrac{1}{x^{1/2}} = x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ in radical notation. Test the near-misses at $x = 4$, where the target is $\tfrac{1}{2}$: $\sqrt{x} = 2$, $x\sqrt{x} = 8$, and $\sqrt{\frac{1}{x^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{16}} = \frac{1}{4}$. Only $\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ lands on $\frac12$. More: $8^{-2/3} = \frac14$ and $16^{-1/2} = \frac14$. A negative exponent inverts the value; it never makes it negative.
- **The domain conditions are mathematics, not fine print.** An even root needs a nonnegative radicand to stay real — recall **The imaginary unit i**, where $\sqrt{-4} = 2i$ left the real numbers entirely — while an odd root is happy with negatives: $(-27)^{1/3} = -3$. And over the reals $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$, not $x$, so $\sqrt{(-5)^2} = 5$; that is the same nonnegativity you enforced in **Absolute value equations**. This is why a question about $x^{1/2}$ says "for $x \ge 0$" before it says anything else.
- **Solving square-root equations: isolate, square, solve, check.** Mechanism: squaring undoes a principal square root, but only once the radical stands alone — everything else on that side must be moved first. Numbers: $\sqrt{x+9} = 4$ squares to $x + 9 = 16$, giving $x = 7$; the check is $\sqrt{7+9} = \sqrt{16} = 4$. With $\sqrt{x+1} = 5$ the correct step once the radical is isolated is to square both sides — not to add $1$ first, not to multiply by $x$ — giving $x + 1 = 25$, so $x = 24$ and $\sqrt{25} = 5$.
- **Why the check is compulsory: squaring is not reversible.** Mechanism: $a = b$ forces $a^2 = b^2$, but $a^2 = b^2$ only forces $a = \pm b$. Squaring therefore solves your equation *and* its sign-flipped twin simultaneously, and any root belonging only to the twin is **extraneous**. Numbers: $\sqrt{x} = -3$ has no solution, since a principal square root is never negative — yet squaring gives $x = 9$, and $\sqrt{9} = 3 \neq -3$. Harder: $\sqrt{x+7} = x - 5$ squares to $x^2 - 11x + 18 = 0$, so $x = 9$ or $x = 2$. Then $x = 9$ checks ($\sqrt{16} = 4 = 9 - 5$) and $x = 2$ fails ($\sqrt{9} = 3$, but $2 - 5 = -3$). The algebra was flawless at every line; the extra root is a property of the operation, exactly like the excluded values in **Solving rational equations and modeling**.
- **Solving exponential equations by matching bases.** Mechanism: write both sides as powers of one base, then set the exponents equal. That last step is legal because $y = b^x$ with $b > 0$ and $b \neq 1$ is one-to-one — recall **Graphing exponential growth and decay**, where every unit step multiplies by a constant ratio, so the curve rises (or falls) forever and never returns to a value it has already taken. Numbers: $2^x = 16 = 2^4$, so $x = 4$. And $3^{x+1} = 27 = 3^3$ gives $x + 1 = 3$, so $x = 2$ — once the bases match, what is left is a linear equation, and the answer is rarely the exponent you first read off.
- **When the two bases differ, rewrite them — and rational exponents are what let you.** $4^x = 8$ becomes $2^{2x} = 2^3$, so $x = \frac32$; check it with the first half of this lesson: $4^{3/2} = (\sqrt{4})^3 = 8$. Likewise $8^x = 2$ becomes $2^{3x} = 2^1$, so $x = \frac13$, which is just $8^{1/3} = 2$ read backwards. The two halves of this lesson are one fact seen from two sides. The exclusion $b \neq 1$ earns its place here: $1^x = 1$ for every $x$, so $1^x = 1$ has infinitely many solutions and $1^x = 5$ has none.
- **Some exponential equations refuse to match, and that is the boundary of the method.** $2^x = 10$ has no common base: $2^3 = 8$ and $2^4 = 16$, so the answer is trapped between $3$ and $4$ and no rewriting will pin it down. The tool for that arrives in the next lesson.

**Rules to remember**

- $x^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{x}$ and $x^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{x}\right)^m$: the denominator roots, the numerator powers.
- Every integer-exponent property holds for rational exponents: multiplying adds them, nesting multiplies them, dividing subtracts them.
- $(ab)^r = a^r b^r$ holds for all positive bases and all real $r$. Exponents never distribute over a sum.
- $x^{-m/n} = 1/x^{m/n}$. A negative exponent inverts; it does not negate and it does not halve.
- Even roots need a nonnegative radicand over the reals; odd roots do not. $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$.
- Square-root equations: isolate the radical, square once, solve, then substitute every candidate back into the original.
- A candidate is extraneous exactly when the two sides come out with opposite signs.
- Exponential equations: rewrite both sides on one base, equate the exponents, solve what remains.
- $b^u = b^v \Rightarrow u = v$ needs $b > 0$ and $b \neq 1$.

**Common pitfalls**

- Reading $x^{1/2}$ as $2x$, as $x^2$, or as $\frac{1}{2x}$. It is a root, and the exponent's denominator is the index.
- Flipping the fraction: $8^{2/3} = 4$ is the cube root squared, while $8^{3/2} = 22.6\ldots$ is something else entirely.
- Adding exponents when nesting: $(x^{2/3})^3$ is $x^2$, not $x^{5/3}$.
- Squaring before the radical is isolated. Squaring $\sqrt{x} + 3 = 7$ as it stands gives $x + 6\sqrt{x} + 9 = 49$ — the radical is still there, and now it is worse.
- Squaring a binomial term by term: $(x-5)^2$ is $x^2 - 10x + 25$, never $x^2 + 25$.
- Skipping the check. It is not tidiness; it is the second half of the method.
- Reading an extraneous root as an arithmetic slip and hunting for the mistake. There is no mistake to find.
- Missing that a negative right-hand side kills a square-root equation instantly: $\sqrt{u} = -4$ has no real solution whatever squaring produces.
- Equating exponents before the bases match. From $4^x = 8$, both "$x = 8/4$" and "$x = 3$" come from skipping the rewrite.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Convert every radical to a rational exponent first. One notation, one set of rules, and the "rewrite in radical notation" questions then run backwards just as easily.
2. For "which rule is valid", test at $a = 2$, $b = 3$, $m = 3$, $n = 2$. A single numeric test can refute a false rule outright; it cannot confirm a true one, so pick the survivor and check it against the derivation.
3. For a "rewrite this expression" question, evaluate the expression and every option at one convenient value such as $x = 4$. Whichever option agrees is the answer, with no manipulation at all.
4. For a square-root equation, isolate, square once, solve, then substitute back — and glance at the sign of the right-hand side before you trust anything.
5. For "why must each candidate be checked", the answer names the operation: squaring can introduce extraneous roots. Distractors blame the radical instead ("every radical equation gives two answers", "radicals produce only negative roots"); the fault lies in the squaring.
6. For an exponential equation, express both sides as powers of the smallest base in sight, then equate exponents. If the exponent is a shift such as $x+1$, remember to solve that leftover equation too.

**Where this leads**: matching bases only works while both sides are powers of a common base, which is why $2^x = 10$ stopped this lesson dead.
