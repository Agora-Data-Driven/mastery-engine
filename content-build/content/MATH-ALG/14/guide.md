**The big idea**: **Graphing exponential growth and decay** handed you a machine that turns an exponent into a value, and **Introduction to inverse functions** together with **Verifying inverse functions** handed you the idea of running a machine backwards. A logarithm is that backwards machine and nothing else: $\log_b(x)$ is the exponent you have to put on $b$ to get $x$. Three consequences carry the whole lesson. First, evaluating a logarithm is a *question*, not a calculation — you ask "$b$ to the what?" and read the answer off. Second, every logarithm property below is one of the **Exponent properties (integer exponents)** or **Rational exponents and properties** you already own, read from the other side — which is why you can rederive any of them in one line if you forget. Third, this is the tool **Solving square-root and exponential equations** was missing: a logarithm is the only way to bring a variable *down* out of an exponent, and that turns an exponential model from something you evaluate into something you can solve.

**Key concepts**

- **Introduction to logarithms and evaluation: a logarithm is a question about an exponent.** The mechanism is a definition, not a procedure: for $b > 0$ with $b \neq 1$ and $x > 0$, $\log_b(x)$ is the unique exponent $c$ such that $b^c = x$. So evaluation is a search you run in your head. Worked: $\log_2(8)$ asks "2 to the what gives 8?" — $2^3 = 8$, so the answer is **3**, not 8 and not 2. $\log_{10}(0.1)$ asks "10 to the what gives 0.1?" — $10^{-1} = 0.1$, so $-1$; a negative *output* is completely ordinary and just means the input sat below 1. "The exponent to which 10 must be raised to get 100" is precisely the expression $\log_{10}(100)$, and it equals 2. Two bases get their own notation because they are used constantly: $\log$ with no base written means base 10 (the common log), and $\ln$ means base $e \approx 2.718$ (the natural log), so $\ln(e^4) = 4$ by inspection. What breaks without this: a root and a logarithm both undo a power, but they undo *different slots*. $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$ solves $x^3 = 8$ and hands you the **base**; $\log_2(8) = 3$ solves $2^x = 8$ and hands you the **exponent**.
- **Relationship between exponentials and logs: one statement, two spellings.** $\log_b(a) = c$ and $b^c = a$ are the same claim about the same three numbers — a base, an exponent and a result. The reading rule that never fails: *the base of the log stays the base of the power, and the value of the log is the exponent.* Worked: $\log_3(81) = 4$ becomes $3^4 = 81$ — the 3 stays underneath, the 4 goes up top, the 81 is what comes out. Worked in the other direction: $10^x = 1000$ becomes $\log_{10}(1000) = x$, and $2^3 = 8$ becomes $\log_2(8) = 3$. And $\log_5(125) = 3$, because $5^3 = 125$. Every wrong option you are offered is one of those three numbers in the wrong slot, so naming the slots out loud is the entire defence.
- **The two functions are inverses, and that is where the domain and range come from.** Set $f(x) = b^x$ and $f^{-1}(x) = \log_b(x)$. **Composite functions** and **Verifying inverse functions** then give the two cancellation identities for free: $\log_b(b^x) = x$ and $b^{\log_b(x)} = x$. That is the one-step route to $\ln(e^4) = 4$. Inverses also swap **Domain and range**: $b^x$ accepts every real input and returns only positive outputs, so $\log_b(x)$ accepts only positive inputs and returns every real. Hence $\log_b(0)$ and $\log_b(-4)$ are undefined — no exponent makes a positive base produce zero or a negative. And, exactly as **Shifting and reflecting functions** predicts, the graph of $\log_b$ is the graph of $b^x$ reflected across the line $y = x$: since $b^0 = 1$ for every base, every logarithm curve passes through $(1, 0)$, and since $b^1 = b$, every one passes through $(b, 1)$.
- **Properties of logarithms and Change of Base formula: the exponent laws, re-read.** Derive them once and you never memorise them. Put $m = \log_b(x)$ and $n = \log_b(y)$, so $x = b^m$ and $y = b^n$. Then $xy = b^m b^n = b^{m+n}$, and reading that back as a logarithm gives the **product rule** $\log_b(xy) = m + n = \log_b(x) + \log_b(y)$. The same move on $b^m / b^n = b^{m-n}$ gives the **quotient rule** $\log_b\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_b(x) - \log_b(y)$, and on $(b^m)^p = b^{mp}$ gives the **power rule** $\log_b(x^p) = p\log_b(x)$. Worked condensing: $2\ln(x) - \ln(y)$ — the power rule pulls the 2 inside as an exponent, giving $\ln(x^2) - \ln(y)$, then the quotient rule closes it into $\ln\left(\frac{x^2}{y}\right)$. Worked expanding: $\log_3(x^2 y)$ — the product rule splits it into $\log_3(x^2) + \log_3(y)$, then the power rule finishes it as $2\log_3(x) + \log_3(y)$. Check it numerically once and you will trust it permanently: $\log_{10}(2 \cdot 5) = \log_{10}(10) = 1$, and $\log_{10}(2) + \log_{10}(5) \approx 0.301 + 0.699 = 1$.
- **Change of base turns any logarithm into one your calculator has.** The formula is $\log_b(x) = \frac{\log_a(x)}{\log_a(b)}$ for any legal new base $a$, and it derives in three lines: let $c = \log_b(x)$, so $b^c = x$; apply $\log_a$ to both sides and use the power rule to get $c \log_a(b) = \log_a(x)$; divide by $\log_a(b)$. Its purpose is exactly that and nothing grander — a calculator offers only $\log$ and $\ln$, so this evaluates a logarithm in *any* base using common or natural logs. Worked: $\log_2(7) = \frac{\ln(7)}{\ln(2)} \approx \frac{1.9459}{0.6931} \approx 2.807$, which you can sanity-check against $2^2 = 4$ and $2^3 = 8$, bracketing it strictly between 2 and 3. Note the shape: a **ratio of two logarithms**, target on top, old base underneath — never $\ln\left(\frac{7}{2}\right)$, never $\ln(7) - \ln(2)$, and never upside down.
- **Exponential model word problems live in the world of constant ratio.** A quantity changing by a fixed *percentage* each period is *multiplied* by a fixed factor each period, so after $t$ periods $A(t) = A_0(1 + r)^t$, where $A_0$ is the initial value and $r$ is the rate as a decimal. Growth of 8% is a factor of $1.08$; decay of 5% is a factor of $0.95$, which is $1 - 0.05$ and emphatically not $-0.05$. Worked: an investment of 2000 losing 5% a year is worth $2000(0.95) = 1900$ after one year — 5% of 2000 is 100, and $2000 - 100 = 1900$. A population starting at 500 and growing 10% a year is $P = 500(1.10)^t$. Doubling is just the case $1 + r = 2$: a culture of 12 cells doubling every hour is $12 \cdot 2^t$, so after 3 hours it is $12 \cdot 8 = 96$ — not $12 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 72$, which multiplies where the model compounds. The signature in a table of data is therefore **a constant ratio over equal intervals**; a linear model shows a constant *difference*, and a quadratic a constant *second* difference. Divide consecutive outputs, do not subtract them.
- **Logarithms are what solve those models for time.** Because $t$ sits in the exponent, no amount of dividing will free it; only a logarithm will. Worked: when does that population of 500 double? Solve $500(1.10)^t = 1000$, so $1.10^t = 2$; take $\ln$ of both sides and apply the power rule to get $t \ln(1.10) = \ln(2)$, hence $t = \frac{\ln(2)}{\ln(1.10)} \approx \frac{0.6931}{0.0953} \approx 7.3$ years. Notice what that expression is: $t = \log_{1.10}(2)$, evaluated by change of base. That formula is not a curiosity — it is how every "how long until…" question gets answered.

**Rules to remember**

- $\log_b(x) = c$ means exactly $b^c = x$. Same three numbers, different spelling.
- $\log$ with no base written means base 10; $\ln$ means base $e$.
- The cancellations: $\log_b(b^x) = x$ and $b^{\log_b(x)} = x$.
- Domain of $\log_b$ is $x > 0$; range is every real number. $\log_b(1) = 0$ and $\log_b(b) = 1$ for every legal base.
- Product: $\log_b(xy) = \log_b(x) + \log_b(y)$. Quotient: $\log_b\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_b(x) - \log_b(y)$. Power: $\log_b(x^p) = p\log_b(x)$.
- There is no rule for $\log_b(x + y)$. None exists.
- Change of base: $\log_b(x) = \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(b)} = \frac{\log(x)}{\log(b)}$ — a ratio, in that order.
- Percentage change compounds: $A(t) = A_0(1 + r)^t$, growth factor $1 + r$, decay factor $1 - r$.
- A table is exponential when consecutive outputs share a constant ratio, and linear when they share a constant difference.
- To solve for an exponent, take a logarithm of both sides and use the power rule.

**Common pitfalls**

- Answering $\log_2(8)$ with 8 or with 2. The value of a logarithm is the exponent: 3.
- Reading a negative result as an error. $\log_{10}(0.1) = -1$ is correct; it is the *input* that must be positive, never the output.
- Rewriting $\log_3(81) = 4$ as $3^{81} = 4$ or as $4^3 = 81$. The base stays the base; the log's value goes up top.
- Treating a logarithm as a root. A root returns the base; a logarithm returns the exponent.
- Inventing $\log(x + y) = \log(x) + \log(y)$. The product rule turns multiplication into addition — it does not turn addition into addition.
- Writing $\log(x^2) = 2 + \log(x)$. The power rule multiplies by the exponent; it never adds it.
- Writing $\log\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \frac{\log(x)}{\log(y)}$. A quotient *of* logarithms is change of base, an entirely different formula.
- Flipping change of base to $\frac{\ln(b)}{\ln(x)}$, or collapsing it into $\ln\left(\frac{x}{b}\right)$ or $\ln(x) - \ln(b)$.
- Modelling 8% growth as $P(0.08)^t$ — that is 92% decay a year — or as $P + 0.08t$, which is linear.
- Multiplying where the model compounds: 12 cells doubling for 3 hours is $12 \cdot 2^3 = 96$, not $12 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 72$.

**How to approach the questions**

1. On any evaluation, say the definition out loud — "$b$ to the what gives $x$?" — and write the exponential form underneath. Most of this bank falls to that single move.
2. On a rewrite question, label the three slots in the form you were given (base, exponent, result) and drop the same three numbers into the other form. Every distractor is a permutation of those three, so a labelled diagram beats recall.
3. Before expanding or condensing, name the rule you are using *and its direction*. A power inside becomes a coefficient outside, a product a sum, a quotient a difference.
4. Test any suspicious identity with easy numbers: base 10, $x = 100$, $y = 10$. Since $\log(110) \neq 2 + 1$, one substitution kills the entire family of invented rules.
5. For change of base, check the shape first — a fraction of two logarithms in the same new base, target on top — then bracket the answer between the two nearest integer powers of the old base.
6. In a word problem, extract $A_0$ and the factor $1 + r$ before doing anything else. Then decide which kind of question it is: a value at a known time (substitute), or a time for a known value (take logarithms).
