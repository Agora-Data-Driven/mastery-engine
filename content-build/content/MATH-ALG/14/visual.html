1. Undoing The Exponential

Graphing exponential growth and decay gave you a machine that turns an exponent into a value. Introduction to inverse functions and Verifying inverse functions gave you the idea of running a machine backwards. A logarithm is that backwards machine — nothing more. Pick a base, drag the exponent, and watch the same pair of numbers appear on both curves with their coordinates swapped. Like folding paper: one question is how thick it is after 6 folds, the other is how many folds got it that thick.

already taught
f(x) = b^x input = the exponent output = the value (always > 0)
this lesson
f^-1(x) = log_b(x) input = the value (must be > 0) output = the exponent

Watch which coordinate is which, because that is what the distractors trade on. On the exponential curve the point is (exponent, value); on the logarithm curve the very same fact is the point (value, exponent). Reflecting across the dashed line y = x is not decoration — it is the picture of an inverse, and it is why the domain of one is the range of the other.

2. Same Three Numbers

A logarithmic statement and an exponential statement are the same claim about the same three numbers: a base, an exponent and a result. Drag either number and watch both spellings update together — then look at what happens when the three get shuffled into the wrong slots. Like "Ana is Ben's mother" and "Ben is Ana's son": two sentences, one fact, and swapping the names breaks it.

The reading rule that never fails: the base of the log stays the base of the power, and the value of the log is the exponent. Every wrong option in this topic is one of these three numbers dropped into someone else's slot, which is why labelling the slots beats trying to remember a pattern — there are only six permutations and five of them are wrong.

3. Three Laws One Source

Nothing new is being asserted here. Each logarithm property is an exponent property you already own, read from the other side — so pick a law, drag the numbers, and watch both sides land on the same value. Then pick the fake rule and watch them refuse to. Like a slide rule: multiplying by sliding two lengths together only works because a log turns multiplication into addition.

All three laws move a level down the arithmetic ladder: a product inside becomes a sum outside, a quotient becomes a difference, a power becomes a coefficient. That is the whole pattern, and it is why no rule for log(x + y) can exist — addition is already the bottom rung, and there is no exponent property that turns b^m + b^n into b^(anything).

4. Change Of Base

Your calculator has two logarithm keys and neither of them is base 2. Change of base fixes that with a single ratio — and the three shapes it is confused with give visibly different numbers. Drag the base and the target and watch the natural-log route and the common-log route agree exactly. Like converting a price into another currency: divide by the same rate top and bottom and the money is unchanged.

The formula is not magic and it is worth deriving once: put c = log_b(x), so b^c = x; take ln of both sides and apply the power rule to get c*ln(b) = ln(x); divide. Any new base works — that is why the natural-log and common-log rows below always agree — so use whichever key your calculator actually has.

5. Constant Ratio

An exponential model is not "adding a bit each year" — it is multiplying by the same factor each year. Set a starting amount and a percentage, then read the two right-hand columns: the differences wander, the ratios never move. That frozen ratio is the signature, and the last line is where logarithms earn their keep. Like a savings account: the interest is the same percentage every year, which is a bigger number of dollars every year.

Two habits pay for themselves on every question in this topic. Build the factor before you build the formula — 8% growth is 1.08 and 5% decay is 0.95, never 0.08 and never -0.05. And divide, do not subtract, when a table asks you to identify the model: a constant difference is linear, a constant second difference is quadratic, and a constant ratio is exponential.
