**The big idea**: everything you already do fluently — **algebra and factoring**, **trigonometric identities**, the function evaluation behind every **linear algebra** and **machine learning** pipeline you write — answers *what is $f$ at this point*. Calculus is built on a different question: *what is $f$ heading toward as the input closes in on a point* — and the whole subject depends on those two answers being allowed to disagree. $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ says $f(x)$ stays arbitrarily close to $L$ for every $x$ sufficiently close to $c$ on both sides, with $x = c$ deliberately excluded. Continuity is the lucky special case where the two questions coincide, $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$. Every technique below either computes a limit when substitution fails, or describes what breaks when continuity does.

**Key concepts**

- **Estimating limits starts with the one-sided limits underneath them.** $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ is the value $f(x)$ approaches from just below $a$; $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ from just above — rigorously, that $f(x)$ stays arbitrarily close to $L$ for $x$ just above $a$, not that $f(a)$ equals anything. The two-sided limit exists **exactly when both sides exist and agree**. For $f(x) = 4 + \frac{|x-2|}{x-2}$ the left limit is $3$ and the right is $5$, so $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$ does not exist: not $3$, not $5$, never the average $4$. If both sides give $2$, the limit is $2$.
- **The value at the point is irrelevant.** $f(c)$ need not be defined, and where defined may be wrong: a table showing $f(x) \to 7$ from both sides as $x \to 4$ pins $\lim_{x \to 4} f(x) = 7$ even when $f(4) = 10$.
- **Estimating from a table can lie, and rapid oscillation is how.** For $f(x) = \sin\!\big(\pi/(x-2)\big)$, the obvious probes $x = 2.1,\ 2.01,\ 2.001$ give $\sin(10\pi),\ \sin(100\pi),\ \sin(1000\pi)$ — exactly $0$ every time, so the numbers appear to settle on $0$. Probe $x = 2 + \tfrac{2}{21} \approx 2.0952$, barely off that grid, and you get $1$: the limit does not exist, and the round-number sampling was hiding it. Unbounded is not a limit either — if both sides run to $\infty$ the two-sided limit **does not exist**, because $\infty$ is not a real number.
- **The limit laws are why algebra survives, and they hold one-sidedly.** Given $\lim_{x \to c^+} f = A$ and $\lim_{x \to c^+} g = B$, the **right-hand** limit of $h = f + g$ at $c$ is $A + B$ — and nothing follows about the left side, the two-sided limit, or continuity at $c$.
- **Limits by direct substitution are a theorem, not a shortcut.** Substitution is legal exactly when the function is continuous at the point, which is why $\lim_{x \to 2}(x^2 + 3x - 1) = 9$ lands immediately: a polynomial is continuous everywhere. The same licence covers rational functions where the denominator is nonzero, and the trigonometric functions on their domains.
- **$\frac{0}{0}$ is indeterminate, which means the algebra is unfinished.** Factoring resolves it by cancelling the common factor: $\frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3} = x + 3 \to 6$; $\frac{x^2 + x - 6}{x - 2} = \frac{(x+3)(x-2)}{x-2} = x + 3 \to 5$; $\frac{x^3 - 1}{x - 1} = x^2 + x + 1 \to 3$. The cancelled expression differs from the original **only at** $x = c$, and the limit never looks there. So a finite limit forces the numerator to vanish too: $\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - c}{x - 3}$ is finite only when $9 - c = 0$, i.e. $c = 9$, giving $x + 3 \to 6$; any other $c$ puts a nonzero numerator over a vanishing denominator and blows up.
- **Conjugates clear radicals by manufacturing a difference of squares.** Multiply by $1$ in the form $\frac{\sqrt{x}+2}{\sqrt{x}+2}$: $\frac{\sqrt{x}-2}{x-4} = \frac{x-4}{(x-4)(\sqrt{x}+2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+2} \to \frac14$. Identically, $\frac{\sqrt{x+9}-3}{x} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+9}+3} \to \frac16$. It is only $(a-b)(a+b) = a^2 - b^2$ turning a radical into a polynomial that cancels.
- **The Squeeze Theorem trades a hard limit for two easy ones.** If $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ near $c$ and $\lim_{x \to c} g = \lim_{x \to c} h = L$, then $\lim_{x \to c} f = L$. That the two bounding functions **share** a limit is the whole hypothesis — their job is to confine the values of $f$ toward a single limit — and $f$ need only be defined on an open interval around $c$; differentiability, monotonicity and positivity are irrelevant. Because $\sin(1/x)$ stays bounded in $[-1, 1]$: $-|x| \le x\sin(1/x) \le |x|$ gives $\lim_{x \to 0} x\sin(1/x) = 0$, and the quadratic pair $-x^2 \le x^2\sin(1/x) \le x^2$ gives $0$. Same move at infinity: $-\frac1x \le \frac{\sin x}{x} \le \frac1x$ for $x > 0$ forces $\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 0$. Where the bounds do *not* share a limit — $-1 \le \sin(1/x) \le 1$ — nothing is concluded, and that limit does not exist.
- **Limits of trigonometric functions all reduce to two.** First, $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$, proved by squeezing with $\cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1$ near $0$ and letting $\cos x \to 1$. Second, $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac12$ — the conjugate trick again: multiply by $\frac{1+\cos x}{1+\cos x}$ to get $\frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2(1+\cos x)} \to 1^2 \cdot \frac12$. The companion is free: $\frac{1-\cos x}{x} = x \cdot \frac{1-\cos x}{x^2} \to 0$.
- **Everything else is rescaling or shifting.** Match each argument to the denominator: $\frac{\sin 7x}{x} = 7 \cdot \frac{\sin 7x}{7x} \to 7$, so $\frac{\sin 3x}{x} \to 3$ while $\frac{\sin 5x}{5x} \to 1$. Ratios collapse to the ratio of arguments: $\frac{\tan 3x}{\sin 5x} = \frac{\tan 3x}{3x} \cdot \frac{5x}{\sin 5x} \cdot \frac{3}{5} \to \frac{3}{5}$, and $\frac{\sin 4x}{\tan 2x} \to \frac42 = 2$. Away from zero, substitute: $u = x - \pi$ turns $\lim_{x \to \pi} \frac{\sin x}{x - \pi}$ into $\lim_{u \to 0} \frac{-\sin u}{u} = -1$.
- **The types of discontinuities are a taxonomy built from one-sided limits.** *Removable*: the two-sided limit exists but $f(c)$ is missing or wrong — a single hole on the graph. For a rational $P/Q$ that is exactly when $(x-a)$ appears in both $P$ and $Q$ and cancels completely, as in $\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ at $x = 1$. *Jump*: both one-sided limits exist and are finite but unequal — $\frac{|x-3|}{x-3}$ at $x = 3$ approaches $-1$ then $+1$, so the limit fails because the sides differ. *Infinite*: at least one one-sided limit is $+\infty$ or $-\infty$ — the signature of $(x-a)$ in the denominator with a nonzero numerator. *Oscillating*: values fluctuate between bounds without ever settling. Composition preserves the classification: feed a jump into a strictly monotonic continuous $f$ and the one-sided limits map to two distinct values, so $f(g(x))$ still has a jump.
- **The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT) converts continuity into existence.** If $f$ is continuous on the **closed** interval $[a, b]$ and $N$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then $f(c) = N$ for some $c$ in $(a, b)$. Continuity on $[a,b]$ is the entire hypothesis: not differentiability, not monotonicity, not linearity. So $f$ continuous on $[1, 4]$ with $f(1) = 2$, $f(4) = 9$ must attain $5$; on $[-2, 3]$ with $f(-2) = -1$, $f(3) = 4$ it must have a root. It is pure existence — no count and no location — and one-directional: $f(1) = 3$, $f(2) = 5$ guarantees nothing, because $0$ is not between the endpoint values.
- **Most IVT questions are really about an auxiliary function.** If $f(a) < g(a)$ and $f(b) > g(b)$, apply the IVT to $h = f - g$, whose endpoint values have opposite signs: the graphs cross at least once. If $f$ maps $[0,1]$ into $[0,1]$, then $g(x) = f(x) - x$ has $g(0) \ge 0 \ge g(1)$, so some point has input equal to output — a fixed point. The contrapositive is equally useful: a continuous $f$ with no root on $[2, 6]$ must have $f(2)$ and $f(6)$ carrying the **same** sign. And when the endpoints tie — $f(1) = f(5) = 10$, target $5$ — the theorem is silent until $f$ is known to drop below $5$ inside, then applies on that subinterval.
- **A break lets a function skip values, which is why that hypothesis is so strict.** On $[0, 4]$, $f(x) = \frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ equals $x + 2$ and runs from $2$ to $6$, yet never takes the value $4$: the only input producing it is the hole at $x = 2$.

**Rules to remember**

- A limit describes the neighbourhood of $c$, never $c$ itself. $f(c)$ may be absent, present, or present and different.
- The two-sided limit exists if and only if both one-sided limits exist and are equal; $\infty$ is a diagnosis of failure, not a value.
- Substitute first: a number means done, $\frac{k}{0}$ with $k \neq 0$ blows up, and $\frac00$ routes by shape — radical to a conjugate, polynomial to factoring, sine or tangent to $\frac{\sin u}{u}$.
- Squeeze needs both bounds to share one limit; the shape to hunt for is bounded $\times$ vanishing.
- $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ and $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2} = \frac12$ generate every other elementary trigonometric limit.
- Classify from left limit, right limit and $f(c)$: all matching is continuous, limit without a matching value is removable, finite and unequal is a jump, a side at $\pm\infty$ is infinite, nothing settling is oscillating.
- The IVT needs continuity on a closed interval and a target between the endpoint values, and returns existence only.

**Common pitfalls**

- Averaging the one-sided limits when they disagree, or reporting one of them as "the limit".
- Insisting $f(c)$ must be defined or equal $L$; calling a limit "$\infty$" and existent at once; or treating a table of round-number probes as proof.
- Believing the cancellation removed the discontinuity. $\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ and $x+2$ differ at one point, and the hole is still there.
- Squeezing with bounds whose limits differ, or with $\pm 1$ where the vanishing factor was needed — for $x^2\sin(1/x)$ the pair is $\pm x^2$.
- Forgetting to rescale: $\frac{\sin 3x}{x}$ is $3$, not $1$.
- Using the IVT on a function with a hole, on an open interval, or with a target outside the endpoint values — or reading it backwards, since same-sign endpoints do not prove there is no root.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Substitute first. What comes back names the technique: a real number means continuity did the work; $\frac00$ means factor, conjugate, or a known trigonometric limit; nonzero over zero means an infinite discontinuity.
2. For "does it exist", compute both one-sided limits and compare. Nearly every distractor is a side, an average, or $f(c)$.
3. For Squeeze Theorem questions, find the bounded factor and multiply its bound by the vanishing factor — the pair is always $\pm$(that factor) — then check the bounds share a limit.
4. For trigonometric limits, force each argument to match its denominator and read off the ratio; away from $0$, substitute $u$ = input minus target first.
5. To classify a discontinuity, compute left limit, right limit, then $f(c)$, and read the taxonomy off those three numbers, not off the look of the formula.
6. For IVT questions, tick three boxes — continuity on a *closed* interval, target between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, conclusion of existence only; distractors usually break exactly one. If graphs crossing, a fixed point or a sign is mentioned, build the auxiliary function ($f-g$, or $f(x)-x$) first.
