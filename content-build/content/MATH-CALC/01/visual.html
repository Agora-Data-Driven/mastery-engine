1. Value Versus Approach

Everything you already do — substitute the number, get the answer — asks what is f AT the point. A limit asks what is f heading TOWARD. Pick a function, then shrink the distance h and watch the two columns close in (or refuse to). Like a train's destination board versus the platform it is standing on: usually the same place, and the interesting cases are when they are not.

The limit reads the neighbourhood; f(2) is one isolated fact it never consults — which is why a limit can exist where the function is undefined, and can disagree with a value that is defined. The reverse reading is the definition of continuity, and it is the exception rather than the rule from here on.

2. Clearing 0/0

Substitution answers first. A number means you are done; 0/0 means the algebra is unfinished. Watch the raw quotient and the rewritten one produce the same numbers everywhere except at the point itself — which is the whole justification for cancelling. Like cancelling a common factor in a fraction: it is the same fraction, just written so you can read it.

A conjugate is not a separate theorem — it is (a−b)(a+b) = a^2 − b^2 used to turn a radical into a polynomial so the offending factor can cancel. Reach for it when the 0/0 contains a root, and for factoring when it does not. Either way the goal is identical: get the vanishing factor out of the denominator.

3. Squeeze The Oscillation

Some functions have no limit worth computing directly, and are trapped between two that do. Drag the zoom and watch the band between the bounds pinch shut — the function inside has nowhere left to go. Like two lift doors closing on someone: you never watch them, but you know exactly where they end up.

What f must supply is small: defined on an open interval around the point. Not differentiable, not monotonic, not positive — none of that is asked for, and none of it would help. What the theorem needs is the pair of bounds, and it needs them to share one limit. Everything else is bookkeeping.

4. Small Angle Ratios

One squeeze — cos(x) < sin(x)/x < 1 — gives sin(x)/x → 1, and every other elementary trigonometric limit is that fact plus bookkeeping. Change k and m and watch the answer track the ratio of the arguments. Like a gear train: a small angle and its sine turn at the same rate, so only the gear ratios survive.

Two facts generate the rest: sin(x)/x → 1 and (1−cos x)/x^2 → 1/2, the second being the conjugate trick again — multiply by (1+cos x)/(1+cos x) to get sin^2(x)/(x^2(1+cos x)). Away from zero nothing new is needed either: substitute u = x − (the target) and you are back at zero.

5. Breaks And Skipped Values

Four ways a function can break at a point, classified from left limit, right limit and f(2) alone — and then the reason the Intermediate Value Theorem insists on continuity. Drag the target line and see which values actually get hit. Like a road with a missing bridge: every milepost between the two towns exists on the map, and you can still never drive over that one.

The IVT is pure existence: it never counts the solutions and never locates them, and it is one-directional — a target outside the endpoint values, or a single break anywhere inside, and it simply declines to speak. That silence is not a claim that nothing is attained, which is the trap in most questions written about it.
