1. Secant To Tangent

Estimating limits and one-sided limits and Limits by direct substitution, factoring, and conjugates taught you what a quantity approaches. Aim all of that at ONE quotient and you have the derivative. Drag the step h toward zero and watch the secant line swing onto the tangent line — and switch the step to the left to see why a corner has no derivative at all. Like a camera zoom: from far away a curve looks bent, close enough it looks straight.

The secant slope is not a lesser thing than the derivative — it is the average rate of change over the step, and it is the right answer to a different question. An object at 155 m when t = 12 s and 170 m when t = 13 s has an average velocity of 15 m/s over that second; only the limit turns it into a speedometer reading.

2. Which Rule Applies

The power, product and quotient rules exist so you never run that limit twice. Pick an expression, drag x, and compare the correct rule against the tempting shortcut — with a numerical referee that has never heard of either. Like checking a recipe by tasting: the arithmetic settles the argument, not the confidence of the cook.

The power rule does not care that an exponent looks exotic: since e is a constant, d/dx of x^e is e·x^(e-1). And applied term by term to a polynomial of degree n (n >= 1) it lowers the degree by exactly one — which is the quiet reason a second derivative of a cubic is linear.

3. Trig Derivative Cycle

Sine and cosine differentiate into each other, so their higher-order derivatives run in a loop of length four. Drag x to read the slope off the curve; drag the order n to see which of the four faces the n-th derivative wears. Like the hands of a clock: after four steps you are exactly where you started, so only the remainder matters.

Concavity is just the second derivative wearing its geometric name. On (0, pi/2) the second derivative of cos(x) is -cos(x), which is negative there — so cos(x) is concave down on that interval, and no amount of staring at the picture is needed to say so.

4. Chain Implicit Rates

Three faces of one rule. A composition multiplies the outer derivative by the inner one; implicit differentiation applies that to a y that is secretly a function of x; related rates applies it to a variable that is secretly a function of time. Like gears: whatever you turn, the ratio at the far end is the product of every ratio between.

Implicit differentiation is what lets you differentiate a curve that is not a function at all: the circle fails the vertical-line test, yet every point on it still has a slope. The same move gives logarithmic differentiation — take ln of both sides of y = x^sin(x) first — and the inverse-function rule, where the derivative of the inverse at b is 1 divided by f'(a).

5. Tangent Line Zoom

Three functions that agree on everything the tangent line knows — same point, same slope at a = 2 — and differ only in curvature. Drag the step and watch the approximation error appear out of the second derivative alone. Like a straight road drawn on a curved map: fine for the next mile, hopeless by the next county.

Read the geometry backwards and the same line answers a different question: a tangent that passes through (2, 5) and (2.2, 5.6) has slope (5.6 - 5)/0.2 = 3.0, so f'(2) = 3. And a tangent slope of -4 means the function is falling at 4 output units per input unit — a statement about the rate, never about the value.

6. Minimize The Loss

Setting a derivative to zero is one move with four costumes: a shape problem, a cost problem, and the two loss functions a model is trained on. Drag the variable and watch the derivative tell you which way to move — and go silent exactly at the optimum. Like walking downhill blindfolded: the slope under your feet is all the instruction you need, until it flattens.

Two habits separate a correct answer from a plausible one. On a CLOSED interval the absolute extreme can sit at an endpoint, where the derivative is never zero — so the endpoints get checked alongside the critical points. And the derivative supplies a direction, not the size of the error: that is exactly the role it plays when a model is trained.
