**The big idea**: **Derivative as slope and rate of change** gave you a machine that turns a function into its rate, and **Derivative rules (Power rule, Product, Quotient)**, **Derivatives of sin(x) and cos(x)** and the **Chain Rule and Implicit differentiation** made that machine fast. This lesson runs it backwards, and two questions that look unrelated turn out to be one. *What function has this rate?* is the antiderivative. *How much area sits under this curve?* is the definite integral. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says they are the same object, which collapses an infinite limiting process into a single subtraction. Everything else is either estimating that area before you can compute it (Riemann sums), running one differentiation rule backwards (the Reverse Power Rule, the standard table, u-substitution), or aiming the finished machinery (area between curves).

**Key concepts**

- **Riemann sums (Left, Right, Midpoint, Trapezoidal) approximate area with rectangles, and the four names differ only in where each rectangle gets its height.** Cut $[a,b]$ into $n$ pieces of width $\Delta x = (b-a)/n$; the left rule takes the height at the left endpoint of each subinterval, the right rule at the right endpoint, the midpoint rule at the midpoint, and the trapezoidal rule uses width times the *mean* of the two endpoint heights. On $[0,2]$ with $f(x)=x$ and $n=2$, $\Delta x = 1$: the left sum is $1\cdot f(0) + 1\cdot f(1) = 0 + 1 = 1$; the right sum is $1\cdot f(1) + 1\cdot f(2) = 1 + 2 = 3$; the true area is the triangle, $2$. The midpoint sum is $f(0.5) + f(1.5) = 2$ and the trapezoidal sum is $\frac{0+1}{2} + \frac{1+2}{2} = 2$ — both exact, because a trapezoid *is* a straight line's region.
- **Monotonicity fixes the direction of the error for the left and right rules, and nothing else does.** On each subinterval an increasing function is smallest at the left endpoint, so every left rectangle sits under the curve: **increasing means the left sum underestimates and the right sum overestimates**. Decreasing flips both: **left overestimates, right underestimates**. That $1 < 2 < 3$ above is the increasing case in miniature. It is also a design tool — a biologist modelling bacterial growth with a strictly decreasing rate $G(t)$ who needs a guaranteed *underestimate* must use the right sum. And it explains the limiting behaviour: for a positive increasing $f$, $L_n$ *increases* toward the true value as $n$ grows, always approaching from below, never crossing it.
- **Concavity fixes the direction for the trapezoidal and midpoint rules.** A trapezoid's top is the chord joining two points on the curve, and on a concave-up arc the chord lies above the curve, so **the trapezoidal sum overestimates when $f$ is concave up** and underestimates when concave down. The midpoint rule is secretly the tangent line at the midpoint (the tangent rectangle and the midpoint rectangle have equal area), and on a concave-up arc the tangent lies below, so **the midpoint sum underestimates when $f$ is concave up** and overestimates when concave down. For smooth $f$ the midpoint error is typically about half the trapezoidal error and of opposite sign — *typically*, not always: "midpoint always beats trapezoidal" is false as a universal claim.
- **The definite integral is the signed area accumulated across the interval.** $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the limit of those Riemann sums as $n \to \infty$, and area below the axis counts as negative. Two consequences follow: $\int_a^a f(x)\,dx = 0$, because the region has zero width, and a net answer can be small even when a lot of area accumulated in both directions.
- **Antiderivatives and Indefinite integrals: $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ exactly when $F'(x) = f(x)$** — not $F(x) = f'(x)$, and not $F'' = f$. The indefinite integral $\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C$ names the whole *family*. The constant is not decoration: functions differing by a constant have identical derivatives, so if $F$ works so does $F + 5$. The converse is the sharp version: if $F' = G'$ on an interval then $(F-G)' = 0$, and zero derivative on an interval means constant, so **$F(x) - G(x)$ is a constant** — never $f(x)$, and not necessarily zero. Two examples: $F(x)=\ln(7x)$ and $G(x)=\ln(x)$ are both antiderivatives of $1/x$, because $\ln(7x) = \ln 7 + \ln x$; and $x^3+2x$ and $x^3+2x+5$ are both antiderivatives of $3x^2+2$.
- **The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus ties the two together: if $F' = f$, then $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$.** The $+C$ cancels in the subtraction, which is why definite integrals never carry one. $\int_0^2 3x^2\,dx = [x^3]_0^2 = 8 - 0 = 8$. If $F(x) = x^3$ then $\int_1^3 F'(x)\,dx = 27 - 1 = 26$ — you never even need to know what $F'$ was. And with velocity $v(t) = 2t+1$ on $[1,3]$, an antiderivative is $F(t) = t^2 + t$, so the net displacement is $F(3)-F(1) = 12 - 2 = 10$ metres. Note what is *not* the answer: $v(3)-v(1) = 4$ subtracts rates, and $F(1)-F(3)$ has the ends backwards.
- **Reverse Power Rule: $\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$ for every real $n \neq -1$.** Raise the exponent, then divide by the new exponent — precisely undoing the power rule, since differentiating brings $n+1$ down to cancel the denominator. $\int x^4\,dx = \frac{x^5}{5}+C$. $\int x^{-2}\,dx = \frac{x^{-1}}{-1}+C = -x^{-1}+C$. $\int x^{-3}\,dx = \frac{x^{-2}}{-2}+C = -\frac12 x^{-2}+C$. Rewrite roots as powers first: $\int \sqrt{x}\,dx = \int x^{1/2}\,dx = \frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}+C = \frac23 x^{3/2}+C$. Constants are the case $n=0$: $\int 7\,dx = 7x + C$. The rule fails at $n=-1$ for a purely arithmetic reason — **the denominator $n+1$ becomes $0$** — not because $1/x$ lacks an antiderivative.
- **Indefinite integrals of ex,1/x,sin, and cos patch that hole and cover the transcendental cases.** $\int e^x dx = e^x + C$; $\int \frac{1}{x}dx = \ln|x| + C$; $\int \sin(x)\,dx = -\cos(x)+C$; $\int \cos(x)\,dx = \sin(x)+C$, and therefore $\int -\sin(x)\,dx = \cos(x)+C$. Every sign here is just $\frac{d}{dx}\cos = -\sin$ read backwards. The absolute value is not a flourish: $1/x$ is defined for $x<0$, so its antiderivative must be too, and on $(-\infty,0)$ the antiderivative is $\ln(-x)+C$. Combine linearly, term by term: $\int (3e^x - \frac{2}{x} + \sin x - 4\cos x)\,dx = 3e^x - 2\ln|x| - \cos x - 4\sin x + C$, and $\int (e^x - 2\sin x - \frac{3}{x})\,dx = e^x + 2\cos x - 3\ln|x| + C$.
- **u-substitution is the Chain Rule read backwards.** Since $\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x)) = f(g(x))\,g'(x)$, any integrand of the shape $f(g(x))\,g'(x)$ integrates to $F(g(x)) + C$. So the trigger is: **an inner function present together with its derivative as a factor.** Set $u = g(x)$, compute $du = g'(x)\,dx$, rewrite until no $x$ survives, integrate in $u$, then **back-substitute**. $\int 2x(x^2+1)^3dx$ with $u = x^2+1$, $du = 2x\,dx$ becomes $\int u^3du = \frac{u^4}{4} = \frac{(x^2+1)^4}{4}+C$. $\int \cos(3x)\,dx$ with $u=3x$, $dx = du/3$ gives $\frac13\sin(3x)+C$. $\int \sqrt{3x+4}\,dx$ gives $\frac{2}{9}(3x+4)^{3/2}+C$; $\int \frac{3x^2}{x^3+2}dx$ gives $\ln|x^3+2|+C$; $\int \frac{\cos(\ln x)}{x}dx$ with $u=\ln x$ gives $\sin(\ln x)+C$; $\int xe^{x^2}dx$ with $u=x^2$ gives $\frac12 e^{x^2}+C$.
- **The pattern test is what separates the doable from the undoable.** $\int x\cos(x^2)dx$ works ($x$ is $\frac12$ of the derivative of $x^2$); $\int \cos(x^2)dx$ has no $x$ factor at all; $\int x^2\sin(x)dx$ has an $x^2$ that is not a constant multiple of $(x)' = 1$, so it fails outright. $\int \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}dx$ works with $u=\sin x$, and $\int e^x\cos(e^x)dx$ works with $u=e^x$.
- **Area between curves: decide which curve is on top first, then integrate $\int_a^b(\text{top} - \text{bottom})\,dx$.** Between $y=x$ and $y=x^2$ on $[0,1]$, $x \geq x^2$ throughout, so the area is $\int_0^1 (x - x^2)dx = \frac12 - \frac13 = \frac16$. If the curves cross inside the interval, **split at the intersection points** and integrate each piece with its own top, because a single integral lets the pieces cancel. On $[0,1.5]$ the naive $\int_0^{1.5}(x-x^2)dx = 1.125 - 1.125 = 0$, while the true area is $\frac16 + \frac16 = \frac13$. Area is a size, so the answer is always **nonnegative**.

**Rules to remember**

- Left rectangle heights come from left endpoints, right from right, midpoint from midpoints; the trapezoidal rule is width times the *mean* endpoint height.
- Increasing $\Rightarrow$ left under, right over. Decreasing $\Rightarrow$ left over, right under.
- Concave up $\Rightarrow$ trapezoidal over, midpoint under. Concave down $\Rightarrow$ the reverse.
- $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$ whenever $F' = f$; $\int_a^a f = 0$; area under the axis is negative.
- $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ means $F' = f$. Two antiderivatives of the same $f$ differ by a constant.
- $\int x^n dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C$ for $n \neq -1$; the exception exists because $n+1 = 0$ there.
- $\int e^x = e^x$, $\int \frac1x = \ln|x|$, $\int \sin = -\cos$, $\int \cos = \sin$, each $+\,C$.
- u-substitution needs the inner function *and* its derivative (up to a constant multiple), and always ends with back-substitution.
- Area between curves is $\int(\text{top}-\text{bottom})$, split at every crossing, and the result is never negative.

**Common pitfalls**

- Quoting an over/under rule without checking whether the question said increasing/decreasing (left and right) or concave up/down (trapezoidal and midpoint). Distractors mix the two vocabularies deliberately.
- Claiming the midpoint rule always beats the trapezoidal rule. It usually does for smooth functions; "always, on any interval" is a different and false claim.
- Dropping $+C$ on an indefinite integral, or keeping it on a definite one — it cancels in $F(b)-F(a)$.
- Saying two antiderivatives of the same function are equal, or that their difference is $f(x)$. Their difference is a constant.
- Applying the Reverse Power Rule to $x^{-1}$ and producing $\frac{x^0}{0}$. That case is $\ln|x|+C$.
- Writing $\int \frac1x dx = \ln(x)+C$ and losing the whole negative half of the domain.
- Sign slips on $\int \sin$ and $\int \cos$ — the minus belongs to $-\cos$, not to $\sin$.
- Substituting the function but leaving $dx$ untouched, or stopping at $\sin(u)+C$ and never back-substituting for $x$ — the two most-tested u-substitution errors.
- Integrating bottom minus top, or ignoring a crossing point, and then reporting a negative "area".

**How to approach the questions**

1. On any "always true" Riemann statement, classify the rule first: left/right questions are about monotonicity, trapezoidal/midpoint questions are about concavity. Then check the pairing.
2. For a small numeric sum, write $\Delta x$ and the endpoint list before summing. On $[0,2]$ with $n=2$ the heights are $f(0), f(1)$ for left and $f(1), f(2)$ for right — most wrong answers use the other list.
3. For a definite integral, find *any* antiderivative, then subtract in the order $F(\text{upper}) - F(\text{lower})$. If the integrand is already written as $F'$, do not differentiate anything.
4. For "which of these could both be antiderivatives of the same $f$", test whether the difference is constant — $\ln(7x) - \ln(x) = \ln 7$ passes; $x^3+2x$ and $x^3-2x$ differ by $4x$ and fail.
5. Before substituting, name $g(x)$ and check that $g'(x)$ appears as a factor up to a constant. If it does not, the integral is not a straightforward u-substitution.
6. Verify by differentiating. Every answer in this lesson is checkable in one line, and it catches sign and coefficient errors faster than re-deriving.
