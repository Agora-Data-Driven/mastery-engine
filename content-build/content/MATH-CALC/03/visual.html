1. Undo The Derivative

Derivative as slope and rate of change, the Derivative rules (Power rule, Product, Quotient) and Derivatives of sin(x) and cos(x) all ran one way: function in, rate out. Here is the same picture read backwards. Drag the point to move along the curve, then drag C and watch what refuses to change. Like a photograph of a hillside: the slope at every point is fixed, but the whole hill can sit at any altitude.

This is why an indefinite integral is a family and a definite integral is a number. Antidifferentiating loses the constant, so you write + C; but in F(b) − F(a) the two copies of C cancel, which is exactly why definite integrals never carry one.

2. Rectangles To Area

Four rules, one difference: where each strip gets its height. Pick a shape, pick a rule, then drag n and watch the error shrink — and watch which side of the true value it shrinks from. Like paying for a curved lawn with rectangular tiles: where you measure each tile decides whether you buy too many or too few.

Two rules of thumb worth keeping apart. Left versus right is decided by whether f rises or falls; trapezoidal versus midpoint is decided by which way f bends. And for smooth f the midpoint error is usually about half the trapezoidal error and of the opposite sign — usually. "Midpoint always beats trapezoidal on any interval" is a different, false claim.

3. Signed Area Ledger

Let n run to infinity and the rectangles become the definite integral. Drag the upper limit and watch two numbers move together: the shaded area, and the single subtraction F(b) − F(a) that the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says is the same thing. Like a bank balance: deposits add, withdrawals subtract, and closing minus opening is the whole story.

Signed area is a feature, not a rounding error. A velocity integrated over time gives net displacement, not distance travelled — reverse for a while and the integral shrinks. When a question wants total distance or total area it will say so, and then you integrate the absolute value, or split at every crossing and add the magnitudes.

4. The Antiderivative Table

Almost every antiderivative in this lesson comes from one mechanical rule, plus four entries you learn by heart. Drag the exponent and watch the rule work — then watch it fall over at exactly one value. Like a multiplication table with one square blacked out: everything is automatic except the entry you have to be told.

The exception at n = −1 is arithmetic, not deep: nothing is wrong with 1/x, the formula just divides by zero there. And the bars in ln|x| are load-bearing — 1/x is perfectly happy at x = −2, so its antiderivative must be too, which on (−∞, 0) reads ln(−x) + C.

5. Chain Rule Backwards

The Chain Rule multiplies by the inner function's derivative on the way out. So an integrand that already carries that factor can be run in reverse. Pick one, step through, and stop early on purpose to meet the most common mistake in the topic. Like reading a recipe backwards: you can only undo a step if the ingredient it used is still on the counter.

The test is mechanical: name g(x), then look for g′(x) as a factor, up to a constant multiple you can pull outside. It is there in x·cos(x²) and in cos(x)/sin(x); it is absent in cos(x²); and in x²·sin(x) the x² is not a constant multiple of (x)′ = 1. Failing the test is information, not defeat — it tells you the answer choices are about something else.

6. Top Minus Bottom

Point the machinery at a region rather than at a curve and one decision comes first: which function is on top. Drag the right-hand edge past the crossing point with splitting switched off, and watch a real area report itself as zero. Like measuring the gap between two roads on a map: you subtract the lower from the upper, and where they cross you start a new measurement.

Where do the crossings come from? Solve top = bottom: x = x² gives x(1 − x) = 0, so x = 0 and x = 1. Find those before you write any integral, and the "which curve is on top" question answers itself on each piece. An area is a size, so a negative answer is never the area — it is a sign that the subtraction ran the wrong way round somewhere.
