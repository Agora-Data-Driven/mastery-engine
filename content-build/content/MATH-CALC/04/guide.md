**The big idea**: **Derivative as slope and rate of change** gave you one slope for one input, and **Tangent lines and linear approximations** turned that slope into a line standing in for the curve nearby. Every derivative you have taken so far had exactly one input to vary. Nothing you actually optimise does: a loss depends on many parameters at once, and "the slope" stops being one number because there is no longer one direction to move in. This lesson makes that jump, and the surprise is how little new machinery it needs. Freeze every input but one and you are back in single-variable calculus, so **Derivative rules (Power rule, Product, Quotient)** and **Chain Rule and Implicit differentiation** apply unchanged. Stack those one-at-a-time slopes into a vector and you get the gradient, which answers what a single slope cannot: of infinitely many directions you could step, which climbs fastest? Stack the second slopes into a matrix and you get the Hessian, heir to **Higher-order derivatives and Second derivatives**, which separates a minimum from a maximum from a saddle.

**Key concepts**

- **Functions of several variables take several inputs and return exactly one number.** $f(x,y)$ is *two inputs, one output* — its domain is a region of the plane, its output a scalar, and its graph the surface $z = f(x,y)$ sitting above that plane. Worked: $f(x,y) = x^3y^2$ at $(2,3)$ gives $8 \cdot 9 = 72$, a single height. It all generalises to $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$; two variables is the case you can draw. Setting the output constant gives a **level curve** — where $x^3y^2 = 72$ is one contour, and a family of them is a contour map.

- **A partial derivative is a slice, and slicing turns the new problem into the old one.** Hold $y$ fixed at $y_0$ and $z = f(x, y_0)$ is an ordinary one-variable curve cut out of the surface; its slope is the partial derivative with respect to $x$, written $\partial f/\partial x$ or $f_x$. Formally $f_x = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h,\,y) - f(x,\,y)}{h}$ — the same limit as always, with the second input frozen. In practice: treat every other variable as a constant and differentiate normally. Worked: for $f = x^3y^2$, freezing $y$ makes $y^2$ a constant multiplier, so the power rule gives $f_x = 3x^2y^2$; freezing $x$ makes $x^3$ constant, so $f_y = 2x^3y$. At $(2,3)$: $f_x = 3\cdot4\cdot9 = 108$, $f_y = 2\cdot8\cdot3 = 48$.

- **Two slices fix the tangent plane, because two intersecting lines determine a unique plane.** The $x$-slice gives one tangent line through the point, the $y$-slice another; they meet there and are not parallel, and two intersecting lines span exactly one plane — so the pair of partial derivatives already determines it. The formula is **Tangent lines and linear approximations** with a second term bolted on: $z = f(a,b) + f_x(a,b)(x-a) + f_y(a,b)(y-b)$. Worked for $f = x^3y^2$ at $(1,1)$, where $f = 1$, $f_x = 3$, $f_y = 2$: the plane is $z = 1 + 3(x-1) + 2(y-1)$. Step to $(1.1, 0.9)$ and it predicts $1 + 0.3 - 0.2 = 1.1$; the true value is $1.331 \times 0.81 = 1.078$. Error $0.022$ for a step of $0.14$ — and shrinking faster than the step. Without this local flatness every optimiser is blind, because each step is chosen from the plane, not from the surface.

- **Partial derivatives and the Gradient vector: the gradient is the partials stacked into a vector.** $\nabla f = (f_x, f_y)$ — one component per input. It is a *vector*, not a single number measuring curvature and not the matrix of second partials. At $(1,1)$ for $f = x^3y^2$, $\nabla f = (3, 2)$, magnitude $\sqrt{13} \approx 3.606$. Two facts make it central: **it points in the direction of steepest ascent**, and its length is the rate of climb there. So $-\nabla f$ is the direction of steepest descent — where to move to decrease the value fastest in one tiny step. That is why gradients are central to machine learning: they give the direction for optimisation steps like gradient descent.

- **Directional derivatives measure the instantaneous rate of change as you move from a point in a chosen direction, and the formula is a dot product with a UNIT vector.** Walk from $\mathbf{a}$ along a unit direction $\hat{u}$ and define $g(t) = f(\mathbf{a} + t\hat{u})$. By **Chain Rule and Implicit differentiation**, $g'(0) = f_x u_1 + f_y u_2$, so $D_{\hat u}f = \nabla f \cdot \hat u$. The word *unit* is load-bearing: a direction is a heading, not a distance, so normalise first, $\hat u = \vec u / \lVert \vec u \rVert$. Worked: at $(1,2)$ with $\nabla f = (3,4)$ and $\vec u = (0,3)$, normalise to $\hat u = (0,1)$, then $D_{\hat u}f = (3)(0) + (4)(1) = 4$. Skip the normalisation and you get $12$ — a change over three units of travel, not a rate. Divide by $\lVert \nabla f \rVert = 5$ instead and you get $12/5$, which normalised the wrong vector.

- **One identity explains every "which way should I move" question.** Since $\nabla f \cdot \hat u = \lVert \nabla f \rVert \cos\theta$, with $\theta$ the angle between direction and gradient, the directional derivative is a cosine sweep bounded by $\pm\lVert\nabla f\rVert$: largest at $\theta = 0$ (along $\nabla f$, steepest ascent), most negative at $\theta = 180°$ (along $-\nabla f$, steepest descent), and exactly **zero at $\theta = 90°$** — perpendicular to the gradient you move along a level curve and the value does not change. Worked on room temperature $T(x,y)$ with $\nabla T = (3,4)$: heading $(0.6, 0.8)$ warms at $+5$ per unit of distance, $(-0.6,-0.8)$ cools at $-5$, and $(0.8,-0.6)$ changes nothing. That is why moving opposite the gradient is the best single small step toward lower values: it decreases the function the most **per unit of distance moved**. Note the two limits on that claim — per unit distance, and local. It does not point at the minimum and does not get you there in one move.

- **Higher-order derivatives and the Hessian matrix collect all the second-order partial derivatives.** Differentiate twice and there are four results in two variables: $f_{xx}$, $f_{yy}$, and the mixed $f_{xy}$, $f_{yx}$. The **Hessian matrix** is $H = \begin{pmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy}\end{pmatrix}$ — second-order partials only; the first-order ones are the gradient. Clairaut's theorem: when the second partials are continuous the mixed ones are equal, so $H$ is symmetric. Worked: $f(x,y) = 2x^2 + 3y^2 - xy$ gives $f_x = 4x - y$ and $f_y = 6y - x$, so the only critical point is $(0,0)$, and $H = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ -1 & 6\end{pmatrix}$ — constant, because $f$ is quadratic. The mixed partial is $-1$, not zero; a nonzero $f_{xy}$ just means the inputs interact.

- **Eigenvalues of the Hessian are the multivariable second derivative test.** In one variable you asked whether $f''>0$ at a critical point. In many variables curvature depends on direction: it is $\hat u^{T} H \hat u$ along unit $\hat u$, and — recall from linear algebra that a symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues — the largest and smallest values that quantity takes are exactly the largest and smallest eigenvalues. So the analogue of $f'' > 0$ is **all eigenvalues of the Hessian positive**: the matrix is **positive definite**, every direction curves upward, the surface is locally concave up like a bowl, and the critical point is a **local minimum**. All negative is negative definite: concave down, a local maximum. Mixed signs is indefinite: a **saddle**, up one way and down another. A zero eigenvalue makes $H$ singular and the test inconclusive. Worked on the same $H$: trace $10$, determinant $23$, so $\lambda = \frac{10 \pm \sqrt{100-92}}{2} = 5 \pm \sqrt{2} \approx 6.41$ and $3.59$. Both positive, so $(0,0)$ is a local minimum. The two-variable shortcut says the same thing, since the determinant is the product of the eigenvalues: $D = f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2 = 23 > 0$ with $f_{xx} = 4 > 0$. Both objects meet in the second-order Taylor expansion $f(\mathbf{a} + \Delta) \approx f(\mathbf{a}) + \nabla f \cdot \Delta + \tfrac12 \Delta^{T} H \Delta$: gradient the tilt, Hessian the bend.

**Rules to remember**

- $f(x,y)$: two inputs, one output. Its graph is a surface; level curves are where the output is constant.
- To take a partial derivative, treat every other variable as a constant. $\partial(x^3y^2)/\partial x = 3x^2y^2$.
- $\nabla f = (f_x, f_y)$ is a vector of first partials; $H$ is a matrix of second partials. Never swap them.
- $\nabla f$ points along steepest ascent, $-\nabla f$ along steepest descent, $\lVert\nabla f\rVert$ is the rate.
- $D_{\hat u}f = \nabla f \cdot \hat u$ and $\hat u$ must be a unit vector. Normalise before the dot product.
- $D_{\hat u}f = \lVert\nabla f\rVert\cos\theta$: maximum along the gradient, minimum opposite it, and zero perpendicular to it — that direction is tangent to the level curve, so it means *no change*, not descent.
- Tangent plane: $z = f(a,b) + f_x(a,b)(x-a) + f_y(a,b)(y-b)$. Two intersecting tangent lines fix one plane.
- At a critical point: all eigenvalues positive $\Rightarrow$ positive definite $\Rightarrow$ concave up $\Rightarrow$ local minimum. All negative $\Rightarrow$ local maximum. Mixed $\Rightarrow$ saddle. Any zero $\Rightarrow$ inconclusive.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Forgetting to normalise the direction.** The raw dot product $\nabla f \cdot \vec u$ scales with the length of $\vec u$, so it is not a rate at all.
- **Normalising the gradient instead of the direction.** Dividing by $\lVert\nabla f\rVert$ gives a plausible fraction answering a different question.
- **Calling the gradient a number, or a matrix.** It is a vector, one component per input; the matrix of second partials is the Hessian.
- **Saying the gradient points downhill.** It points *uphill*; descent is the negative gradient. Many distractors here hinge on that sign.
- **Believing perpendicular-to-the-gradient descends.** It is the one direction of zero change.
- **Differentiating the wrong letter.** For $x^3y^2$, $f_x = 3x^2y$ has silently differentiated $y$ too.
- **Expecting the mixed partials to vanish.** $f_{xy} = -1$ for $2x^2+3y^2-xy$; interaction terms are ordinary.
- **Putting first derivatives in the Hessian, or taking a determinant of the gradient.** A vector has no determinant.
- **Treating the negative gradient as a route to the minimum.** It is the best *infinitesimal* step, at that point only.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Read what the object *is* before what it does: vector of first partials (gradient), matrix of second partials (Hessian), single number (a partial or a directional derivative). Most distractors are the right sentence about the wrong one.
2. On any directional derivative, write $\lVert\vec u\rVert$ down first. If it is not $1$, normalise — the un-normalised dot product and the divide-by-$\lVert\nabla f\rVert$ variant are both on offer.
3. For "which direction" questions, map the words onto $\cos\theta$: fastest increase $\theta = 0$, fastest decrease $\theta = 180°$, no change $\theta = 90°$. Room-temperature scenarios are this identity in disguise.
4. When asked *why* the negative gradient is best, the answer names the largest decrease **per unit of distance**, never a guarantee of reaching the minimum.
5. For a partial derivative, restate the expression with the other variable replaced by a constant, then apply the power rule.
6. For Hessian classification, get the eigenvalue *signs*, not their values; trace and determinant are the fast route.
7. Options naming a probability, a volume, or a global maximum's location are filler — no derivative here returns those.
