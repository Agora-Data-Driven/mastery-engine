1. Slice To Slope

Derivative as slope and rate of change measured the slope of a curve, and Tangent lines and linear approximations laid a straight line along it. A surface has no single slope — but freeze one input and it hands you back a curve you already know how to differentiate. Choose which input to freeze, then walk the point along. Like slicing a loaf: every cut face is a flat outline you can trace with one finger.

The whole lesson stands on this one move. A partial derivative introduces no new limit and no new rule — it is the ordinary derivative of the ordinary curve you get by holding the other inputs still. Everything that follows is bookkeeping about how many of these slopes you keep and what you do with them once you have them.

2. Partials To Gradient

Two slopes are two numbers. Stack them and they become a vector with a direction on the map: the way uphill. Move the point and watch the two bars swing, the arrow turn, and the arrow stay square to the contour it is standing on. Like a compass needle on a hillside: it does not care where you came from, only which way is up.

The gradient is a vector, one component per input — not a single number measuring curvature and not a matrix of second derivatives. Sizes are the giveaway in an exam question: a function of two variables has a gradient with two entries and a Hessian with four.

3. Unit Vector Rule

A directional derivative is the rate of change as you set off in a chosen direction, and it is one dot product — with a unit vector. Stretch the direction arrow and watch the honest answer refuse to move while the careless one balloons. Like asking how steep a road is: the answer is the same whether you measure one metre of it or three.

Every "which way should I move" question is this cosine in disguise. The answer is bounded by plus and minus the gradient's length, it is largest along the gradient, most negative opposite it, and exactly zero at right angles — and a direction that is not a unit vector answers a different question entirely.

4. Cooling Fastest

A room with two heaters, temperature T(x, y). Stand somewhere, choose a heading and a step length, and compare what the tangent plane predicted with what the room actually did. The negative gradient wins every time — over a small enough step. Like backing away from a bonfire: the quickest relief is straight away from it, and only for the first pace.

Two boundaries on the claim, and both are examinable. The negative gradient is best per unit of distance — not per step of some fixed shape — and it is best only at the point where the gradient was evaluated. Take a long enough step and it can walk you straight past the cool patch and back into the other heater.

5. Hessian Decides

A zero gradient says "critical point" and nothing more. The second derivatives say which kind. Move the coefficients of f = a·x^2 + b·y^2 + c·x·y and watch the curvature curve, its two extremes — the eigenvalues — and the verdict change together. Like a skateboard park: a bowl holds you, a hump throws you off, and a saddle does both depending on which way you roll.

The one-variable test asked a single question, f''(x) > 0. In many variables curvature depends on the direction you leave in, so the honest generalisation is a question about every direction at once — and the eigenvalues of the Hessian are exactly the best and worst cases. All positive is the bowl; all negative is the dome; one of each is the saddle.
