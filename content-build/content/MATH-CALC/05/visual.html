1. Gradient To Step

Partial derivatives and the Gradient vector already hand you an arrow pointing straight uphill, and Directional derivatives already say its length is the fastest rate of increase available at that point. This lesson adds one minus sign and one number. Drag the point, then drag the learning rate, and watch the direction and the distance come from two different places. Like a hiker in fog who can only feel the slope under their boots: turn to face downhill, then decide how far to walk before feeling again.

Notice what the minus sign is doing and what it is not. It flips direction only — the gradient still has to be recomputed at the new point, because it is a function of where you stand and not a constant you may cache. Flip the sign back to a plus and the identical machinery becomes gradient ascent and climbs the surface, which is how the same code maximises a likelihood.

2. Why Squares Not Signs

Three points, one line, two candidate costs. Press the two preset lines and read the signed total on both: it cannot tell a good fit from a terrible one, because the residuals cancel. Then take gradient steps and watch m and b — not the data — move. Like a bank statement that only shows your net change: minus 500 and plus 500 reads as a quiet month.

Both preset lines score a signed total of exactly zero, and that is not a coincidence: whenever the intercept is free to move, the least-squares fit drives the signed residuals to zero anyway. A cost that both lines satisfy equally cannot rank them, which is the real argument for squaring — not that squaring is convenient, but that the signed sum carries no information about size at all.

3. The Learning Rate Dial

One cost, J(theta) = a·theta^2, and the entire update collapses to theta ← (1 - 2a·alpha)·theta. Drag alpha to walk through all four regimes, then drag the curvature and watch the same alpha change verdict — which is why no formula picks it for you. Like the hot tap in an unfamiliar shower: the right quarter-turn here scalds you in the next hotel.

The stable window is alpha < 1/a and the perfect step is alpha = 1/(2a) — both written in terms of the curvature, which you can rarely measure and which changes from point to point on a real surface. That is the honest form of "no single method is guaranteed to work for every problem": the ideal rate exists, it is just not something you get to know in advance.

4. Valleys And Restarts

The same algorithm, the same learning rate, one thing changed: where it started. On f(x) = x^4 - 4x^2 + 0.5x descent is a purely local rule, so it falls into whichever valley it woke up in. Drag the start, then press restart-many and watch the strategy do what a single run cannot. Like dropping a marble on a mountain range in the dark: it always finds a low point, never the lowest.

Restarts are a bet, not a proof. Twelve runs raise the chance of meeting the global minimum; nothing here can certify that you have found it, because gradient descent never sees more of the surface than the point it is standing on. On a convex cost the question disappears — there is only one basin — which is exactly why convexity is worth so much.

5. Newton Versus Descent

Newton's method fits a parabola through the local slope and curvature and jumps to its vertex: x - g'(x)/g''(x). Pick a function, drag the start, and compare iteration counts — then try the double well from near the origin and watch what "chases critical points" really means. Like judging a hill by feeling both the slope and how sharply it curves, instead of taking fixed paces.

Everything Newton buys is paid for by the second derivative. In n dimensions that is the Hessian: about n^2 numbers to store and n^3 work to invert every step, so at a million parameters it is not merely slow but impossible — which is why large models stay first-order and settle for tuning a learning rate instead.
