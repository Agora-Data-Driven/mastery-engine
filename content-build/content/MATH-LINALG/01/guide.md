**The big idea**: This is the first lesson of the course, so the ground it stands on is your own. Recall from **algebra** that a function is a rule from an input to an output, and that $g(f(x))$ means "do $f$ first, then $g$"; recall from **trigonometry** that turning the plane by $90^\circ$ sends the point $(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$; recall from **core ML** that a linear layer scores an example with a weighted sum of its features. This lesson makes those three things one thing. A vector is an arrow with size and heading that combines with other arrows one coordinate at a time. A linear transformation is a rule that sends every point of the plane to another point of the plane, holding the origin still and keeping the grid straight and evenly spaced — which forces it to be completely determined by where it sends just two arrows, and a matrix is exactly the table that stores those two answers. And multiplying matrices is not a new arithmetic to memorise: it is what happens when you do one transformation after another.

**Key concepts**

- **Vector operations: Addition, scalar multiplication, and dot products.** A vector represents **both a magnitude and a direction** — not a location with no size, and not a square matrix written compactly. Addition and subtraction are carried out **component by component**, never by multiplying magnitudes and adding angles; scalar multiplication multiplies every component by the same number, which rescales the magnitude and flips the direction when the scalar is negative. The dot product is the odd one out: $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2$ returns a single *number*, not a vector. Worked: with $\mathbf{u} = (3,1)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (-1,3)$, $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = (2,4)$, $\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = (4,-2)$, $2\mathbf{u} = (6,2)$, and $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = (3)(-1) + (1)(3) = 0$.
- **Length and angle both fall out of the dot product.** $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}}$, and $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$. Above, $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{10}$, so $\cos\theta = 0/10 = 0$ and the two arrows sit at exactly $90^\circ$. The sign alone is a fast read: positive means the angle is acute, zero means perpendicular, negative means obtuse. Without this one operation you could measure nothing — no length, no angle, no projection later.
- **The dot product is the atom of every product in this course.** In matrix multiplication, the operation applied between a row of the first matrix and a column of the second to produce a single entry **is the dot product** — not the determinant, not a cross product, not an inverse. Every claim in the rest of this lesson is that same sum of pairwise products, arranged differently.
- **Geometric interpretation of vectors and transformations.** Draw a vector as an arrow from the origin to its coordinates. Then addition is tip-to-tail: walk $\mathbf{u}$, then walk $\mathbf{v}$ from where you landed, and the arrow from origin to finish is $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$. Scalar multiplication slides the tip along the line through the origin. **In the plane, a linear transformation is a rule that sends each point of the plane to another point in the plane** — not a rule assigning a single number to each point, and not the graph of a surface over the plane. Two visual consequences: straight lines stay straight and evenly spaced, and **the origin is always sent to the origin itself**. That last one is forced, not decreed: $T(\mathbf{0}) = T(0\cdot\mathbf{v}) = 0\,T(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}$.
- **Watch the unit square and you have watched the whole plane.** The image of the fundamental square spanned by the standard basis vectors $\mathbf{e}_1 = (1,0)$ and $\mathbf{e}_2 = (0,1)$ is so informative because **the square and its image show how the matrix reshapes the entire plane**. Three worked cases. $A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}$: $\mathbf{e}_1$ stays at $(1,0)$, $\mathbf{e}_2$ moves to $(0,-1)$, and $(2,3)$ moves to $(2,-3)$ — a reflection across the $x$-axis. $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ sends $(3,1)$ to $(-1,3)$, and the whole plane rotates $90^\circ$ counterclockwise about the origin. $A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ sends $(2,1)$ to $(4,1)$: a **horizontal shear**, because heights are untouched while horizontal displacement grows with height.
- **One vector's image never identifies the transformation — the square does.** A horizontal scaling $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ *also* sends $(2,1)$ to $(4,1)$. The two rules are told apart only by the basis: the shear leaves $\mathbf{e}_1$ at $(1,0)$ and drags $\mathbf{e}_2$ to $(2,1)$, while the scaling stretches $\mathbf{e}_1$ to $(2,0)$ and leaves $\mathbf{e}_2$ alone. This is the entire reason the unit square, rather than a favourite test vector, is the object to look at.
- **Linear transformations and matrix-vector multiplication.** The definition is two equations: $T(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$ and $T(c\,\mathbf{u}) = c\,T(\mathbf{u})$. Now derive the matrix rather than accepting it. Any input splits as $\mathbf{x} = x\,\mathbf{e}_1 + y\,\mathbf{e}_2$, so linearity gives $T(\mathbf{x}) = x\,T(\mathbf{e}_1) + y\,T(\mathbf{e}_2)$. The whole rule is pinned down by two output vectors, and the matrix is simply the table holding them: **its columns are $T(\mathbf{e}_1)$ and $T(\mathbf{e}_2)$**. Worked: for $T(x,y) = (2x - y,\; x + 3y)$, $T(\mathbf{e}_1) = (2,1)$ and $T(\mathbf{e}_2) = (-1,3)$, so $A = \begin{bmatrix}2 & -1\\ 1 & 3\end{bmatrix}$.
- **Verifying additivity means general vectors, not a lucky pair.** For that same $T$, take $\mathbf{u} = (x_1,y_1)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (x_2,y_2)$: $T(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = (2(x_1{+}x_2) - (y_1{+}y_2),\; (x_1{+}x_2) + 3(y_1{+}y_2))$, and regrouping term by term splits it into $(2x_1 - y_1,\; x_1 + 3y_1) + (2x_2 - y_2,\; x_2 + 3y_2) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$. Numerically with $\mathbf{u} = (1,2)$, $\mathbf{v} = (3,-1)$: $T(4,1) = (7,7)$, while $T(\mathbf{u}) = (0,7)$ and $T(\mathbf{v}) = (7,0)$ sum to $(7,7)$.
- **Two readings of $A\mathbf{x}$, and you want both.** Row reading: output entry $i$ is the dot product of row $i$ with $\mathbf{x}$ — that is the arithmetic. Column reading: $A\mathbf{x} = x\,(\text{column }1) + y\,(\text{column }2)$ — that is the geometry. With $A$ above and $\mathbf{x} = (2,1)$: rows give $(2\cdot2 - 1\cdot1,\; 1\cdot2 + 3\cdot1) = (3,5)$; columns give $2(2,1) + 1(-1,3) = (3,5)$. Same number, and the column view is why the answer is called a linear combination.
- **Matrix multiplication as a composition of transformations.** Geometrically, multiplying two matrices means **composing two linear transformations into a single one**: $(AB)\mathbf{x} = A(B\mathbf{x})$, so $B$ acts first. The product exists only when the **number of columns of $A$ equals the number of rows of $B$** — the inner dimensions must agree — and the result carries the outer ones: a $2\times3$ times a $3\times4$ is $2\times4$. Entry $(i,j)$ of the product is the dot product of **a row of the first matrix with a column of the second**. Column-wise, column $j$ of $AB$ is $A$ applied to column $j$ of $B$, which is precisely where the composite sends $\mathbf{e}_j$.
- **Why the written order looks backwards.** The vector multiplies on the right, so the transformation applied first sits closest to it — read $AB\mathbf{x}$ right to left. It matters because order changes the answer: with $R = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ (rotate) and $S = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ (shear), $RS = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ but $SR = \begin{bmatrix}2 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$. Applied to $\mathbf{e}_1$: shear-then-rotate gives $(0,1)$, rotate-then-shear gives $(2,1)$. Composition is associative, never commutative.

**Rules to remember**

- Vectors add and subtract component by component; a scalar multiplies every component.
- $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ is a number: positive is acute, zero is perpendicular, negative is obtuse. $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}}$.
- Linear means additive *and* homogeneous. The origin staying fixed is a consequence, so a moving origin instantly disqualifies a rule.
- The columns of a matrix are the images of the basis vectors. To read a matrix geometrically, read its columns.
- $A\mathbf{x}$ two ways: rows dotted with $\mathbf{x}$ for the arithmetic, columns combined with weights $\mathbf{x}$ for the picture.
- $AB$ exists only when columns of $A$ equal rows of $B$; the shape is rows of $A$ by columns of $B$.
- Entry $(i,j)$ of $AB$ is row $i$ of $A$ dotted with column $j$ of $B$.
- $AB$ means "$B$ first, then $A$", because the vector sits on the right. Expect $AB \neq BA$.

**Common pitfalls**

- Calling a vector a location with no size, or a compact way of writing a square matrix. It is magnitude plus direction.
- Trying to add vectors by multiplying magnitudes and adding angles, or by converting them to matrices first. Components, always.
- Naming the determinant or the cross product as the operation behind a matrix-product entry. It is the dot product.
- Assuming a rule is linear because it has a tidy formula. $T(x,y) = (2x - y + 1,\; x + 3y)$ sends the origin to $(1,0)$, so it is affine, not linear.
- Assuming a fixed origin is enough. $T(x,y) = (x^2, y)$ holds the origin and still fails additivity: $T(1,0) + T(1,0) = (2,0)$ but $T(2,0) = (4,0)$.
- Verifying $T(2\mathbf{u}) = 2T(\mathbf{u})$ and calling addition proven. That is homogeneity; additivity needs two independent vectors.
- Matching dimensions backwards — rows of $A$ against columns of $B$. The *inner* pair must agree.
- Writing the composition in reading order. "Shear then rotate" is $RS$, not $SR$.
- Identifying a transformation from one vector's image, when a shear and a scaling can agree on it.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Given a matrix and asked what it does, read the two columns as the new homes of $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ and sketch the unit square. Rotation, reflection, shear and scaling are then visibly different, even when a single test vector cannot separate them.
2. Given "compute $A\mathbf{v}$ and name the effect", do the arithmetic by rows, then name the effect from the columns. Distractors usually pair the right vector with the wrong description.
3. For a linearity check, the correct answer introduces general $\mathbf{u} = (x_1,y_1)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (x_2,y_2)$, expands $T(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})$, and regroups into $T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$. Reject options that only scale one vector or that argue from the origin alone.
4. For shape questions write the dimensions adjacent — $(2\times\mathbf{3})(\mathbf{3}\times4)$ — check the inner pair, and read the outer pair as the answer.
5. For any "why this order" question, put the vector on the right and work outwards. The matrix nearest the vector acted first.
6. Origin questions are free marks: the answer is always the origin itself, never the determinant and never the first column.

**Where this leads**: every transformation here was handed to you as a matrix, and every question ran forwards — given $A$ and $\mathbf{x}$, find $A\mathbf{x}$. The next lesson, **02 Systems of Linear Equations and RREF**, runs the arrow backwards: given $A$ and the output $\mathbf{b}$, which inputs $\mathbf{x}$ get there? It introduces the machinery for answering that at any size — writing a system of equations as a matrix, elementary row operations and Gaussian elimination, row echelon and reduced row echelon form, the rank of a matrix, linear dependence and independence, and the conditions under which a system has one solution, none, or infinitely many. The column reading you just built is what makes that lesson intelligible: asking whether $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution is asking whether $\mathbf{b}$ can be reached by combining the columns of $A$.
