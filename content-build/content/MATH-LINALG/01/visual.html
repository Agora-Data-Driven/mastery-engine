1. The Linearity Test

You already know how to test a function: feed it things and check the algebra. Linear algebra keeps that test word for word and only changes what goes in — a number becomes an arrow. Pick a rule, move the two inputs, and watch the same two equations decide both sides. Like a scale that stays honest whether you weigh the shopping in one trip or two.

Why a table of numbers and not just one? A linear rule on the number line is pinned down by a single answer, f(1). In the plane there are two independent directions to ask about, so it takes two answers — where e1 = (1, 0) goes and where e2 = (0, 1) goes — and those two answers, written side by side as columns, are the matrix. Visual 3 is that sentence made visible.

2. Add, Scale, Dot

Three operations, and only two of them return an arrow. Drag the components and watch tip-to-tail addition, stretching along a line through the origin, and the one operation that answers with a plain number — the angle between them. Like two walks across a field: adding is doing both walks, the dot product is asking how much the second walk agreed with the first.

Keep the return types straight, because every later formula depends on it: addition and scalar multiplication give arrows, the dot product gives a number. That number is the atom of everything ahead — every single entry of a matrix product is one dot product, which is what visuals 4 and 5 are built out of.

3. Where The Basis Goes

A matrix is not four numbers, it is two answers: where e1 lands and where e2 lands, written as columns. Move them and the whole plane follows — watch the unit square become the image of the whole grid, and the origin never move. Like bending a wire fence: you only need to say where two adjacent posts moved, and every panel follows.

Try the trap on purpose: set the shear, note where the test vector (2, 1) lands, then set the horizontal scaling. Same image, different transformation. One vector can never identify the rule — which is exactly why the fundamental square spanned by the standard basis vectors is the thing to look at, and why exam questions hand you a matrix and expect you to read its columns.

4. Rows Dot, Columns Combine

One product, two honest readings. Rows dotted with the input give you the arithmetic; columns weighted by the input give you the picture. Switch between them and watch the same answer arrive twice — then check when a product is even allowed to exist. Like a recipe read two ways: per-ingredient totals, or "two of these plus three of those".

is the product AB even defined?

The row reading is how you compute and the column reading is how you think. Keep both: the row reading is why every entry of any product is a dot product, and the column reading is why the output of Ax is called a linear combination of the columns — the fact lesson 2 turns into "which right-hand sides are reachable at all".

5. Order Changes Everything

Multiplying matrices is doing one transformation after another. Pick which happens first, watch the square travel through both stages, and tap any entry of the product to see the single dot product that made it. Like getting dressed: socks then shoes is not shoes then socks.

Composition is associative — you may group the multiplications however you like — but almost never commutative. Every chained pipeline you meet later (rotate then project, whiten then rotate, a stack of network layers) is this same product, and its order is part of its meaning, not a formatting choice.
