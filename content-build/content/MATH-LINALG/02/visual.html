1. Columns To Equations

Linear transformations and matrix-vector multiplication ran forwards: pick the weights, watch Ax land somewhere. Solving runs it backwards — the landing spot b is given and the weights are the unknown. Drag the two weights until you hit it. Like mixing two paint colours to match a swatch: the swatch is fixed, the two amounts are what you are hunting for.

A b [ A | b ] [ 2 1 ] [ 5 ] [ 2 1 | 5 ] <- row 1 IS equation 1 [ 1 3 ] [ 5 ] [ 1 3 | 5 ] <- row 2 IS equation 2 ^ ^ ^ ^ ^ x y x y constants ride in the LAST COLUMN

Notice what the sliders never touch: the matrix. The augmented matrix is the question, not the answer — it stores the coefficient structure, and the constants ride along as a final column purely so they cannot be left behind during elimination. That separation is why singularity, later, can be decided from A alone.

2. Row Operations Sandbox

Three legal moves, and nothing else. Apply them by hand to the augmented matrix and watch two numbers refuse to move: the known solution still satisfies every row, and the rank never changes. Like re-arranging the furniture in a room — everything looks different and the room is exactly the same size.

boxed = the leading entry of that row (the pivot, once the staircase is built)  ·  the column after the rule is the constants column

Every one of the three moves is reversible, and that is the whole licence for doing them: undoing one is another move of the same kind, so no solution can be created or destroyed. That is also why k = 0 is refused above — scaling by zero cannot be undone. It would erase an equation and hand you back solutions the original system never had.

3. Echelon Two Ways

REF and RREF differ by exactly one requirement, and it costs a few more row operations to buy an answer you can read without thinking. Then meet the free variable: a column with no pivot, and therefore a dial you are allowed to turn. Like a recipe that says "season to taste" — every other quantity is fixed, that one is yours.

A free variable is free, not zero. Setting it to 0 and reporting a single triple is the most common way to answer an "infinitely many solutions" question wrongly: you have named one point on a line and called it the answer. And the count is bookkeeping you can always do — free variables = columns minus rank — which is the fact lesson 03 promotes into the Rank-Nullity Theorem.

4. Rank And Dependence

The formal definition of dependence asks for one specific object: a set of weights, not all zero, that combines the vectors into the zero vector. Hunt for it with the sliders and watch the rank agree with whatever you find. Like three witnesses at a trial: if one is only repeating what another said, you have two statements, not three.

Two shortcuts save you the search almost every time. If one vector is a scalar multiple of another — two arrows on one line through the origin, say one pointing east and one exactly west — the set is dependent whatever the rest are doing. And if you have more vectors than the dimension of the space, dependence is guaranteed with no arithmetic at all: three vectors in the plane can never be independent. A basis is the set that does both jobs at once — spans the space and is independent.

5. Singular Or Not

Two dials on one system: a coefficient, and a constant. Only one of them can make the matrix singular — and once it is, the other decides between "no solution" and "infinitely many". Like two people giving directions: if they disagree you are stuck, and if one just repeats the other you learned nothing new.

Drag c from end to end and watch rank(A) sit perfectly still. That is the whole reason the right-hand constants can be ignored when you classify a system as singular or non-singular: singularity is a statement about the coefficient structure alone. The constants decide something different — whether a singular system is merely redundant or outright contradictory.
