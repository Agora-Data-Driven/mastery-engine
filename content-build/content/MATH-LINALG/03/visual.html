1. Reading The RREF

You already run Elementary row operations and Gaussian elimination to reach REF and Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF), read the Rank of a matrix off the pivots, and use Linear dependence and independence to say which columns were redundant. That same reduction hands you four subspaces. Edit any entry and watch all four move. Like an X-ray you already take for one broken bone that happens to show the whole skeleton.

Two of the four live in the input space R^n and two in the output space R^m, and one number sets all four sizes: r, n-r, r, m-r. That is why elimination is not just a way to solve a system — it is the one computation that answers every structural question about the matrix at once.

2. Is It A Subspace

A subspace has to contain the origin and survive both operations — for every scalar, negatives included. Pick a candidate set, slide the vector along it, then slide the scalar and watch where the result lands. Like a club that must stay a club after every member is doubled, reversed or added to another.

The same three tests decide sets that are not arrows at all. Polynomials of degree at most 2 form a vector space; polynomials of degree exactly 2 do not, because (x^2 + x) + (-x^2 + 1) = x + 1 has degree 1 and the zero polynomial is missing. Closure is the whole definition, whatever the vectors happen to be.

3. Span Line Or Plane

A span is measured in independent directions, not in vectors. Switch vectors in and out, and slide v's last entry through the moment it stops being a multiple of u. Like torches in a dark room: a second torch pointing the same way lights nothing new.

Every span is a subspace, and the smallest of them is not empty: span{0} = {0}, the trivial subspace of dimension 0. At the other end, a set can span a space perfectly well and still fail to be a basis — {(1,2), (2,4), (3,5)} spans all of R^2, yet three vectors in a 2-dimensional space can never be independent, so the set is not minimal.

4. Pivot Columns Win

Row operations keep the row space and wreck the column space. That single asymmetry decides where each basis comes from: rows from the RREF, columns from the original A. Switch sides and check every vector for yourself. Like re-sorting a deck: the set of cards survives, the order of the piles does not.

Consistency is the same question in disguise: b is in Col A exactly when Ax = b is solvable, which the RREF of [A b] shows as no pivot in the last column. And the columns span the whole of R^m only when every row of rref(A) carries a pivot — here a zero row appears, so they never do.

5. Free Variable Machine

Pivot variables are hostages: the free variables choose, and the pivots are forced. Turn the free dials and watch a whole subspace of solutions appear, with Rx staying exactly zero. Like a recipe where you pick the spices and the flour is then decided for you.

The dimension of the null space is the number of free variables, so it is n - r by counting alone: a 5 x 7 matrix of rank 3 has nullity 4, whatever its entries are. And the special solutions are independent for a structural reason — each one holds a 1 in a slot where all the others hold 0, so no combination of the rest can imitate it.

6. Rows That Vanish

The left null space is the list of recipes that make the rows cancel — the very combinations elimination used when it produced a zero row. Mix the three rows and hunt for zero. Like three ingredients that, in one exact ratio, taste of nothing at all.

Dimension by counting: an m x n matrix of rank r has a left null space of dimension m - r, so a 4 x 3 matrix of rank 2 leaves 2 independent relationships among its 4 rows. It is the Rank-Nullity Theorem applied to A transpose, and the reason the four subspaces come in two matched pairs — r and n - r inside R^n, r and m - r inside R^m.
