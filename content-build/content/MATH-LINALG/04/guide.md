**The big idea**: **Singularity and solvability of systems** already handed you a verdict — a square matrix either has an inverse or it collapses — and you reached it by counting pivots with **Elementary row operations and Gaussian elimination** through **REF and Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF)**, which is the **Rank of a matrix**. That verdict is binary: invertible, or not. This lesson compresses the same fact into a single signed number that also says *how much* the transformation stretches space and *which way round* it leaves it — and then shows that this number, assembled the right way, writes the inverse down in closed form. **Linear dependence and independence** is the thread running underneath: dependent columns enclose less volume than independent ones, and "less" turns out to mean exactly zero.

**Key concepts**

- **Determinants: Geometric meaning and calculation.** Mechanism: push the unit square through $A$ and it leaves as a parallelogram spanned by the columns of $A$; $\det(A)$ is that parallelogram's *signed* area. The magnitude $|\det(A)|$ is the factor every area is multiplied by; the sign records orientation — positive means orientation preserved, negative means the plane was flipped over. For $A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$, $\det(A)=3\cdot2-1\cdot1=5$: areas scale by 5, orientation preserved. Run it backwards — a transformation that sends the unit square of area 1 to a region of area 5 without flipping has determinant $5$. In three dimensions the same number is a volume factor. The $2\times2$ rule is $ad-bc$, main diagonal minus anti-diagonal; $ac-bd$ and $a+d-b-c$ are the classic decoys.
- **Why zero means singular, and the reciprocal rule.** If the columns are dependent, the parallelogram is squashed flat onto a line, and a line has zero area — so $\det=0$ is the exact geometric statement of "collapses to a lower-dimensional set", of rank deficiency, and of "no inverse". Determinants also multiply, $\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)$, which derives the reciprocal rule in one line: $AA^{-1}=I$ gives $\det(A)\det(A^{-1})=\det(I)=1$, so $\det(A^{-1})=1/\det(A)$. With $\det(A)=8$, $\det(A^{-1})=\tfrac18$ — undoing an eightfold area stretch has to shrink area eightfold.
- **Minors: deleting a row and a column.** The minor $M_{ij}$ is the determinant of what survives after **deleting** row $i$ and column $j$ — the indices name what you *cross out*, never what you keep. An $n\times n$ matrix therefore yields $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ minors: in a $4\times4$, $M_{24}$ is the determinant of a $3\times3$ block. Worked: for $A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&3\\0&4&5\\6&7&8\end{bmatrix}$, $M_{23}$ deletes row 2 and column 3, leaving $\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\6&7\end{bmatrix}$, so $M_{23}=2\cdot7-1\cdot6=8$. For $\begin{bmatrix}1&0&2\\3&1&4\\2&5&1\end{bmatrix}$, $M_{11}=\det\begin{bmatrix}1&4\\5&1\end{bmatrix}=1-20=-19$. In a $2\times2$ the survivor is a single entry: for $B=\begin{bmatrix}3&-2\\1&5\end{bmatrix}$, $M_{22}=3$.
- **Cofactors and the checkerboard sign pattern.** A cofactor is a signed minor: $C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$. The signs form a checkerboard starting with $+$ at the top left, and the rule depends only on the *position*, never on the entry's own sign or value. So $C_{ij}=M_{ij}$ exactly when $i+j$ is even, and $C_{ij}=-M_{ij}$ when it is odd: $M_{31}=7$ gives $C_{31}=7$ because $3+1=4$; $C_{23}$ carries $-1$ because $2+3=5$ is odd (not because $2\cdot3$ is even — the exponent is a *sum*). Size is irrelevant: in a $5\times5$, $C_{32}$ is still negative. Worked: for $\begin{bmatrix}2&1&0\\3&4&5\\0&1&1\end{bmatrix}$, $M_{12}=\det\begin{bmatrix}3&5\\0&1\end{bmatrix}=3$ and $C_{12}=-3$. Without these alternating signs the expansion below would not agree with the geometry, and different rows would give different answers.
- **Cofactor expansion: the recursive determinant.** Fix any single row $i$ and $\det(A)=\sum_j a_{ij}C_{ij}$; fix any single column $j$ and $\det(A)=\sum_i a_{ij}C_{ij}$. Every line gives the same number — expanding along row 2 and along column 3 of the same matrix both return $\det(A)$, with no sign correction and no symmetry requirement. It is genuinely recursive: each cofactor is a smaller determinant, shrinking until $1\times1$. Worked along row 1 of $\begin{bmatrix}2&1&0\\3&-1&2\\0&1&1\end{bmatrix}$: $2(-1-2)-1(3-0)+0=-6-3=-9$. Zeros are free, because a zero entry kills its whole term before you compute the minor, so **pick the line with the most zeros**: in $\begin{bmatrix}3&0&2\\5&0&1\\4&7&6\end{bmatrix}$ column 2 has two zeros and costs one minor instead of three. Along column 2 of $\begin{bmatrix}1&0&2\\3&0&-1\\2&4&5\end{bmatrix}$ only $a_{32}=4$ survives: $C_{32}=-\det\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&-1\end{bmatrix}=-(-7)=7$, so $\det=4\cdot7=28$. One entry's contribution is always $a_{ij}C_{ij}$: in $\begin{bmatrix}2&0&1\\1&3&2\\0&4&0\end{bmatrix}$, $a_{32}=4$ contributes $4\cdot(-1)\cdot\det\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}=-12$. Expanding an upper triangular $4\times4$ repeatedly along its first column leaves exactly one surviving term each time, which proves $\det(U)=u_{11}u_{22}u_{33}u_{44}$ — the product of the diagonal, not the sum.
- **The adjugate: transpose of the cofactor matrix.** Replace every entry by its cofactor to get the cofactor matrix, then **transpose**: $\operatorname{adj}(A)=C^{T}$, so $\operatorname{adj}(A)_{ij}=C_{ji}$ and the $(2,3)$ position of the adjugate holds $C_{32}$, not $C_{23}$. The payoff is the identity $A\operatorname{adj}(A)=\det(A)I$ for *every* square matrix. Its diagonal entries are just cofactor expansions along a row; the off-diagonal ones are expansions of a matrix with two identical rows, which has zero volume, so they vanish. Taking determinants of both sides gives $\det(A)\det(\operatorname{adj}A)=\det(A)^n$, hence $\det(\operatorname{adj}A)=\det(A)^{n-1}$: a $3\times3$ with $\det=2$ has $\det(\operatorname{adj}A)=4$, and with $\det=5$ it is $25$. For $2\times2$ the whole construction collapses to swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal: $\operatorname{adj}\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\1&4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4&-2\\-1&3\end{bmatrix}$.
- **Two roads to the inverse: adjugate formula and Gauss-Jordan.** Road one is algebraic and falls straight out of the identity above: divide it by the scalar $\det(A)$ to get $A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det(A)}\operatorname{adj}(A)$ — one over the determinant, times the adjugate, with no extra transpose. For $A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$, $\det=10$ and $A^{-1}=\frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix}4&-1\\-2&3\end{bmatrix}$. Road two is the one you already own: row-reduce the augmented block $[A\mid I]$, and when the left block reaches $I$ the right block is $A^{-1}$. It works because each row operation is left-multiplication by an elementary matrix, so $E_k\cdots E_1A=I$ means that product *is* $A^{-1}$ — and it has been applied to $I$ on the right the whole time. Landing on $[I\mid B]$ therefore justifies $AB=BA=I$, i.e. $B=A^{-1}$, and nothing about symmetry or orthogonality. Worked: $\begin{bmatrix}2&5\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}3&-5\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$, $\begin{bmatrix}4&3\\5&4\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}4&-3\\-5&4\end{bmatrix}$, $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&7\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}7&-2\\-3&1\end{bmatrix}$ — all clean because each has $\det=1$. Both roads break on the same condition and for the same reason: $C=\begin{bmatrix}2&4\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ has $\det=0$, so the formula divides by zero while elimination produces a zero row and can never reach $I$. Choose by size: the adjugate needs $n^2$ minors and blows up factorially, while Gauss-Jordan is about $n^3$ — so the formula is for $2\times2$, $3\times3$ and proofs, and elimination is for everything a computer does.
- **Inverse matrices and their role in transformations.** Their role is to *undo*: if $A$ maps $x$ to $b$, then $A^{-1}$ maps $b$ back to $x$, which is exactly why $Ax=b$ is solved by $x=A^{-1}b$. Undoing is only possible when no two distinct inputs share an output — if $Ax_1=Ax_2=b$ with $x_1\neq x_2$, then $A^{-1}b$ would have to be two different vectors at once. A determinant of zero is precisely that failure: the collapse crushes a whole line of inputs (a nontrivial null space) onto a single point, so $\det(A)=0$ means $A$ has **no** inverse — not infinitely many, and not one with determinant zero. Worked: $A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\6&4\end{bmatrix}$ has $\det=2$ and $\operatorname{adj}(A)=\begin{bmatrix}4&-1\\-6&2\end{bmatrix}$, so $A^{-1}=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}4&-1\\-6&2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&-0.5\\-3&1\end{bmatrix}$, and multiplying $AA^{-1}$ returns $I$.

**Rules to remember**

- $|\det(A)|$ is the area/volume factor; its sign is orientation. $\det(A)=0$ means collapse.
- $2\times2$: $ad-bc$. $\det(A^{-1})=1/\det(A)$, and $\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)$.
- $M_{ij}$ deletes row $i$ and column $j$, leaving an $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ determinant.
- $C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$. Equal when $i+j$ is even, opposite when odd. Position only.
- $\det(A)=\sum_j a_{ij}C_{ij}$ along any row, or any column. Every line agrees.
- Triangular matrices: the determinant is the product of the diagonal.
- $\operatorname{adj}(A)=C^{T}$, and $A\operatorname{adj}(A)=\det(A)I$ always, so $\det(\operatorname{adj}A)=\det(A)^{n-1}$.
- $A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det(A)}\operatorname{adj}(A)$, or the right block of $[A\mid I]\to[I\mid A^{-1}]$.

**Common pitfalls**

- Reading $M_{23}$ as "keep row 2 and column 3". It deletes them.
- Attaching the checkerboard sign from the entry's own sign, or from $i\cdot j$ rather than $i+j$.
- Forgetting the sign entirely and reporting $M_{ij}$ where $C_{ij}$ was asked, or the reverse.
- Expanding along a dense row when a column has two zeros. Same answer, triple the work.
- Transposing twice: $A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det}\operatorname{adj}(A)$, never $\operatorname{adj}(A)^{T}$ — the transpose is already inside the adjugate.
- Multiplying by $\det(A)$ instead of dividing by it.
- Claiming $\det(\operatorname{adj}A)=\det(A)$; for $3\times3$ it is $\det(A)^2$.
- Treating a *negative* determinant as non-invertible. Only zero is fatal; $-5$ is a perfectly good flip.
- Believing determinants add. $\det(A+B)\neq\det(A)+\det(B)$ in general.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For a geometric question, split the determinant into magnitude and sign: magnitude is the area or volume factor, sign is orientation. Distractors pair a negative factor with a preserved orientation.
2. For a minor, physically cross out row $i$ and column $j$ first, then evaluate. Most wrong options are the determinant of a differently-cut block.
3. For a cofactor, compute the minor and then apply $(-1)^{i+j}$ as a separate step. Half the distractors are the unsigned minor.
4. Before expanding, scan every row and every column and expand along whichever has the most zeros — and remember any of them is legal.
5. For adjugate questions, check whether the transpose has been applied and which cofactor lands in the asked position; for $\det(\operatorname{adj}A)$, take determinants of $A\operatorname{adj}(A)=\det(A)I$ rather than guessing.
6. For $2\times2$ inverses, use swap-diagonal, negate-off-diagonal, divide by $ad-bc$, and sanity-check by multiplying back to $I$. If $ad-bc=0$, the answer is "no inverse", whichever road was named.

**Where this leads**: with the inverse in hand you can undo any transformation that does not collapse — but most real systems are rectangular and have no inverse at all, because there are more equations than unknowns.
