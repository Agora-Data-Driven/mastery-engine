1. Pivots Become Volume

Singularity and solvability of systems answered yes/no by counting pivots through RREF, which is the rank. Drag column 2, or jump straight to a case, and watch that same verdict arrive instead as one signed number — the area of the box the two columns enclose. Like a shadow at noon: as two poles line up, the patch of shade shrinks to a bare line.

unit square, area 1 column 1 (fixed) column 2 (you drag)

The determinant does not replace the pivot count — it agrees with it, and carries two extra facts for free: the size of the stretch and the orientation. Determinants also multiply, so from A times A-inverse = I you get det(A) × det(A-inverse) = 1 at once: undoing a 5× area stretch must shrink area by 5, and no number times 0 will ever give 1 — which is the whole of "singular means no inverse", in one line of arithmetic.

2. Crossing Out A Minor

The minor written M(i,j) is the determinant of what is left over after deleting row i and column j. The indices name what you cross out, never what you keep. Tap any entry and watch its row and column go. Like a crossword: strike out one across and one down, and the puzzle you are left with is one square smaller in each direction.

Two habits keep this clean. First, an n × n matrix always yields an (n−1) × (n−1) minor — in a 4 × 4, M(2,4) is a 3 × 3 determinant, not a 2 × 2. Second, in a 2 × 2 the survivor is a single entry, and the determinant of a 1 × 1 is just that number: for [[3, −2], [1, 5]], M(2,2) = 3. Nothing here has a sign attached yet — the checkerboard is the next visual, and it is a separate step.

3. The Checkerboard Signs

A cofactor is a signed minor: C(i,j) = (−1) to the power (i+j), times M(i,j). The sign depends on the position and nothing else — not the entry's value, not its own sign, not the size of the matrix. Tap a square. Like the squares of a chessboard: a1 is dark and h8 is dark no matter how big you draw the board.

Why bother with an alternating sign at all? Because without it the expansion in the next visual would give different answers along different rows. The signs are exactly the correction that makes every row and every column agree on one number — which is also why they are fixed by position and could not possibly depend on the numbers you happen to be holding.

4. Pick Your Expansion Line

Fix any one row or any one column, multiply each entry by its cofactor, add. Every line returns the same determinant — so the only thing your choice changes is how much work you do. Pick a matrix, then pick a line. Like counting a crowd row by row or column by column: same crowd, and you would be foolish not to count along the emptiest one.

The recursion is the whole definition: each cofactor is a smaller determinant, which is itself a sum of still smaller ones, until you reach a 1 × 1. That is also the cost problem — an n × n costs about n! multiplications this way, so cofactor expansion is a tool for 3 × 3 matrices, for matrices stuffed with zeros, and for proofs. Elimination, which you already have, is what a computer uses.

5. Build The Adjugate

Replace every entry by its cofactor, then transpose. The reward is one identity that does all the work later: A times adj(A) equals det(A) times the identity, for every square matrix. Drag the middle entry and watch both sides move together. Like a set of gears cut to mesh: turn one and the product comes out as a clean multiple of the identity, every time.

Why the off-diagonal entries vanish is the elegant part. A diagonal entry of the product is row i of A dotted with its own cofactors — a cofactor expansion, so it gives det(A). An off-diagonal entry is row i dotted with row k's cofactors, which is the cofactor expansion of a matrix whose row k has been replaced by a copy of row i. That matrix has two identical rows, so it encloses zero volume, so its determinant is 0. Nothing is being asserted here that visual 1 and visual 4 have not already earned.

6. Two Roads, One Inverse

The adjugate formula and Gauss-Jordan on the block [A | I] are different journeys to the same matrix — and they break down at the same instant, for the same reason. Slide the bottom-right entry until the determinant reaches 0 and watch both fail together. Like two routes to the same island: different roads, one bridge, and when the bridge is out neither gets you there.

Which road to take is a question of size, not of taste. The adjugate needs n squared minors, each an (n−1) × (n−1) determinant, so its cost explodes factorially; Gauss-Jordan is about n cubed and is what every library actually runs. Use the formula for 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 and for proofs — it is the formula that tells you why det(A) = 0 is fatal — and use elimination for anything larger.
