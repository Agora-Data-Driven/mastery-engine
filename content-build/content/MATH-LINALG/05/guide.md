**The big idea**: **Column space and the span of pivot columns** told you exactly which right-hand sides are reachable — $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has a solution precisely when $\mathbf{b}$ lies in $\text{Col}(A)$ — and **Singularity and solvability of systems** told you what to do when it does not: nothing. This lesson is what a working engineer does instead. When $\mathbf{b}$ sits outside the column space, you stop demanding equality and take the *closest reachable point*: drop a perpendicular from $\mathbf{b}$ onto the subspace and solve for that. Everything here is that single geometric move, written algebraically in four increasingly powerful ways — the shadow formula for one direction, the normal equations for a whole subspace, a projection matrix that does it in one multiply, and the observation that the leftover error lands in **Left null space: relationships among rows**. That last fact is why the four fundamental subspaces from earlier in this course are not bookkeeping: the projection *is* the split of $\mathbb{R}^m$ into $\text{Col}(A)$ and its perpendicular complement.

**Key concepts**

- **Orthogonality and orthonormal bases.** Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero — that is the definition, and it is the only test you need. A basis is *orthonormal* when its vectors are mutually perpendicular **and** each has length 1. Mechanism: stack them as columns of $Q$ and $Q^TQ=I$, because entry $(i,j)$ of that product is $\mathbf{q}_i\cdot\mathbf{q}_j$ — 1 on the diagonal, 0 off it. Why it matters: with an ordinary basis, finding coordinates means solving a system; with an orthonormal one each coordinate is a single dot product. Take $\mathbf{q}_1=(0.6,0.8)$ and $\mathbf{q}_2=(-0.8,0.6)$: the dot product is $-0.48+0.48=0$ and both have length 1, so $\mathbf{v}=(2,1)$ has coordinates $\mathbf{q}_1\cdot\mathbf{v}=2.0$ and $\mathbf{q}_2\cdot\mathbf{v}=-1.0$, and indeed $2\mathbf{q}_1-\mathbf{q}_2=(1.2+0.8,\;1.6-0.6)=(2,1)$. The standard way to *build* such a basis is Gram-Schmidt: from each new vector subtract its projection onto the directions already accepted, which strips out the overlap and keeps only the genuinely new direction, then normalize to length 1. Its whole purpose is turning an independent set into an orthonormal basis — not determinants, not eigenvalues.
- **The shadow formula: projecting one vector onto another.** Derive it rather than memorise it. You want $\mathbf{p}=t\mathbf{b}$ with the error $\mathbf{a}-t\mathbf{b}$ perpendicular to $\mathbf{b}$, so $(\mathbf{a}-t\mathbf{b})\cdot\mathbf{b}=0$, giving $t=\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{b}}$ and $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{a}=\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{b}$. Note which vector appears in the denominator and outside: you divide by the *target's* squared length, never the source's. With $\mathbf{a}=(2,5)$ and $\mathbf{b}=(4,3)$: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=8+15=23$, $\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{b}=25$, so $\mathbf{p}=\frac{23}{25}(4,3)=\left(\frac{92}{25},\frac{69}{25}\right)$. The **scalar projection** is the signed length of that shadow, $\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert}=\frac{23}{5}$ — one power of $\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert$, not two. The sign carries meaning: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}<0$ means the angle is obtuse, so the scalar projection is negative and the shadow points backwards along $\mathbf{b}$. And the projection is the zero vector exactly when $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=0$ — orthogonality and "casts no shadow" are the same statement.
- **The perpendicular component is the other half, and it is the piece least squares cares about.** Once you have $\mathbf{p}$, the residual is $\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{p}$. For $\mathbf{a}=(1,7)$, $\mathbf{b}=(2,-1)$: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=2-7=-5$, $\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{b}=5$, so $t=-1$ and $\mathbf{p}=(-2,1)$. Then $\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{p}=(3,6)$, whose length is $\sqrt{9+36}=\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt{5}$. Check it: $(3,6)\cdot(2,-1)=6-6=0$.
- **Projections of vectors onto subspaces.** Replace the single direction with a subspace $S$ — a line, a plane, or $\text{Col}(A)$ for any $A$. Projecting $\mathbf{b}$ onto $S$ finds the vector in $S$ **closest** to $\mathbf{b}$: shine a flashlight straight down and the projection is the shadow on the floor, not the beam and not the object. The mechanism is the perpendicularity condition, and here is why it is forced: if the residual $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p}$ had any component lying inside $S$, you could add that component to $\mathbf{p}$, stay inside $S$, and get strictly closer to $\mathbf{b}$ — so $\mathbf{p}$ was not the closest point. Minimum distance and perpendicular residual are two descriptions of one configuration. Writing $\mathbf{p}=A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ turns that condition into $A^T(\mathbf{b}-A\hat{\mathbf{x}})=0$, since a vector is orthogonal to a subspace exactly when it is orthogonal to every spanning vector — i.e. to every column of $A$.
- **The Gram matrix and the normal equations.** Expanding $A^T(\mathbf{b}-A\hat{\mathbf{x}})=0$ gives the **normal equations** $A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}}=A^T\mathbf{b}$. The **Gram matrix** $A^TA$ is just the table of pairwise dot products of the columns: entry $(i,j)$ is $\mathbf{a}_i\cdot\mathbf{a}_j$. For $A$ with columns $(1,1,1)$ and $(1,2,3)$, that is $\begin{bmatrix}3&6\\6&14\end{bmatrix}$ — $3=1+1+1$, $6=1+2+3$, $14=1+4+9$. It is always square and symmetric, and it is **invertible if and only if the columns of $A$ are linearly independent** (dependent columns give a nonzero $\mathbf{z}$ with $A\mathbf{z}=0$, hence $A^TA\mathbf{z}=0$). Two special cases pay off immediately: if the columns are orthonormal then $A^TA=I$ and the solution collapses to $\hat{\mathbf{x}}=A^T\mathbf{b}$, one dot product per coordinate; and fitting a single constant $c$ to $\mathbf{b}=(1,2,5)$ uses $A=(1,1,1)^T$, so $3c=8$ and $c=\frac{8}{3}$ — least squares rediscovers the mean. One numerical caution experts insist on: $\kappa(A^TA)=\kappa(A)^2$, so forming the Gram matrix **squares the condition number** and can destroy accuracy on ill-conditioned problems.
- **The projection matrix onto a column space.** Substitute $\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\mathbf{b}$ back into $\mathbf{p}=A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and the whole operation becomes one matrix: $P=A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$, with $\mathbf{p}=P\mathbf{b}$. Two properties define it. It is **symmetric**, $P^T=P$, and **idempotent**, $P^2=P$ — projecting an already-projected vector changes nothing, which is exactly why $P\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}$ and why $P\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}$ whenever $\mathbf{b}$ already lies in $\text{Col}(A)$. Concretely, $A=(1,0)^T$ gives $A^TA=[1]$ and $P=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$, which zeroes the second coordinate. Projecting $\mathbf{b}=(3,1)$ onto the line through $\mathbf{a}=(1,1)$ gives $\mathbf{p}=\frac{4}{2}(1,1)=(2,2)$. And if $A$ is $n\times n$ and invertible, $\text{Col}(A)$ is all of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so $P=A A^{-1}(A^T)^{-1}A^T=I$: nothing to project. Note $P$ is *not* invertible unless it is $I$ — it has rank equal to the dimension of the subspace, and its only eigenvalues are 0 and 1.
- **Applications: Least squares and linear regression foundations.** The problem least squares solves is: choose the $\mathbf{x}$ minimising $\lVert A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert$ when $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has no exact solution — which happens because $\mathbf{b}$ lies outside $\text{Col}(A)$, the usual state of an overdetermined system with more measurements than parameters. Minimising that length *is* projecting, so the answer is the normal equations, and with independent columns the closed form $\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\mathbf{b}$. Linear regression is this and nothing more: put a column of ones and a column of the predictor in $A$, and minimising $\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^2$ is minimising the sum of squared errors you know from statistics. Fit $y=c+dt$ to $(0,2),(1,2),(2,8)$: $A^TA=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}$, $A^T\mathbf{b}=(12,18)$, giving $d=3$, $c=1$. Predictions $(1,4,7)$, residuals $(1,-2,1)$ — no line fits all three points, and this one is provably the closest.
- **The residual lives in the left null space.** Rearrange the normal equations directly: $A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}}=A^T\mathbf{b}\Rightarrow A^T(\mathbf{b}-A\hat{\mathbf{x}})=0\Rightarrow A^T\mathbf{r}=0$. That last line is the definition of membership in $N(A^T)$, the **left null space** — so the residual is orthogonal to every column of $A$, always. Check the regression above: $A^T\mathbf{r}=(1-2+1,\;0-2+2)=(0,0)$. Do not say the null space: for $A$ of shape $m\times n$ with $m>n$, $\mathbf{r}$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^m$ while $N(A)$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so it cannot belong to it on shape grounds alone. With $A=(1,1)^T$ and $\mathbf{b}=(1,3)$, $\hat{x}=2$, $A\hat{x}=(2,2)$ and $\mathbf{r}=(-1,1)$, which satisfies $A^T\mathbf{r}=-1+1=0$. If $\mathbf{b}$ already lies in $\text{Col}(A)$ then $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}$ — and the zero vector is still a member of $N(A^T)$, so the rule never has an exception. Equivalently $\mathbf{r}=(I-P)\mathbf{b}$, and $I-P$ is the projection onto $N(A^T)$: every $\mathbf{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ splits into a piece in $\text{Col}(A)$ and a piece in the left null space.

**Rules to remember**

- Orthogonal means dot product zero. Orthonormal adds unit length, and then $Q^TQ=I$.
- $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{a}=\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{b}$; the scalar projection divides by $\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert$ once, not twice.
- The projection is the closest point; equivalently, the residual is perpendicular to the subspace. Same fact.
- Normal equations: $A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}}=A^T\mathbf{b}$. Closed form $\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\mathbf{b}$ needs independent columns.
- $A^TA$ is invertible exactly when the columns of $A$ are independent — a statement about columns, never rows.
- $P=A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ is symmetric and idempotent; $P\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}$ for $\mathbf{b}$ already in the column space, and $P=I$ when $A$ is square and invertible.
- $A^T\mathbf{r}=0$: the residual is in $N(A^T)$, in $\mathbb{R}^m$, never in $N(A)$.
- $\kappa(A^TA)=\kappa(A)^2$ — the price of forming the Gram matrix explicitly.

**Common pitfalls**

- Writing $\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{a}$ and calling it a projection onto $\mathbf{b}$. Every symbol in the formula must point at the target direction.
- Confusing scalar and vector projection. One is a signed number, the other a vector along $\mathbf{b}$; the denominators differ by one power of $\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert$.
- Treating a negative dot product as an error. It just means the shadow points opposite to $\mathbf{b}$, and the scalar projection is negative.
- Reaching for $A^{-1}$ in a least squares problem. $A$ is $m\times n$ with $m>n$ and has no inverse; that is the entire reason the method exists.
- Claiming $A^TA$ is invertible because $A$ has independent *rows*. With $m>n$ the rows are usually dependent and it changes nothing.
- Saying the residual is in the null space of $A$. Wrong subspace, and usually the wrong ambient dimension.
- Expecting $\mathbf{r}\ne\mathbf{0}$ always. When $\mathbf{b}$ is already reachable the residual is zero, and zero belongs to every subspace.
- Assuming $P$ is invertible because it is a matrix that "does something". It is idempotent, so it is singular unless it is the identity.

**How to approach the questions**

1. On any formula question, rebuild it from perpendicularity in one line: set the error's dot product with the target to zero and solve. That kills every swapped-denominator distractor.
2. For numeric projections, compute $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{b}$ first, write the ratio, and only then multiply. Most wrong options are the right ratio applied to the wrong vector.
3. For a least squares solve, build $A^TA$ and $A^T\mathbf{b}$ explicitly, then verify by checking $A^T\mathbf{r}=0$ — a candidate answer that fails that check is not the projection.
4. When an option mentions the null space, check dimensions before anything else: $\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^m$, $\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^n$.
5. For property questions about $P$, ask whether projecting twice should change anything. Idempotence answers most of them; symmetry is the other half.
6. If a scenario says "no exact solution exists", translate it immediately to "$\mathbf{b}$ is outside $\text{Col}(A)$" and continue from there.
