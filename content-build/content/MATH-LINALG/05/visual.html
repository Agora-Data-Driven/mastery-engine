1. When Ax Misses b

Column space and the span of pivot columns already told you when Ax = b is solvable: only when b lies inside Col(A). Move b off that line and elimination reports no solution and stops. This lesson starts exactly there — stop demanding equality, take the nearest reachable point. Like a train line: you cannot leave the rails, so you get off at the stop closest to where you actually wanted to be.

Notice what did not change when b moved: A, its column space, and the rule for solvability. Only the question changed. Every formula in this lesson — the shadow formula, the normal equations, the projection matrix — is a different algebraic dress on this one picture, so if you can rebuild this picture you can rebuild all of them.

2. The Shadow Formula

One direction, one formula: proj_b(a) = (a·b)/(b·b) · b. Move a and watch the shadow it casts on b — its length, and its sign. Like a sundial: the shadow shortens as the sun climbs, vanishes when the sun is straight overhead, and falls the other way in the afternoon.

This one formula is the seed of the whole lesson. Subtract the shadow and what is left, a − proj_b(a), is perpendicular to b — which is exactly the move Gram-Schmidt repeats to build an orthonormal basis: strip out the overlap with the directions you already have, keep only the new direction, then scale it to length 1. Mutually perpendicular, each of length one: that is the whole definition.

3. Closest Point Wins

Projecting onto a subspace finds the vector in the subspace closest to the target. Slide the candidate along the line and watch the distance curve dip — the minimum lands exactly where the leftover is perpendicular, and nowhere else. Like walking along a riverbank to get nearest a moored boat: you are closest at the spot where you are looking straight out at it.

Shine a flashlight straight down on a floor: the projection is the shadow on the floor — not the beam, and not the object. The perpendicularity is forced, not chosen: if the leftover r = b − p had any component lying inside the subspace, adding that component to p would keep you inside the subspace and move you strictly closer to b, so p was not the closest point. Minimum distance and perpendicular residual are one configuration described twice.

4. The Gram Matrix

A-transpose-A is nothing but the table of pairwise dot products of the columns of A, and the normal equations A^T A x = A^T b are the perpendicularity condition written out. Close the angle between the columns and watch the table go singular. Like two faders on a mixing desk wired to almost the same channel: the closer they get, the less anyone can tell which one you moved.

Worked by hand, with the columns (1,1,1) and (1,2,3): the Gram matrix is [[3, 6], [6, 14]] — 1+1+1, then 1+2+3, then 1+4+9. Every entry is a dot product, which is why A^T A is always square and symmetric whatever shape A had. And the reason numerical analysts avoid forming it explicitly is on the pills above: the condition number is squared, so a merely awkward A becomes a genuinely dangerous A^T A.

5. The Projection Matrix

Substitute the least squares solution back into p = Ax and the whole operation becomes one matrix: P = A (A^T A) inverse A^T. Pick a column space, move b, and watch P b land in it — then watch P do nothing at all the second time. Like a rubber stamp: pressing it twice leaves exactly the same mark as pressing it once.

Two properties define a projection matrix and both are worth reading off the numbers above: it is symmetric and it is idempotent, P squared = P. Idempotence is why P p = p and why P b = b for any b already in the column space; it also means P is singular unless it equals the identity, so "P is a matrix, therefore invertible" is exactly backwards. Its eigenvalues are only 0 and 1 — keep that, it returns in lesson 09.

6. Least Squares Residuals

Fitting a line to three points is exactly this lesson: A holds a column of ones and a column of t values, b holds the measurements, and no exact solution exists. Move the measurements and watch the fit chase them — while A^T r stays pinned at zero. Like a see-saw that must balance twice over: once about the middle, and once about every distance from it.

The residual is in the left null space N(A^T), never in N(A): r has one entry per measurement, so it lives in R3, while N(A) is a subspace of R2 — different ambient spaces, so the claim fails on shape before it fails on anything else. And when the points do line up, r is the zero vector, which is still a member of N(A^T): every subspace contains it, so the rule has no exceptions to memorise.
