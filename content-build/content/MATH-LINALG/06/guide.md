**The big idea**: **Basis and dimension of a space** established that coordinates are never intrinsic — they are a recipe written against a chosen basis — and **Vector spaces and subspaces** established that the space itself does not care which recipe you use. **The shadow formula: projecting one vector onto another** then gave you a way to split any vector into a piece along a direction and a piece orthogonal to it, and **Two roads to the inverse: adjugate formula and Gauss-Jordan** gave you the machinery to undo an invertible matrix. This lesson fuses those into the most reusable move in applied linear algebra: *you get to choose the basis, so choose a better one.* First comes translation — one matrix that relabels coordinates from one basis into another without moving anything. Then comes construction — Gram-Schmidt turns any independent set into a right-angled one by repeatedly subtracting shadows, normalization rescales the result to unit length, and the arithmetic you did along the way, written down in a table, *is* the QR decomposition.

**Key concepts**

- **Changing the basis of a vector space relabels coordinates; the vector itself never moves.** A vector is a geometric object; its coordinate list is only the recipe $v = c_1 b_1 + \dots + c_n b_n$ for one particular basis. Switch basis and you switch map grids — latitude/longitude to street numbers — while the building stays put. Nothing intrinsic changes: not the vector, not the dimension of the space, not any determinant attached to the space. Concretely, with $\mathcal B = \{(1,2),(3,4)\}$ and $v = (1,0)$ in standard coordinates, we need $c_1(1,2) + c_2(3,4) = (1,0)$. Stacking the basis vectors as columns of $B$, that is exactly $B[v]_{\mathcal B} = v$, so $[v]_{\mathcal B} = B^{-1}v = (-2, 1)$. Check it: $-2(1,2) + 1(3,4) = (1,0)$. Same arrow, new label.
- **Constructing the change-of-basis matrix: column $j$ is $[b_j]_{\mathcal C}$.** The map $P_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}$ takes $\mathcal B$-coordinates in and hands $\mathcal C$-coordinates out. Because the map is linear, it is completely determined by where it sends the $\mathcal B$-coordinate vectors $e_1,\dots,e_n$ — and $e_j$ in $\mathcal B$-coordinates *is* $b_j$, whose $\mathcal C$-coordinates are $[b_j]_{\mathcal C}$. So you place $[b_j]_{\mathcal C}$ as **column** $j$, never as row $j$; a transposed build silently computes something else that is still the right shape.
- **The shortcut formula is $P_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B} = C^{-1}B$.** Let $B$ and $C$ hold each basis's vectors as columns *in standard coordinates*. Then $B[v]_{\mathcal B} = v = C[v]_{\mathcal C}$, so $[v]_{\mathcal C} = C^{-1}B\,[v]_{\mathcal B}$. Note the order: $B$ goes to standard, then $C^{-1}$ comes back down into $\mathcal C$. With $\mathcal C = \{(1,1),(1,-1)\}$ and $\mathcal B = \{(3,1),(1,3)\}$: $C^{-1} = \frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}$, so $P = C^{-1}B = \begin{bmatrix}2&2\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}$. Read column 1 back: $b_1 = (3,1) = 2(1,1) + 1(1,-1)$. The column rule and the formula agree, because they are the same statement.
- **Going back is inverting; moving a matrix between bases is conjugating.** $P_{\mathcal B\leftarrow\mathcal C} = (P_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B})^{-1}$ — e.g. $\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ inverts to $\begin{bmatrix}\frac12&-\frac12\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$. For a linear map $T$ the rule is $[T]_{\mathcal C} = P[T]_{\mathcal B}P^{-1}$ with $P = P_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}$, and it reads right to left: take $\mathcal C$-coordinates, $P^{-1}$ drops them into $\mathcal B$, $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ does the work there, $P$ lifts the answer back into $\mathcal C$. Same transformation, different clothes.
- **Gram-Schmidt: subtracting the shadows.** Change of basis lets you move between bases; Gram-Schmidt *builds* a good one. Set $u_1 = v_1$, then for each new vector remove everything it already has in common with what came before: $u_k = v_k - \sum_{j<k}\operatorname{proj}_{u_j}v_k$, where $\operatorname{proj}_{u}v = \frac{v\cdot u}{u\cdot u}u$. The subtraction removes the part of $v_k$ lying **along** the earlier directions — it does not shrink it to unit length, and it does not flip it. With $v_1 = (1,1,0)$ and $v_2 = (3,1,2)$: $v_2\cdot u_1 = 4$, $u_1\cdot u_1 = 2$, so the shadow is $2(1,1,0) = (2,2,0)$ and $u_2 = (1,-1,2)$. Verify orthogonality: $u_1\cdot u_2 = 1 - 1 + 0 = 0$. Simpler case, same machine: $v_1 = (3,0)$, $v_2 = (2,2)$ gives shadow $\frac{6}{9}(3,0) = (2,0)$ and $u_2 = (0,2)$.
- **Gram-Schmidt preserves the span, and reports dependence by producing zero.** Every $u_k$ is $v_k$ minus a combination of earlier vectors, and the relation inverts, so $\operatorname{span}\{u_1,\dots,u_k\} = \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}$ at every step — the process recombines, it never adds or discards directions. Two edge behaviours fall straight out. If $v_2$ is already orthogonal to $u_1$ the projection is the zero vector, so $u_2 = v_2$ unchanged. If $v_2$ lies in the span of $v_1$, the shadow is all of it and $u_2 = \mathbf 0$ — which is **linear dependence** detected arithmetically, the same fact a free column in RREF reports.
- **From orthogonal to orthonormal: normalization.** Orthogonal fixes the angles; normalization fixes the lengths. Divide each vector by **its own** norm: $q = u/\lVert u\rVert$, same direction, length exactly $1$, because $\lVert u/\lVert u\rVert\rVert = \frac{1}{\lVert u\rVert}\lVert u\rVert = 1$. With $u_1 = (3,4)$, $\lVert u_1\rVert = 5$ and $q_1 = (3/5, 4/5)$. Divide by the norm, never by its square: for $v = (1,2,2)$, $\lVert v\rVert^2 = 9$ but $\lVert v\rVert = 3$, so $\hat v = (1/3, 2/3, 2/3)$ — dividing by $9$ leaves a vector of length $1/3$. And each vector needs its own divisor: an orthogonal set with norms $2, 5, 10$ becomes orthonormal as $v_1/2,\,v_2/5,\,v_3/10$; dividing all three by $10$ leaves lengths $0.2, 0.5, 1$. Orthonormality is the pair of conditions $q_i\cdot q_i = 1$ and $q_i\cdot q_j = 0$ for $i\neq j$ — in matrix form $Q^TQ = I$. It matters because in an orthonormal basis coordinates are just dot products, and the Gram matrix from the normal equations collapses to the identity.
- **QR decomposition from Gram-Schmidt: keep the receipts.** Run Gram-Schmidt on the columns of $A$, store the $q$'s as the columns of $Q$, and store every coefficient you used in $R$. That gives $A = QR$ with $r_{jk} = q_j^Ta_k$ off the diagonal and $r_{kk} = \lVert v_k\rVert$ on it, so each column reconstructs as $a_k = r_{1k}q_1 + \dots + r_{kk}q_k$ — the third column is $r_{13}q_1 + r_{23}q_2 + r_{33}q_3$, indices read *down a column of $R$*. Worked in full: $a_1 = (1,2,2)$, $a_2 = (3,0,3)$. Then $r_{11} = 3$ and $q_1 = \frac13(1,2,2)$; $r_{12} = q_1^Ta_2 = (3+0+6)/3 = 3$; $v_2 = a_2 - 3q_1 = (2,-2,1)$, so $r_{22} = 3$ and $q_2 = \frac13(2,-2,1)$. Another: $q_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,0,1)$ with $a_2 = (2,1,3)$ gives $q_1^Ta_2 = 5/\sqrt2$, $v_2 = (-\tfrac12, 1, \tfrac12)$, and $q_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}(-1,2,1)$.
- **Why $R$ is upper triangular, and what pins it down.** $a_k$ is built from only $q_1$ through $q_k$, so any later $q_j$ contributes nothing: $r_{jk} = 0$ whenever $j > k$. Three consequences worth holding. From $Q^TQ = I$ you get $R = Q^TA$ directly. For a tall $m\times n$ matrix of full column rank the *reduced* factorization has $Q^TQ = I_n$ but $QQ^T \neq I_m$ in general — that product is the projection matrix onto the column space, not the identity. And the factorization is unique only once you demand every $r_{kk} > 0$; without that convention you may negate any $q_k$ and the matching column of $R$. Scaling matters too: replacing $a_k$ by $ca_k$ with $c>0$ leaves $Q$ untouched and scales column $k$ of $R$ by $c$, since the direction did not change. If some $v_k$ comes out as $\mathbf 0$, that column lay in the span of its predecessors: $A$ is rank deficient and has no full-rank QR.

**Rules to remember**

- A change of basis changes coordinates only. The vector, the space and its dimension are untouched.
- $P_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B} = C^{-1}B$, and column $j$ of it is $[b_j]_{\mathcal C}$.
- $P_{\mathcal B\leftarrow\mathcal C} = (P_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B})^{-1}$; for maps, $[T]_{\mathcal C} = P[T]_{\mathcal B}P^{-1}$.
- $[v]_{\mathcal B} = B^{-1}v$ when $v$ is given in standard coordinates.
- Gram-Schmidt subtracts shadows: $u_k = v_k - \sum_{j<k}\operatorname{proj}_{u_j}v_k$. Spans are preserved exactly.
- A zero output from a Gram-Schmidt step means linear dependence, not an error in your arithmetic.
- Normalize by dividing by $\lVert u\rVert$, each vector by its own norm.
- Orthonormal means $q_i\cdot q_i = 1$, $q_i\cdot q_j = 0$, i.e. $Q^TQ = I$.
- In $A = QR$: $r_{jk} = q_j^Ta_k$, $r_{kk} = \lVert v_k\rVert$, $R = Q^TA$, and $r_{jk} = 0$ for $j > k$.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Writing $B^{-1}C$ or $BC^{-1}$ instead of $C^{-1}B$.** Track where each factor sends coordinates: $B$ lifts to standard, $C^{-1}$ lowers into $\mathcal C$. Order is not cosmetic.
- **Reading $P_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}$ backwards.** The arrow points at the *output* basis. It consumes $\mathcal B$-coordinates.
- **Building $P$ from rows.** $[b_j]_{\mathcal C}$ is column $j$. A row build is the transpose and produces wrong coordinates that look plausible.
- **Conjugating the wrong way**: $P^{-1}[T]_{\mathcal B}P$ is the map in the other direction. Sanity-check by chasing one vector through.
- **Dividing by $\lVert v\rVert^2$.** The commonest normalization slip: for $(1,2,2)$ the norm is $3$, not $9$.
- **Normalizing a whole set by one shared number.** Only dividing each vector by its own norm makes every length $1$.
- **Expecting the projection subtraction to shorten to unit length or to reverse direction.** It removes a component, nothing else.
- **Treating a zero $v_k$ as a computational failure.** It is information: the columns are dependent.
- **Claiming $QQ^T = I$ for a tall $Q$.** Only $Q^TQ = I_n$ holds in reduced QR.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For any change-of-basis expression, ask "what goes in, what comes out?" and chase a single vector. That resolves $C^{-1}B$ versus $B^{-1}C$ and $P[T]P^{-1}$ versus $P^{-1}[T]P$ faster than re-deriving.
2. When a question hands you $B$ and $C$ as matrices, invert the $2\times2$ by the adjugate shortcut — swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by the determinant — then multiply. Twenty seconds, no elimination.
3. For a Gram-Schmidt computation, compute $v\cdot u$ and $u\cdot u$ first and keep the scalar $\frac{v\cdot u}{u\cdot u}$ visible; most distractors are that fraction misplaced or the shadow left unsubtracted.
4. Always verify with a dot product. If $u_1\cdot u_2 \neq 0$ your answer is wrong regardless of how it looks.
5. Check norms before choosing a normalized option: distractors divide by $\lVert v\rVert^2$, by the sum of entries, or by the wrong vector's norm.
6. For QR entries, remember $r_{jk} = q_j^Ta_k$ — a dot product with the *unit* vector, so no extra division — and $r_{kk} = \lVert v_k\rVert$ after subtraction.
7. When a question changes an input (scale a column, flip a sign), ask which factor absorbs it. Direction changes land in $Q$; magnitude changes land in $R$.

**Where this leads**: choosing a basis for convenience is one thing; letting the *matrix* choose its own is the next. **07 Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, and Diagonalization** finds the basis in which a transformation is merely a set of stretches — vectors it does not rotate at all — via the characteristic equation, eigenspaces computed as null spaces with your RREF toolkit, and diagonalization $A = PDP^{-1}$, which is exactly the conjugation rule from this lesson with $P$ built from eigenvectors.
