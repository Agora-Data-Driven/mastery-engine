1. One Arrow Two Grids

Basis and dimension of a space said a coordinate list is a recipe written against a chosen basis, not the vector itself. Here is that claim you can push on: move the arrow, then swap the grid underneath it and watch the numbers change while the arrow does not. Like giving one building a latitude/longitude and a street address.

Nothing intrinsic moved: not the arrow, not its length, not the dimension of the space. Only the recipe changed. Everything else in this lesson is built on that permission — if the coordinates are yours to choose, you may as well choose the ones that make the problem easy. The matrix that converts one readout directly into another is visual 2.

2. Build The P Matrix

One matrix that takes B-coordinates in and hands C-coordinates out. Pick a pair of bases, then slide a vector's B-coordinates and watch every representation of it move together. Like a currency converter: one rate, applied to any amount.

Two directions, one object: PC←B and PB←C are inverses of each other, so mixing them up is the single most common error here — the arrow in the subscript points at the output. And a map moves by conjugation rather than multiplication: with P = PC←B, a transformation written in B becomes P [T]B P-1 in C — read right to left, it drops into B, works there, and comes back.

3. Subtract The Shadow

Gram-Schmidt has exactly one move: take the next vector, work out how much of it already lies along what you have, and subtract that off. Drag v2 anywhere and watch the leftover stay perpendicular. Like cutting a plank flush to a wall: you remove only the part that overlaps.

The step never rescales to length 1 and never flips a direction — it removes a component, nothing more. And it does not change what you can reach: every u is the v's recombined and the relation runs both ways, so span{u1, u2} = span{v1, v2} at every step. That is why orthogonalising a basis is safe: you get right angles for free and lose no directions.

4. Divide By The Norm

Orthogonality fixed the angles; normalization fixes the lengths. Slide a vector and compare the three things people divide by — only one of them lands on length exactly 1. Like resizing a photo: you divide by the width, not by the area.

A whole orthogonal set at once — norms 2, 5 and 10. Which rule makes it orthonormal?

Orthonormal is two conditions at once: q·q = 1 for each vector and q·q' = 0 for each pair — which is exactly the statement QᵀQ = I. It is worth the effort because in an orthonormal basis a vector's coordinates are just dot products, and the Gram matrix behind the normal equations collapses to the identity, so a projection needs no inverse at all.

5. Where R Comes From

Run Gram-Schmidt on the columns of A and write down every number you used: the unit directions become Q, the coefficients become R. Pick a matrix, then stretch its second column and watch which factor absorbs the change. Like a receipt: Q is what you bought, R is what you paid.

Two facts that catch people out. The factorization is only unique once you insist every diagonal entry of R is positive — otherwise you may negate any column of Q and the matching column of R and get an equally valid answer. And for a tall A with more rows than columns, the reduced Q satisfies QᵀQ = I (columns are orthonormal) but not QQᵀ = I: that product is the projection matrix onto the column space, which is precisely the least-squares machinery from the previous lesson wearing a cheaper, more stable coat.
