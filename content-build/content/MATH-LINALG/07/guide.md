**The big idea**: **Linear transformations and matrix-vector multiplication** taught you to read a matrix as a map, and **Matrix multiplication as a composition of transformations** taught you to read a product as a sequence of maps. In the standard basis almost every map looks like mixing — each output coordinate is a blend of every input coordinate. This lesson finds the directions where the mixing stops. A nonzero $v$ with $Av = \lambda v$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$, and the number $\lambda$ is its **eigenvalue**: along that one direction the matrix does nothing but multiply by a scalar. Everything needed to find them is already yours. **Determinants: Geometric meaning and calculation** together with **Singularity and solvability of systems** produce the eigenvalues; **REF and Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF)** with **Null space: free variables and special solutions** produce the eigenvectors; **The Rank-Nullity Theorem** counts them; and **Changing the basis of a vector space** is what diagonalization turns out to be.

**Key concepts**

- **Intuition behind eigenvectors and eigenvalues: the directions the map will not turn.** Feed $A$ a generic vector and it comes back pointing somewhere else. Feed it an eigenvector and it comes back on the same line, merely rescaled. Concretely: a vector at $(1,0)$ that lands at $(3,0)$ never left the $x$-axis, so it is an eigenvector with $\lambda = 3$; a vector at $(2,1)$ that lands at $(4,-2)$ has swung from the first quadrant into the fourth and is not one. The eigenvalue reports **only the scale factor** — not a rank, not a dimension, not a determinant, not the size of $A$. $|\lambda| > 1$ stretches, $|\lambda| < 1$ shrinks, $\lambda < 0$ flips the arrow onto the other half of the same line, $\lambda = 0$ collapses it to the origin. Why this is worth the trouble: iterating a general map is opaque, but along an eigenvector $A^{k}v = \lambda^{k}v$, and the whole long-run behaviour is one number raised to a power.
- **The characteristic equation and solving for eigenvalues, derived.** $Av = \lambda v$ carries two unknowns at once, which is exactly why the eigenvalues must be found first: until $\lambda$ is a specific number there is no linear system to solve for $v$. Rewrite it as $Av - \lambda v = 0$ and, since $v = Iv$, as $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$. That homogeneous system always admits $v = 0$; a **nonzero** solution exists only when $A - \lambda I$ is singular, and by **Singularity and solvability of systems** that means $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. This is the characteristic equation, and its left side is the characteristic polynomial — for a $3 \times 3$ matrix, the determinant of $A - \lambda I$, not the trace of $A$, not the sum of its entries, not its null space. Worked: for $A = \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$, $\det\begin{bmatrix}2-\lambda&1\\1&2-\lambda\end{bmatrix} = (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)$, so the eigenvalues are $1$ and $3$. They are the **roots**: $(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3) = 0$ gives $2$ and $3$, never $5$ and $6$. Two free checks come with it — the roots sum to the trace ($1+3 = 4$) and multiply to the determinant ($1 \cdot 3 = 3$).
- **Eigenspaces are null spaces: reusing the RREF toolkit.** Once $\lambda$ is fixed, $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$ is an ordinary homogeneous system, so the eigenvectors for $\lambda$ together with the zero vector are exactly $E_\lambda = \mathcal{N}(A - \lambda I)$. Nothing new is required: row reduce $A - \lambda I$ — not $A$, not $A + \lambda I$, not $A^{T} - \lambda I$ — mark pivot and free columns, and write one special solution per free variable. If $\operatorname{rref}(A - 2I) = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&-3\\0&1&2\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}$, the rows say $x_1 = 3x_3$ and $x_2 = -2x_3$, so $x_3 = 1$ gives $E_2 = \operatorname{span}\{(3,-2,1)\}$ — signs flipped from the entries you can see. With two free variables, $\operatorname{rref}(A - 4I) = \begin{bmatrix}1&-1&2\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}$ means $x_1 = x_2 - 2x_3$, giving the basis $\{(1,1,0),\,(-2,0,1)\}$.
- **Count the dimension with rank-nullity, and read "not an eigenvalue" straight off the RREF.** $\dim E_\lambda = n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$, which is **The Rank-Nullity Theorem** applied to $A - \lambda I$ — the number of free columns. For a $4 \times 4$ matrix with $\operatorname{rank}(A - 7I) = 2$, $\dim E_7 = 2$; if the RREF instead has pivots in columns 1, 2 and 4, the rank is 3 and $\dim E_\lambda = 1$. And if $\operatorname{rref}(A - 5I) = I_3$ the rank is full, the null space is $\{0\}$, and the honest conclusion is that $5$ is **not an eigenvalue of $A$** at all.
- **Eigenspaces and basis of eigenvectors: a subspace, so quote a basis rather than "the" eigenvector.** $E_\lambda$ is a null space, hence a genuine subspace. It **contains $0$**, even though $0$ is never called an eigenvector, and it is closed under linear combination, so if $u$ and $v$ both lie in $E_3$ then $2u - 5v$ lies in $E_3$ too. Scaling is the easy half of that: $A(cv) = c(Av) = c(\lambda v) = \lambda(cv)$, which is why every nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is another eigenvector for the same $\lambda$ and eigenvectors always arrive as whole lines. Worked: $A = \begin{bmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$ gives $A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$, so $x_1 = -x_2$ and $E_2 = \operatorname{span}\{(-1,1)\}$ — one independent direction, so its basis is a single vector.
- **Algebraic vs geometric multiplicity, and the exact test for diagonalizability.** The **algebraic** multiplicity of $\lambda$ is its exponent in the characteristic polynomial; the **geometric** multiplicity is $\dim E_\lambda = \operatorname{nullity}(A - \lambda I)$ — the nullity of $A - \lambda I$, never its rank. Always $1 \le \text{geometric} \le \text{algebraic}$, so an eigenvalue with algebraic multiplicity 3 may have geometric multiplicity 1, 2 or 3, and never 0. The test: $A$ is diagonalizable **if and only if the geometric multiplicities sum to $n$**. Worked: $\begin{bmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&3\end{bmatrix}$ has characteristic polynomial $(\lambda-2)^2(\lambda-3)$, so $\lambda = 2$ is algebraically double — but $A - 2I$ has rank 2, hence nullity 1, so geometric multiplicity 1 and a total of $1 + 1 = 2 < 3$: not diagonalizable. Move the offending entry and $\begin{bmatrix}4&1&0\\0&4&0\\0&0&4\end{bmatrix}$ has algebraic multiplicity 3 with $\operatorname{rank}(A - 4I) = 1$, so geometric multiplicity 2 — still short. One deficit is decisive on its own: a $5 \times 5$ matrix with $\dim E_3 = 1$ against algebraic multiplicity 2 fails however healthy its other eigenvalues are, and a $4 \times 4$ of rank 3 whose $\lambda = 0$ is doubled has $\dim E_0 = 4 - 3 = 1 < 2$.
- **$n$ distinct eigenvalues is sufficient, never necessary.** Each eigenvalue contributes geometric multiplicity at least 1, so $n$ distinct ones already sum to $n$ and a $5 \times 5$ matrix with roots $1,2,3,4,5$ must be diagonalizable. But $\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}$ repeats its only eigenvalue and diagonalizes anyway, while $\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$, $\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}5&4\\0&5\end{bmatrix}$ each repeat one and supply only a single independent eigenvector. A repeated eigenvalue is a *warning*, not a verdict: the repeat may fail to yield enough independent eigenvectors.
- **Diagonalization of matrices: collect the basis into $P$ and read off $A = PDP^{-1}$.** Suppose you have $n$ independent eigenvectors $p_1,\dots,p_n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$, and put them in the columns of $P$. Column $i$ of $AP$ is $Ap_i = \lambda_i p_i$, which is precisely column $i$ of $PD$ when $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ — so $AP = PD$, and because the columns are independent $P$ is invertible: $A = PDP^{-1}$. The diagonal entries of $D$ are the eigenvalues **for the chosen eigenvector basis**, in the same order as $P$'s columns; reorder the columns and $D$ reorders with them. Read right to left, $P^{-1}$ rewrites a vector in eigen-coordinates, $D$ scales each coordinate independently, and $P$ translates back — which is the benefit in one sentence: the action is recast as scaling along eigenvector directions. Worked: $A = \begin{bmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$ has $E_3 = \operatorname{span}\{(1,0)\}$ and $E_2 = \operatorname{span}\{(-1,1)\}$, so $P = \begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, $D = \begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$, and $A^{k} = PD^{k}P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}3^{k}&3^{k}-2^{k}\\0&2^{k}\end{bmatrix}$ — one line instead of $k$ matrix products, and at $k=5$ that reads $\begin{bmatrix}243&211\\0&32\end{bmatrix}$. A consequence worth memorising: if a diagonalizable matrix has $\lambda = 5$ as its only eigenvalue then $D = 5I$ and $A = P(5I)P^{-1} = 5I$, so an upper triangular matrix carrying 5s on the diagonal and anything nonzero above them is not diagonalizable.
- **Application: PCA and PageRank algorithm.** Principal Component Analysis eigendecomposes the covariance matrix, because its eigenvectors identify the main directions of variation in the data and each eigenvalue is the variance along its own direction — which is why PCA is the standard tool for reducing the dimensionality of data such as images. Worked with numbers you will meet: a centred 2D dataset of $n = 5$ points with $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}8&2\\2&5\end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 9$, $\lambda_2 = 4$ and eigenvectors $v_1 = (2,1)$, $v_2 = (-1,2)$. The first principal component is $v_1$, the eigenvector of the **largest** eigenvalue, and it captures $\lambda_1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2) = 9/13 \approx 69\%$ of the total variance — note $9 + 4 = 13 = 8 + 5$, the trace, so "total variance" and "sum of eigenvalues" are the same quantity. PageRank runs the other classic: build a column-stochastic transition matrix $P$ whose column $j$ holds the probabilities of leaving state $j$, and the equilibrium or steady-state vector $\pi$ satisfies $P\pi = \pi$ — an eigenvector with eigenvalue exactly 1. It reports the long-run probabilities of being in each state, the equilibrium of the system rather than any transient state, and ranking pages is just sorting those probabilities. Both matter in a machine-learning context because they show eigen ideas driving real ranking and real data reduction.

**Rules to remember**

- $Av = \lambda v$ with $v \neq 0$: $v$ is the eigenvector, $\lambda$ is the factor it is scaled by, and nothing else.
- Eigenvalues first, from $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$; eigenvectors second, from $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$.
- The determinant is set to zero because a nonzero $v$ requires $A - \lambda I$ to be singular.
- The roots of the characteristic polynomial are the eigenvalues — not the eigenvectors, not the inverse.
- $E_\lambda = \mathcal{N}(A - \lambda I)$: row reduce $A - \lambda I$, never $A$, $A + \lambda I$ or $A^{T} - \lambda I$.
- $E_\lambda$ contains $0$ and is closed under linear combinations; the eigenvectors are its nonzero members.
- $\dim E_\lambda = n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$ = the number of free columns. $\operatorname{rref} = I$ means $\lambda$ is not an eigenvalue.
- Geometric = nullity; algebraic = exponent in the characteristic polynomial; $1 \le \text{geometric} \le \text{algebraic}$.
- Diagonalizable $\iff$ the geometric multiplicities sum to $n$ $\iff$ there are $n$ independent eigenvectors forming a basis.
- In $A = PDP^{-1}$, eigenvectors go in $P$'s columns and their eigenvalues down $D$ in the matching order.
- Trace = sum of the eigenvalues, determinant = their product. A free arithmetic check on any candidate.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Reading RREF rows as the answer.** $x_1 - 3x_3 = 0$ yields $(3,-2,1)$, not $(1,0,-3)$; the signs move when you solve.
- **Swapping the two multiplicities.** Geometric is the *nullity* of $A - \lambda I$; the rank and the polynomial exponent are the two favourite wrong answers.
- **Treating a repeat as an automatic failure, or distinctness as the only route in.** $\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}$ repeats and diagonalizes.
- **Summing algebraic multiplicities and declaring victory.** Those always total $n$; only the geometric sum decides anything.
- **Calling $E_\lambda$ empty, or excluding $0$ from it.** It is a subspace and always contains the zero vector.
- **Normalising away the freedom.** $(3,-2,1)$ and $(-3,2,-1)$ span the same eigenspace, so a sign or scale difference from the printed option is not a mistake.
- **Mismatching $P$ and $D$.** The $i$-th diagonal entry must be the eigenvalue belonging to the $i$-th column of $P$.
- **Expecting orthogonal eigenvectors.** Diagonalization promises independence, not orthogonality, and not an identity matrix at the end.
- **Taking the smallest eigenvalue's eigenvector as the first principal component.** Largest eigenvalue, largest variance, first component.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Sort by which half of the pipeline is being asked about. "Find $\lambda$" is a determinant question; "find $v$", "find $E_\lambda$" or "find a basis" is an RREF-and-null-space question. Distractors freely swap the two.
2. Given an RREF, count the free columns first — that number *is* $\dim E_\lambda$ — then write one special solution per free variable and check the signs before matching an option.
3. For any diagonalizability question, build the three-column table: eigenvalue, algebraic, geometric. Sum the geometric column against $n$, and stop at the first eigenvalue where geometric $<$ algebraic, because one deficit settles it.
4. When the matrix is triangular, its eigenvalues are already sitting on the diagonal, so skip the polynomial and go straight to $\operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$.
5. Sanity-check candidate eigenvalues against the trace and the determinant before trusting them.
6. In application questions, name the eigenvalue out loud: PageRank's steady state is the eigenvector for $\lambda = 1$; PCA's first component is the eigenvector for the largest $\lambda$, and its share of the variance is that $\lambda$ over the sum of them all.
7. If two options are numerically identical, prefer the one that states the result plainly over the one that restates the arithmetic.
