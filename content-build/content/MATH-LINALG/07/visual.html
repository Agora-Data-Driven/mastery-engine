1. Arrows That Stay

Linear transformations and matrix-vector multiplication said a matrix is a map that mixes coordinates: every output number is a blend of every input number. Spin the input arrow and watch how far the output swings off its line — then hunt for the directions where the swing is exactly zero. Like a spinning globe: everything moves except the axis it turns on.

The eigenvalue reports only the scale factor — never a rank, a dimension, a determinant or the size of the matrix. That is also why eigenvalues have to be found before eigenvectors: the equation carries two unknowns at once, and until the number is pinned down there is no linear system left to solve. Visual 2 pins it down.

2. Slide Lambda To Zero

A nonzero v can survive (A − lambda·I)v = 0 only where that matrix is singular, so the hunt for eigenvalues is a hunt for the lambdas that kill the determinant. Slide lambda and watch the determinant curve cross the axis. Like tuning a radio: almost every setting is silence, and the stations are the exact spots where something comes through.

Two free checks ride along, and they cost nothing. The roots must sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant of A — so a candidate pair that fails either test is wrong before you ever substitute it back. Note also what the roots are not: they are the eigenvalues, not the eigenvectors, and never the inverse.

3. Free Columns To Basis

Once lambda is a number, finding eigenvectors is the null-space drill you already own: row reduce, mark the free columns, set one free variable to 1, solve. Move the RREF entries and watch the basis vectors flip sign underneath them. Like a recipe with one ingredient left free: fix it at one unit and every other amount follows.

The eigenspace is a null space, so it is a genuine subspace: it contains the zero vector, and any combination of its members — 2u − 5v included — lands back inside it. The zero vector is still never called an eigenvector, which is the distinction the wording of a question usually turns on. And a basis is what you quote, never "the" eigenvector: scaling any of these vectors by a nonzero number gives an equally correct answer.

4. Two Multiplicities Compared

Algebraic multiplicity counts how many times a root repeats in the polynomial; geometric multiplicity counts how many independent directions the eigenspace actually supplies. Drag the geometric numbers and watch a single shortfall decide the verdict. Like seats sold against seats that exist: sell four and only three exist and the flight is short, however healthy the rest of the plane.

The bound runs one way only: geometric is at least 1 and at most algebraic, never more. So an eigenvalue of algebraic multiplicity 3 can have geometric 1, 2 or 3 — and never 0, because a root of the characteristic polynomial always makes the shifted matrix singular. Summing the algebraic column proves nothing: it always totals n.

5. Build P And D

Put the eigenvectors in the columns of P and their eigenvalues down the diagonal of D, and A splits as P D P inverse. Raise the power and compare the one-line formula against grinding out the products; swap the columns and watch D follow. Like changing into the local currency, doing the arithmetic there, and changing back.

Read the product right to left and the benefit is one sentence: P inverse rewrites a vector in eigen-coordinates, D scales each coordinate on its own, and P translates back — the action of A recast as pure scaling along eigenvector directions. What diagonalization promises is independence, not orthogonality: these columns need not meet at right angles, and the result is not the identity. Right angles are next lesson's gift, and only for symmetric matrices.

6. Variance And Steady State

Two famous algorithms are the same idea wearing different clothes. PCA takes the eigenvectors of a covariance matrix as the directions data actually varies in; PageRank takes the eigenvector for lambda = 1 as where a random surfer ends up. Reshape the cloud, or run the walk. Like a crowd in a stadium: which way it spreads, and which exit it eventually all uses.

Both are eigen-questions with the eigenvalue named out loud, and naming it is how you keep them straight. PageRank wants the eigenvector for lambda = 1 — an equilibrium, not a transient state, and ranking is just sorting those long-run probabilities. PCA wants the eigenvector for the largest lambda, because that eigenvalue is the variance along its own direction; take the smallest and you have kept the noise and thrown away the signal.
