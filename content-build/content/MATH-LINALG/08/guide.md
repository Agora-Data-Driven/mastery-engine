**The big idea**: **Diagonalization of matrices** handed you $A = PDP^{-1}$ along with two loose ends — the eigenvalues might be complex, and $P$ might be ill-conditioned or fail to exist at all (**Algebraic vs geometric multiplicity and diagonalizability**). Symmetric matrices close both ends at once, which is why almost every matrix that matters in statistics and machine learning is symmetric by construction: a covariance matrix, the Gram matrix from **The Gram matrix and the normal equations**, a projection matrix, a Hessian. When $A = A^T$ the eigenvalues are always real, eigenvectors from different eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal (**Orthogonality and orthonormal bases**), and the diagonalizing matrix can be chosen orthogonal, so $P^{-1} = P^T$ and the whole apparatus of **Two roads to the inverse: adjugate formula and Gauss-Jordan** collapses into a transpose. The payoff is a dictionary you will use constantly: a symmetric matrix *is* a quadratic function, and the signs of its eigenvalues *are* that function's shape.

**Key concepts**

- **From quadratic functions to symmetric matrices.** Every homogeneous quadratic polynomial is $\mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x}$ for a matrix $A$, because expanding that product gives $\sum_{i,j} a_{ij} x_i x_j$. The mechanism to hold onto: the coefficient of $x_i x_j$ in the polynomial is $a_{ij} + a_{ji}$, so an off-diagonal entry contributes *twice*. Reading the polynomial back into a matrix therefore means **halving every cross coefficient**: $q(x_1,x_2) = 2x_1^2 + 6x_1x_2 + 5x_2^2$ becomes $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$, not a matrix with $6$ in the corners. Forward, with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{x} = (1,2)$: $A\mathbf{x} = (5,8)$, so $\mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x} = 1(5) + 2(8) = 21$. Equivalently $\mathbf{x}^T\!\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 4\end{bmatrix}\!\mathbf{x} = 2x^2 + 2xy + 4y^2$ — the $1$ appears twice.
- **The skew-symmetric part is invisible, so we may always insist on symmetry.** Split any square $B$ as $\tfrac{1}{2}(B + B^T) + \tfrac{1}{2}(B - B^T)$: a symmetric piece plus a skew-symmetric one, $S^T = -S$. Now derive why the second piece vanishes. $\mathbf{x}^T S \mathbf{x}$ is a $1\times1$ scalar, so it equals its own transpose: $\mathbf{x}^T S \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{x}^T S \mathbf{x})^T = \mathbf{x}^T S^T \mathbf{x} = -\mathbf{x}^T S \mathbf{x}$, hence $2\mathbf{x}^T S \mathbf{x} = 0$. So $\mathbf{x}^T B \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^T \big[\tfrac{1}{2}(B + B^T)\big] \mathbf{x}$ for every $\mathbf{x}$. Concretely $B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and the symmetric $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ both give $2x^2 + 4xy + 4y^2$. The converse matters too: if $\mathbf{x}^T B\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^T C\mathbf{x}$ for *all* $\mathbf{x}$, you may conclude only that $B - C$ is skew-symmetric — not that $B = C$. Symmetry is what makes the matrix unique.
- **Classifying definiteness by eigenvalue signs.** Write $\mathbf{x}$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis, $\mathbf{x} = c_1\mathbf{q}_1 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{q}_n$. Then $\mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x} = \lambda_1 c_1^2 + \dots + \lambda_n c_n^2$: a weighted sum of squares whose weights are the eigenvalues. Every coefficient $c_i^2$ is non-negative, so the *signs* of the $\lambda_i$ decide the sign of the whole thing. All $\lambda_i > 0$ is **positive definite** ($q > 0$ for every nonzero $\mathbf{x}$); all $\lambda_i < 0$ is **negative definite**; all $\lambda_i \ge 0$ with at least one zero is **positive semidefinite**; mixed signs is **indefinite**. Eigenvalues $2,5,7 \Rightarrow$ positive definite. $-1,-3,-6 \Rightarrow$ negative definite. $4,0,1 \Rightarrow$ positive semidefinite, not definite, because $q$ vanishes on the eigenvector for $0$. $3,-2,4 \Rightarrow$ indefinite. Multiplicity is irrelevant: a matrix whose only eigenvalue is $6$, repeated three times, is positive definite — every weight is $6 > 0$.
- **Definiteness as shape: bowls, domes, and saddles.** Graph $z = \mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x}$ over the plane and each class becomes a surface through the origin. Positive definite is an upward **bowl** with a strict minimum at the origin; negative definite is a downward **dome** with a strict maximum; indefinite is a **saddle**, rising along one eigenvector and falling along another; semidefinite is a trough, flat along the zero eigenvector's direction. $Q = 2x^2 + 4xy + 5y^2$ has $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 5\end{bmatrix}$, both eigenvalues positive: a bowl. $Q = x^2 - 4xy + 4y^2 = (x-2y)^2$ is positive semidefinite, hitting $0$ along the whole line $x = 2y$ — a trough, not a bowl, so the minimum is not strict. $Q = 2x^2 - 4xy + y^2$ rewrites as $2(x-y)^2 - y^2$, which takes both signs: a saddle. And $Q = 4xy$, with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 2 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ and eigenvalues $\pm 2$, is a saddle even though it has no squared terms at all — read the eigenvalues, never the visible terms.
- **Three cheap tests that skip the eigenvalues.** (1) **Sylvester's criterion**: $A$ is positive definite exactly when *every leading principal minor* is positive; it is negative definite exactly when those minors alternate in sign starting negative ($-,+,-$ for a $3\times3$). Nothing weaker works — $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ has positive diagonal entries, all entries positive, and is still indefinite, since its second minor is $1 - 16 = -15$. (2) For $2\times2$, $\det A = \lambda_1\lambda_2$ and $\operatorname{tr} A = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$. Trace $-7$ with determinant $10$: the positive product forces matching signs, the negative sum makes them both negative — a dome, with no characteristic polynomial solved. (3) **Complete the square**: $x^2 + kxy + 4y^2 = \left(x + \tfrac{k}{2}y\right)^2 + \left(4 - \tfrac{k^2}{4}\right)y^2$, positive definite exactly when $4 - k^2/4 > 0$, i.e. $-4 < k < 4$.
- **Definiteness survives any orthogonal change of variables.** Substituting $\mathbf{x} = P\mathbf{y}$ with $P$ orthogonal turns $\mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x}$ into $\mathbf{y}^T (P^T A P)\mathbf{y}$, and since $P^T = P^{-1}$, the new matrix is *similar* to $A$ — same characteristic polynomial, same eigenvalues, same verdict. Rotating your coordinates (**Changing the basis of a vector space**) moves every entry of the matrix and cannot turn a bowl into a saddle.
- **The Hessian makes this the second derivative test.** For $f(x,y) = x^2 + xy + 2y^2$ the Hessian at the origin is $H = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 4\end{bmatrix}$, whose minors are $2 > 0$ and $8 - 1 = 7 > 0$: positive definite, so the critical point is a strict local minimum. Indefinite Hessian gives a saddle point; a semidefinite one leaves the test inconclusive, because the fourth-order terms decide.
- **The spectral theorem: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors.** For real $A$, symmetry alone ($A = A^T$) guarantees that all eigenvalues are real and that there is an *orthonormal* basis of eigenvectors. Derive the orthogonality — it is two lines. With $A\mathbf{v}_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1$ and $A\mathbf{v}_2 = \lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2$, evaluate $\mathbf{v}_2^T A \mathbf{v}_1$ two ways: grouping right gives $\lambda_1 \mathbf{v}_2^T\mathbf{v}_1$; grouping left gives $(A^T\mathbf{v}_2)^T\mathbf{v}_1 = (A\mathbf{v}_2)^T\mathbf{v}_1 = \lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2^T\mathbf{v}_1$. So $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\,\mathbf{v}_2^T\mathbf{v}_1 = 0$, and distinct eigenvalues force $\mathbf{v}_2^T\mathbf{v}_1 = 0$. A repeated eigenvalue is not a problem here: for symmetric matrices geometric multiplicity always equals algebraic multiplicity, so the eigenspace is full-dimensional and you simply choose an orthonormal basis inside it with **Gram-Schmidt: subtracting the shadows**. Symmetry is doing real work — $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ has perfectly real eigenvalues $1, 2$ but eigenvectors $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$, whose dot product is $1$, not $0$.
- **$A = Q\Lambda Q^T$, and what it buys.** Put the orthonormal eigenvectors in the columns of $Q$; then $Q^TAQ = \Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ and $A = \sum_i \lambda_i \mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^T$ — a weighted sum of the rank-one projections from **The projection matrix onto a column space**. Two details are not free: the columns must be *unit* vectors, or $Q^TQ \ne I$ and the sandwich collapses; and column $j$ must be the eigenvector for the $j$-th diagonal entry, so swapping two columns of $Q$ swaps the corresponding entries of $\Lambda$. Powers become trivial: $A^2 = Q\Lambda^2 Q^T$. With $\mathbf{q}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, $\mathbf{q}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$ and $\lambda = 1, 3$, we get $A^2 = 1\cdot\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix} + 9\cdot\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}5&-4\\-4&5\end{bmatrix}$. Shifts are just as easy: $Q^T(A + 3I)Q = \Lambda + 3I$, so $\operatorname{diag}(1,4,-2)$ becomes $\operatorname{diag}(4,7,1)$. And values come free: with $\lambda = 1, -2$ and $\mathbf{x} = 3\mathbf{q}_1 + 4\mathbf{q}_2$, $\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x} = 1(9) + (-2)(16) = -23$.

**Rules to remember**

- Off-diagonal entries count twice: halve every cross coefficient when building $A$ from a polynomial.
- Only the symmetric part of $B$ is visible to $\mathbf{x}^T B \mathbf{x}$; the unique symmetric representative is $\tfrac12(B + B^T)$.
- $\mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x} = \sum \lambda_i c_i^2$ in the eigenbasis. Signs of $\lambda$, and nothing else, decide definiteness.
- Positive definite = bowl, negative definite = dome, indefinite = saddle, semidefinite = flat direction.
- Sylvester: all leading principal minors positive means positive definite; alternating from negative means negative definite.
- For $2\times2$: $\det = \lambda_1\lambda_2$, $\operatorname{tr} = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$. Two numbers give both signs.
- $A = A^T$ over the reals is *exactly* the hypothesis of the spectral theorem — real spectrum, orthonormal eigenbasis, $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $Q^{-1} = Q^T$.
- $P^TAP$ with $P$ orthogonal has the same eigenvalues, so the same definiteness.

**Common pitfalls**

- Putting the full cross coefficient in both off-diagonal slots, which doubles the cross term.
- Concluding $B = C$ from $\mathbf{x}^TB\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^TC\mathbf{x}$. Their difference is skew-symmetric, and that is all.
- Judging definiteness from the diagonal, from the determinant alone, or from "all entries positive". Each fails on $\begin{bmatrix}1&4\\4&1\end{bmatrix}$.
- Calling a semidefinite form definite. A zero eigenvalue means a whole line where $q = 0$, so the extremum is not strict.
- Assuming a repeated eigenvalue costs you eigenvectors. For symmetric matrices it never does.
- Expecting eigenvectors of *any* matrix to be orthogonal. That is a symmetry privilege, not a general fact.
- Reading shape off visible squared terms: $4xy$ has none and is a saddle.
- Forgetting that a negative definite $3\times3$ has minors $-,+,-$, not three negatives.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Given a polynomial, write the matrix first — diagonal from the squares, half the cross coefficients off-diagonal — then judge.
2. Given eigenvalues, count signs. Any negative kills positive definiteness; any zero downgrades definite to semidefinite; both signs means indefinite.
3. For $2\times2$ classification, reach for trace and determinant before the characteristic polynomial; for $3\times3$, reach for leading principal minors.
4. If a question offers a parameter range, complete the square or force the relevant minor positive and solve the inequality.
5. When a question asks *why* eigenvectors are orthogonal, look for the argument that computes $\mathbf{v}_2^TA\mathbf{v}_1$ two ways. Distractors assert orthogonality from independence or from distinct eigenvalues alone.
6. For anything of the form $Q^Tf(A)Q$, apply $f$ to the diagonal. Shifts add, powers exponentiate, and nothing moves the eigenvectors.

**Where this leads**: the spectral theorem only speaks about symmetric matrices, and most data matrices are not square, let alone symmetric. The next lesson, **09 SVD and PCA: The Capstone**, removes that restriction by manufacturing symmetry — $A^TA$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite for *any* $A$, so everything here applies to it. Its eigenvectors become the right singular vectors $V$, the square roots of its eigenvalues become the singular values, the left null space fills out $U$, and the whole factorisation reads as rotate, stretch, rotate. PCA is then this lesson applied to a covariance matrix.
