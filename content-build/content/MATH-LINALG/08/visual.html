1. Eigenvectors Turn Perpendicular

Diagonalization of matrices gave you A = P D P-1 with no promise that P is pleasant — or even that it exists. Slide the lower-left entry k of A = [1 1 ; k 2] and watch the two eigenvectors swing. Exactly one value of k makes A symmetric, and that is exactly where they meet at a right angle. Like a pair of scissors: only at one setting do the blades cross square.

This is the whole reason symmetric matrices get their own lesson. With an orthonormal eigenbasis the change-of-basis matrix Q satisfies Q-1 = QT, so "diagonalize" costs a transpose instead of an inversion, and the basis is perfectly conditioned. It is also why numerical libraries ship a separate symmetric eigensolver (eigh, not eig): it can assume real output and orthogonal vectors, and it is both faster and steadier for it.

2. Polynomial To Matrix

Every quadratic polynomial is a matrix sandwich xTA x, but the translation has one trap: an off-diagonal entry gets counted twice. Move the coefficients and watch the symmetric matrix, a lopsided rival that produces the identical polynomial, and the skew part that both of them hide. Like splitting a bill two ways: the total is the same however you write each share.

The consequence runs the other way too. If two different matrices B and C satisfy xTB x = xTC x for every x, you may conclude only that B - C is skew-symmetric — never that B = C. Requiring A to be symmetric is exactly what removes that freedom and makes the matrix a well-defined property of the function.

3. Signs Make Shape

Every point of the square is coloured by the sign of q = xTA x. Set the two eigenvalues and the surface follows: all positive is a bowl, all negative a dome, mixed signs a saddle. Then rotate — every entry of A changes and the verdict does not. Like turning a Pringle in your hand: still a saddle, whichever way you hold it.

q > 0 · rises q < 0 · falls q = 0 · flat

The Hessian of a twice-differentiable function is symmetric, so the second derivative test is this panel. For f = x2 + xy + 2y2 the Hessian at the origin is [2 1 ; 1 4]: both eigenvalues positive, so a strict local minimum. An indefinite Hessian is a saddle point; a semidefinite one is inconclusive, because along the flat direction the quadratic term contributes nothing and the cubic and quartic terms decide.

4. Four Tests One Verdict

You rarely need the eigenvalues. Leading principal minors, trace and determinant, completing the square, and the eigenvalues themselves all answer the same question about q = a x2 + k xy + c y2 — and they always agree. Drive the coefficients and watch all four move together. Like four instruments in a cockpit: if one disagrees, you misread it.

Sylvester's criterion generalises upward with a sign pattern, not with "all negative": a symmetric 3x3 is negative definite exactly when its leading principal minors alternate starting negative — minus, plus, minus. The reason is mechanical: negating A flips the sign of the 1x1 minor once, the 2x2 minor twice, the 3x3 minor three times.

5. Rebuild From Eigenvalues

The spectral theorem runs backwards: fix an orthonormal pair q1, q2, choose two eigenvalues, and A = Q L QT is determined. Apply a function to the diagonal and the whole matrix follows — squares, shifts, inverses, all without touching an eigenvector. Like a graphic equaliser: the sliders are the eigenvalues, the bands never move.

This is also where the whole lesson points. Most data matrices are not square, let alone symmetric — but ATA always is, and it is always positive semidefinite, because xTATA x = |Ax|2 >= 0. Everything on this page therefore applies to it, which is precisely how the next lesson builds the SVD out of an eigendecomposition.
