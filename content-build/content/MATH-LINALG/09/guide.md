**The big idea**: **Diagonalization of matrices** gave you $A=PDP^{-1}$, and **Algebraic vs geometric multiplicity and diagonalizability** told you how often that fails — a square matrix short of independent eigenvectors has no such factorization, and a rectangular matrix cannot even be asked the question. **The spectral theorem: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors** rescued exactly one family: a real symmetric matrix always factors as $Q\Lambda Q^{T}$ with $Q$ orthogonal. The capstone move is therefore not to hope your matrix is symmetric but to *manufacture* symmetry. **The Gram matrix and the normal equations** already put $A^{T}A$ in your hands, and it is square, symmetric and positive semidefinite for every real $A$ whatsoever. Eigendecompose it, take square roots of the eigenvalues, and you have the singular value decomposition $A=U\Sigma V^{T}$: every matrix — rectangular, singular, rank-deficient, it makes no difference — is a rotation, then an axis-aligned stretch, then another rotation. PCA is that same factorization pointed at a centered data matrix, and nothing else.

**Key concepts**

- **Why A transpose A: turning any matrix symmetric.** The mechanism is one transpose rule, $(XY)^{T}=Y^{T}X^{T}$, applied to the product itself: $(A^{T}A)^{T}=A^{T}(A^{T})^{T}=A^{T}A$. So the product equals its own transpose for *any* $A$, which is not true of two arbitrary symmetric matrices multiplied together. Shape does the rest: $A$ is $m\times n$, so $A^{T}A$ is $n\times n$ — square even when $m\neq n$. Entry $(i,j)$ is the dot product $\mathbf{a}_i\cdot\mathbf{a}_j$ of two *columns*, so the diagonal holds squared column norms and $\operatorname{tr}(A^{T}A)$ is the sum of the squares of all entries of $A$. For $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}$, $A^{T}A=\begin{bmatrix}10&14\\14&20\end{bmatrix}$: $10=1+9$, $14=2+12$, $20=4+16$, and the trace $30=1+9+4+16$. Positive semidefiniteness is one line: $\mathbf{x}^{T}A^{T}A\mathbf{x}=(A\mathbf{x})^{T}(A\mathbf{x})=\lVert A\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}\ge 0$, so by **Classifying definiteness by eigenvalue signs** every eigenvalue is real and nonnegative — nonnegative, not strictly positive. Four consequences you will use constantly: $A^{T}A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ forces $\lVert A\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}=0$ and hence $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$, so the two matrices share a null space and $A^{T}A$ is invertible exactly when the columns of $A$ are linearly independent; $A^{T}A=I_n$ says the columns of $A$ are orthonormal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{m}$; $A^{T}A=0$ forces every squared column norm to zero, so $A$ itself is the zero matrix; and minimising $\lVert A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert^{2}$ gives $A^{T}A\mathbf{x}=A^{T}\mathbf{b}$, the normal equations from earlier in this course.
- **Finding V and the singular values.** $A^{T}A$ is symmetric, so the spectral theorem hands you an orthonormal eigenbasis; normalize those eigenvectors and stack them as the columns of $V$, ordered by eigenvalue, largest first. The singular values are $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ — square roots, which is safe precisely because the $\lambda_i$ are nonnegative. Take $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$: $A^{T}A=\begin{bmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{bmatrix}$, and the characteristic equation $(5-\lambda)^2-16=0$ gives $\lambda=9,1$, so $\sigma_1=3,\ \sigma_2=1$ and $V=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}$. Two habits matter. Normalize before placing: an eigenvector computed as $(3,4)$ enters $V$ as $(3/5,4/5)$. And the sign is genuinely free — $-\mathbf{v}_2$ is as valid as $\mathbf{v}_2$, because $\mathbf{u}_2$ flips with it and $A\mathbf{v}_2=\sigma_2\mathbf{u}_2$ still holds. Since $\sigma_i^2=\lambda_i$, the **Rank of a matrix** is just the count of nonzero singular values: eigenvalues $25,9,0$ mean $\sigma=5,3,0$ and rank 2. Watch the squaring — $A=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&4\end{bmatrix}$ has $A^{T}A=\operatorname{diag}(9,16)$ and singular values $4$ and $3$, not $16$ and $9$.
- **Building U: the translation formula.** $V$ is fixed; $U$ is then forced, not chosen. Demanding $A\mathbf{v}_i=\sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i$ and dividing gives $\mathbf{u}_i=A\mathbf{v}_i/\sigma_i$ for each $i\le r$, where $r$ is the rank. These are automatically orthonormal, and the proof is worth doing once: $\mathbf{u}_i\cdot\mathbf{u}_j=\frac{(A\mathbf{v}_i)^{T}(A\mathbf{v}_j)}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_i^{T}A^{T}A\mathbf{v}_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}=\frac{\lambda_j\,\mathbf{v}_i\cdot\mathbf{v}_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}$, which is 1 when $i=j$ and 0 otherwise. Continuing the example, $A\mathbf{v}_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(3,3)$ so $\mathbf{u}_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, and $A\mathbf{v}_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1,1)$ so $\mathbf{u}_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1,1)$. A $3\times2$ case: with $\sigma_1=10$ and $A\mathbf{v}_1=(6,8,0)$, $\mathbf{u}_1=(0.6,0.8,0)$; with $\sigma_2=4$ and $A\mathbf{v}_2=(0,0,4)$, $\mathbf{u}_2=(0,0,1)$; their dot product is 0, as the proof promised. The formula dies at $\sigma_i=0$ — you cannot divide — which is exactly why it stops at $r$.
- **Filling U: silent dimensions from the left null space.** $U$ must be $m\times m$ and orthogonal, but the translation formula produced only $r$ columns, so $m-r$ are missing. They come from an orthonormal basis of **Left null space: relationships among rows**, $N(A^{T})$, whose dimension is $m-r$ by the **Rank-Nullity Theorem** applied to $A^{T}$ — the counts always match. Any $\mathbf{y}\in N(A^{T})$ is orthogonal to every column already built, automatically: $\mathbf{y}^{T}A\mathbf{v}_i=(A^{T}\mathbf{y})^{T}\mathbf{v}_i=\mathbf{0}^{T}\mathbf{v}_i=0$. So the added columns need only be orthonormal among themselves and orthogonal to $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, which is what belonging to $N(A^{T})$ means. Count first, always: a $5\times3$ matrix of rank 2 needs $5-2=3$ filler columns; a $4\times6$ matrix of full row rank 4 needs $4-4=0$. For $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$, rank 1 with $\mathbf{u}_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, the left null space is spanned by $(1,-1)$, so $\mathbf{u}_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$ — note the normalization; $\frac12(1,-1)$ has length $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and is not a unit vector. These columns are silent because the matching rows of $\Sigma$ are zero: they contribute nothing to $A$, which is why *any* orthonormal basis of $N(A^{T})$ works and two correct answers can differ.
- **SVD as rotate, stretch, rotate.** Read $A\mathbf{x}=U\Sigma V^{T}\mathbf{x}$ right to left. $V^{T}$ rotates, carrying each $\mathbf{v}_i$ onto a coordinate axis; $\Sigma$ scales along those coordinate axes by the singular values; $U$ rotates the result into place. Because $U$ and $V^{T}$ are orthogonal they preserve every length, so all stretching lives in $\Sigma$: the largest stretch any unit vector receives is $\sigma_1$, and $\mathbf{v}_1$ is the direction that receives it. In the running example $\lVert A\mathbf{x}\rVert\le3$ for unit $\mathbf{x}$, attained at $\mathbf{v}_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ where $A\mathbf{v}_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(3,3)$ has length exactly 3. Geometrically the unit circle maps to an ellipse whose semi-axes are $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ pointing along $\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2$ — the outputs, never the inputs. If $\sigma_2=0$ the ellipse degenerates and the whole plane is flattened onto the single line spanned by $\mathbf{u}_1$, not rotated onto it and not collapsed to the origin.
- **PCA by hand: center, covariance, eigendecompose, project.** Four steps, all of them tools you already own. *Center*: subtract each column's mean, because covariance must measure variation about the mean rather than the location of the mean — skip it and the leading direction chases the mean vector instead of the spread. *Covariance*: $C=\frac{1}{n}X_c^{T}X_c$, a scaled Gram matrix, hence symmetric and positive semidefinite. For rows $(1,1),(2,3),(3,2)$ the means are $(2,2)$, the centered rows are $(-1,-1),(0,1),(1,0)$, and $C=\begin{bmatrix}2/3&1/3\\1/3&2/3\end{bmatrix}$. *Eigendecompose*: eigenvectors are the principal component directions and eigenvalues are the variances along them; here $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ with $\lambda_1=1$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$ with $\lambda_2=\frac13$, so PC1 explains $1/(4/3)=75\%$ of total variance. The same arithmetic on eigenvalues $5,3,2$ gives $\frac{5+3}{10}=0.8$ for the first two components. *Project*: the score is $z=\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{u}$ and the rank-one reconstruction is $\hat{\mathbf{x}}=z\mathbf{u}$ — for $\mathbf{x}=(2,0)$ and $\mathbf{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, $z=\sqrt2$ and $\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(1,1)$. The residual $\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(1,-1)$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$ and lies along the remaining eigenvector, exactly as **The residual lives in the left null space** predicted for projections. And the SVD shortcut: the right singular vectors of $X_c$ *are* the eigenvectors of $C$, with $\lambda_i=\sigma_i^{2}/n$, so PCA never needs $C$ formed explicitly.
- **The toolkit dependency graph: how it all composes.** Draw the course as a directed graph in which an edge from $A$ to $B$ means "$A$ requires $B$ to be present before $A$ can work". SVD requires the eigenvalues of $A^{T}A$; those require **The characteristic equation and solving for eigenvalues**, which requires **Determinants: Geometric meaning and calculation** and its cofactor machinery; the eigenvectors require **Eigenspaces are null spaces: reusing the RREF toolkit**, which requires **Elementary row operations and Gaussian elimination**; the tail of $U$ requires the left null space and the Rank-Nullity Theorem; orthonormality requires **Orthogonality and orthonormal bases** and, underneath everything, dot products and matrix multiplication. A valid learning or build order is a *topological order* — never take a component before everything it points to. A cycle would make that impossible: if $A$ needs $B$, $B$ needs $C$ and $C$ needs $A$, no component can be built first and no ordering exists. Dependencies are transitive, so a shaky idea at the base propagates upward through intermediates you never mention by name, and reversing the edges answers the other question: change one component and everything downstream of it must be rebuilt.

**Rules to remember**

- $(A^{T}A)^{T}=A^{T}A$ for every real $A$; it is $n\times n$, and its $(i,j)$ entry is $\mathbf{a}_i\cdot\mathbf{a}_j$.
- $\mathbf{x}^{T}A^{T}A\mathbf{x}=\lVert A\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}\ge0$: eigenvalues real and *nonnegative*, and $N(A^{T}A)=N(A)$.
- $A^{T}A$ is invertible exactly when the columns of $A$ are independent. $A^{T}A=I$ means orthonormal columns.
- $V$ from normalized eigenvectors of $A^{T}A$; $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, sorted descending; rank = number of nonzero $\sigma_i$.
- $\mathbf{u}_i=A\mathbf{v}_i/\sigma_i$ for $i\le r$ only; the remaining $m-r$ columns of $U$ are an orthonormal basis of $N(A^{T})$.
- Order of operations: rotate by $V^{T}$, scale by $\Sigma$, rotate by $U$. Maximum stretch $\sigma_1$, achieved at $\mathbf{v}_1$.
- Unit circle $\to$ ellipse with semi-axes $\sigma_i$ along $\mathbf{u}_i$ — output directions.
- PCA: center, $C=\frac1n X_c^{T}X_c$, eigendecompose, project. Variance explained is $\lambda_k/\sum\lambda_i$.

**Common pitfalls**

- Applying $(AB)^{T}=A^{T}B^{T}$. The order reverses; the whole symmetry argument depends on it.
- Reporting eigenvalues as singular values. $\sigma=\sqrt\lambda$, so $9$ and $16$ become $3$ and $4$.
- Putting raw eigenvectors into $V$. Every column must be normalized, or $V$ is not orthogonal.
- Treating the sign of a singular vector as an error. $\pm\mathbf{v}_i$ are equally correct; $\mathbf{u}_i$ absorbs the flip.
- Using $\mathbf{u}_i=A\mathbf{v}_i/\sigma_i$ when $\sigma_i=0$. That is division by zero, and the reason the left null space exists in this construction.
- Counting filler columns as $n-r$. It is $m-r$: $U$ is $m\times m$ and lives on the output side.
- Drawing the ellipse's axes along $\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2$. Those are the pre-image directions; the image axes are $\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2$.
- Forming the covariance matrix before centering, or expecting a projection residual to vanish. The residual is zero only when the point already lies on the component.

**How to approach the questions**

1. On any "why is this symmetric / nonnegative" question, write the one-line proof rather than reasoning about entries: $(A^{T}A)^{T}=A^{T}A$, and $\mathbf{x}^{T}A^{T}A\mathbf{x}=\lVert A\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}$. Distractors are usually true-sounding claims about diagonals.
2. For numerical SVD, follow the fixed pipeline — $A^{T}A$, eigenvalues, square roots, normalized eigenvectors, then $A\mathbf{v}_i/\sigma_i$ — and check shapes before arithmetic: $V$ is $n\times n$, $U$ is $m\times m$.
3. For any counting question, get $m$, $n$ and $r$ on paper first. Almost every answer is $m-r$, $n-r$, or the number of nonzero $\sigma_i$.
4. For geometry, ask which side of the factorization the vector lives on. Inputs are $\mathbf{v}$'s, outputs are $\mathbf{u}$'s, and stretching only ever happens in $\Sigma$.
5. One strand of this bank uses *translation* in its ordinary graph sense from your earlier algebra, not as the $\mathbf{u}_i=A\mathbf{v}_i/\sigma_i$ formula: $y=f(x+4)$ shifts a graph 4 units left, $y=(x-3)^2+2$ is 3 right and 2 up, and applying $(x+3,y+1)$ then $(x-5,y-4)$ composes to $(x-2,y-3)$. Read which sense is being asked.
6. Likewise the dependency-graph questions use software-toolkit vocabulary literally: an edge $A\to B$ means $A$ requires $B$; a valid install order is a topological order; a cycle blocks the build because nothing can go first; a change propagates transitively to everything downstream; reverse edges give the minimal rebuild set; and a diamond where two components demand different versions of one shared component is a version conflict.

**Where this leads**: this is the final lesson of the course — the spec lists nothing after it, because there is nothing left to assemble. What it opens is the applied half of your work. Keeping only the $k$ largest singular values gives the best rank-$k$ approximation of $A$, which is the same act as keeping the top $k$ principal components: compression, denoising, and the latent factors behind recommender systems. The pseudoinverse $V\Sigma^{+}U^{T}$ solves least squares even when the columns are dependent and $A^{T}A$ has no inverse at all, and the ratio $\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ is the condition number that decides whether any of it survives finite precision.
