1. Manufacture The Symmetry

The spectral theorem: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors only fires on a symmetric matrix, and this A is 3 x 2 — you cannot even ask it for an eigenvalue. So build one. Drag the second column of A and watch A^T A stay square, stay symmetric and keep every eigenvalue at or above zero, whatever you do to it. Like a photocopier that always prints both sides at once: whatever lands top-right lands bottom-left too.

Column 1 is fixed at (1, 3, 0) so you can watch one thing at a time. Note what never moves however hard you drag: the shape stays 2 x 2 = n x n, the two off-diagonal entries stay equal, and no eigenvalue ever goes negative. Those three facts are the entire licence for the rest of this page — every step after this one is just the spectral theorem applied to the matrix you have manufactured here.

2. Eigenvalues Become Sigmas

V is not invented, it is inherited: the normalised eigenvectors of A^T A, in order. The singular values are the square roots of its eigenvalues — the step everyone forgets. Drive the entries of A and watch lambda, sigma, V and the rank move together, then flip the sign of v2 and watch nothing break. Like reading a room's area off the floor plan: to get a wall length back you take a square root.

Ordering is a convention, not a theorem: sigma1 >= sigma2 >= ... simply means you sorted the eigenvalues of A^T A before building V, and every column of U, every diagonal entry of Sigma and every column of V must be permuted together. Break the pairing and A = U Sigma V^T stops holding — which is why "sort, then build" is one step and not two.

3. Building U Columns

u_i = A v_i / sigma_i builds exactly r columns and then stops dead at the first zero sigma — but U has to be m x m. Set the shape and the rank and watch the shortfall appear, then pick the right filler vector for a concrete rank-1 case. Like a choir where only the front row sings: the back row still has to stand in line.

Concrete case: A = [1 1 ; 1 1], rank 1, u1 = (1, 1)/sqrt2. Which vector may fill the second column of U?

This is why two people can hand in different, equally correct U matrices for the same rank-deficient A. The first r columns are forced by the formula, but the last m - r are any orthonormal basis of N(A^T) you like — and it does not matter, because the rows of Sigma that would multiply them are zero. They contribute nothing to A and everything to U being square and orthogonal.

4. Rotate Stretch Rotate

A x = U Sigma V^T x, read right to left: V^T turns the input so the special directions land on the axes, Sigma stretches along those axes, U turns the result into place. Step through the three actions and watch the unit circle become an ellipse. Like a potter's wheel: turn the clay, press it out, turn it again.

Because U and V^T are orthogonal they change no length at all, so every bit of the stretching lives in Sigma. That single sentence answers a whole family of questions: the largest stretch any unit vector can receive is sigma1 and v1 is the direction that receives it; the smallest is sigma2; and the area scale factor is the product. Nothing about the entries of A is needed to say so.

5. PCA By Hand

Four steps, every one of them a tool you already own. Walk them on three points you can check by hand, then shear the cloud and watch the covariance matrix, the principal direction and the variance explained all move together. Like finding the long axis of a swarm of bees: first the middle, then the direction it stretches.

Two shortcuts worth carrying. The covariance matrix is a scaled Gram matrix, C = (1/n) Xc^T Xc, so everything from visual 1 applies to it unchanged: symmetric, positive semidefinite, real nonnegative eigenvalues. And you never have to form it — the right singular vectors of the centered Xc are its eigenvectors, with lambda_i = sigma_i^2 / n. PCA is the SVD of centered data, wearing a statistician's hat.

6. The Dependency Graph

Every idea in this course is a component, and an edge from A to B means "A requires B to be present before A can work". Tap a component to see what it needs and what breaks if you change it, then inject a cycle and watch the resolver give up. Like a recipe: you cannot ice a cake you have not baked.

One shape this graph happily allows is the diamond: two components that both depend on a third. That is fine here, because there is only one version of the Rank-Nullity Theorem — but in a software toolkit, where X wants lib v1 and Y wants lib v2 and one app needs both, the same diamond is a version conflict. Same picture, and the difference is only whether the shared component can be two things at once.
