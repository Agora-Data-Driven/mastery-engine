**The big idea**: **Composing functions and modeling**, **Invertible functions and restricting domains**, and **Verifying inverse functions** were the last lesson's tools, and trigonometry is the first place all three carry weight at once. Everything here starts from one map: wrap a real number $\theta$ around the unit circle from $(1,0)$ and read where you land. Cosine is that map composed with "take the $x$-coordinate", sine with "take the $y$-coordinate", tangent with "take $y/x$" — composition in exactly the sense you just practised. Because the wrap repeats every $2\pi$ it is violently many-to-one, so the **Inverse trigonometric functions** do not exist until the domain is restricted; that is the previous lesson's rule applied verbatim, not a new idea. Three facts then carry the rest: the circle's own equation $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ *is* the Pythagorean identity; rotations compose, and that *is* the **Angle addition identities**; and one dropped perpendicular extends right-triangle ratios to every triangle, which is what the **Laws of sines and cosines** are for. **Sinusoidal equations and models** is the payoff — fitting and solving the wave all of this describes.

**Key concepts**

- **Special trig values and identities on the unit circle: the terminal point *is* $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, in that order.** Stand a standard-position angle at the origin, follow its terminal ray out to the unit circle, and that point's coordinates are the *definition* of cosine and sine, not a consequence of them. Every sign rule follows for free: in quadrant II, $x < 0$ and $y > 0$, so cosine is negative there and sine positive. If a quadrant-II angle has $y = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, then $\sin\theta = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ — the quadrant changes nothing, because sine *is* the $y$-coordinate. What the quadrant fixes is the other coordinate: $\cos\theta = -\frac{1}{2}$, and $\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}$.
- **$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ is the circle's equation wearing different notation.** Substitute $x = \cos\theta$ and $y = \sin\theta$ into $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ and the derivation is finished. That is why it holds for *every* $\theta$ — obtuse, negative, past a full turn — it is a statement about the circle, not about a triangle. Check it at $\theta = \pi/4$: $\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1$.
- **The special values come from two triangles, so there is nothing to memorise sixteen times.** The 45–45–90 triangle has legs $1, 1$ and hypotenuse $\sqrt{2}$; scaling the hypotenuse to 1 gives $\sin(\pi/4) = \cos(\pi/4) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. The 30–60–90 triangle is half an equilateral triangle, sides $1, \sqrt{3}, 2$, so at $\pi/3$ the adjacent leg is the short one: $\cos(\pi/3) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\sin(\pi/3) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, the two swapping at $\pi/6$. Reflect those into the other three quadrants using the sign rules and the entire circle is built from two triangles.
- **Angle addition identities give trig values of a sum from angles you already know — and the derivation is a rotation matrix product.** Recall from linear algebra that rotation by $A$ is $R(A) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos A & -\sin A \\ \sin A & \cos A \end{pmatrix}$, and that rotating by $B$ and then by $A$ is rotating by $A + B$, so $R(A)R(B) = R(A+B)$. Multiply the left-hand side and read the first column: $\cos(A+B) = \cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B$ and $\sin(A+B) = \sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$. The minus sign in the cosine formula is not a convention to drill; it is the $-\sin A$ entry of the matrix.
- **Subtraction is the same identity with $B \to -B$, and the cofunction identity falls out of it.** Cosine is even and sine is odd — read that off the circle, since $-\theta$ mirrors $y$ and leaves $x$ alone — so $\sin(A - B) = \sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B$ and $\cos(A - B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B$. Then $\cos(90^\circ - \theta) = \cos 90^\circ\cos\theta + \sin 90^\circ\sin\theta = 0 + \sin\theta = \sin\theta$, which is the whole "co-" in cosine.
- **Tangent adds through $\tan(A+B) = \frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A\tan B}$, and the denominator is the interesting part.** Divide the sine formula by the cosine formula, then divide top and bottom by $\cos A\cos B$. Worked: $\tan A = 2$ and $\tan B = 3$ give $\frac{2 + 3}{1 - 6} = -1$. A vanishing denominator is not a bug — $\tan A\tan B = 1$ means $A + B = 90^\circ$, where tangent really is undefined. For an exact non-special value, split the angle into ones you know: $105^\circ = 60^\circ + 45^\circ$, so $\tan 105^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3} + 1}{1 - \sqrt{3}} = -(2 + \sqrt{3})$.
- **Inverse trigonometric functions exist only after a domain restriction — the last lesson's rule, applied.** Sine on all of $\mathbb{R}$ fails the horizontal line test infinitely often, so it has no inverse at all. Restrict it to $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$, where it climbs monotonically from $-1$ to $1$, and it is one-to-one onto $[-1, 1]$; that branch is what $\sin^{-1}$ inverts. So for $x \in [-1,1]$, $\sin^{-1}(x)$ returns *the angle whose sine is $x$* — an angle, never a ratio and never $\frac{1}{\sin x}$. The principal ranges: $\sin^{-1}$ into $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$, $\cos^{-1}$ into $[0, \pi]$, $\tan^{-1}$ into $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$. Hence $\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6}$, $\cos^{-1}(0) = \frac{\pi}{2}$, and $\tan^{-1}(1) = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
- **Verifying inverse functions is where the restriction shows its teeth.** $\sin(\sin^{-1}x) = x$ for every $x \in [-1,1]$, but $\sin^{-1}(\sin\theta) = \theta$ *only* when $\theta$ already lies inside the restricted domain. Take $\theta = \frac{5\pi}{6}$: $\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6} \neq \frac{5\pi}{6}$. Nothing is broken — the composition returns $\theta$'s representative on the principal branch, which is all an inverse of a restricted function can promise.
- **Laws of sines and cosines carry those ratios to triangles with no right angle.** The Law of Cosines, $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$, is a dot product identity you already know: with side vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ meeting at angle $C$, $\|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - 2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = ab\cos C$. It is Pythagoras plus a correction that vanishes exactly when $C = 90^\circ$: with $a = 3$, $b = 4$, $C = 90^\circ$, $c^2 = 9 + 16 - 0$, so $c = 5$. The Law of Sines, $\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}$, comes from writing the area three ways as $\frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$. With $A = 30^\circ$, $a = 5$, $B = 60^\circ$: $b = \frac{a\sin B}{\sin A} = \frac{5 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}}{\frac{1}{2}} = 5\sqrt{3}$.
- **Match the law to the shape of the data, and know which shape is ambiguous.** SAS and SSS go to the Law of Cosines — each gives one equation in one unknown immediately. ASA and AAS go to the Law of Sines, because a complete angle–side pair is already in hand. SSA also goes to the Law of Sines and is the **ambiguous case**: two distinct triangles can fit the same data. With $A = 30^\circ$ and $b = 8$, the altitude from the far vertex is $h = b\sin A = 4$; a side $a = 5$ satisfies $4 < 5 < 8$, so an arc of radius 5 cuts the base twice and *both* triangles are valid ($B \approx 53.1^\circ$ and $B \approx 126.9^\circ$, which share a sine). Below $h$ there is no triangle, at exactly $h$ one right triangle, and for $a \ge b$ exactly one.
- **Sinusoidal equations and models: four parameters, each with a geometric name.** In $y = A\sin\big(B(x - C)\big) + D$ the **midline** is $y = D$, the **amplitude** is $|A|$, the period is $\frac{2\pi}{|B|}$, and $C$ is the horizontal shift. Read them from the extremes rather than from the symbols: amplitude $= \frac{\max - \min}{2}$ and midline $= \frac{\max + \min}{2}$, so a model with maximum 9 and minimum 1 has amplitude $\frac{9 - 1}{2} = 4$ and midline $y = 5$. In $y = 3\sin(x) + 2$ the amplitude is 3 and the midline is $y = 2$ — the additive constant, never the multiplier.
- **Solving a sinusoidal equation needs the inverse *and* the circle.** $\sin(x) = \frac{1}{2}$ has two solutions per period because a horizontal line strictly between the midline and the peak crosses each arch twice: one $y$-value is reached at two different angles. The inverse hands you only the principal one, $x = \frac{\pi}{6}$; the circle's mirror symmetry about the vertical axis gives its partner, $x = \pi - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6}$; adding $2\pi k$ then gives all of them. For a full model, first isolate $\sin\big(B(x-C)\big) = \frac{y - D}{A}$ — and if that ratio exceeds 1 in magnitude, the line misses the wave and there is no solution at all.

**Rules to remember**

- The terminal point is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$: cosine first, and it is the horizontal coordinate. Sine is the height.
- $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ is $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. No triangle is needed and no angle is excluded.
- $\sin(A \pm B) = \sin A\cos B \pm \cos A\sin B$; $\cos(A \pm B) = \cos A\cos B \mp \sin A\sin B$. Sine keeps the sign, cosine flips it.
- Trig functions are not linear: $\sin(A + B) \neq \sin A + \sin B$ outside degenerate cases.
- Principal ranges: $\sin^{-1} \to \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$, $\cos^{-1} \to [0, \pi]$, $\tan^{-1} \to \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.
- SAS or SSS $\to$ Law of Cosines. ASA or AAS $\to$ Law of Sines. SSA $\to$ Law of Sines, then check for a second triangle.
- The Law of Cosines at $C = 90^\circ$ *is* Pythagoras; $-2ab\cos C$ is the correction for the angle not being right.
- Amplitude $= \frac{\max - \min}{2}$, midline $= \frac{\max + \min}{2}$, period $= \frac{2\pi}{|B|}$.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Writing the terminal point as $(\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$.** The order is fixed by the definition, and swapping it is the most common distractor on unit-circle questions.
- **Letting the quadrant overwrite a coordinate you were given.** A quadrant-II angle whose $y$ is $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ has $\sin\theta = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$; the quadrant sets the sign of the *other* coordinate.
- **Distributing sine over a sum.** $\sin(60^\circ + 45^\circ) \approx 0.966$, while $\sin 60^\circ + \sin 45^\circ \approx 1.573$ — a value sine can never take.
- **Flipping the sign in $\cos(A + B)$.** It is a minus for a sum and a plus for a difference, the opposite of sine's behaviour.
- **Forgetting the tangent denominator can vanish.** When $\tan A\tan B = 1$, the sum is $90^\circ$ and the value is undefined, not zero.
- **Reading $\sin^{-1}$ as a reciprocal.** It is inverse-function notation; $\frac{1}{\sin x}$ is $\csc x$, a different object entirely.
- **Expecting $\sin^{-1}(\sin\theta) = \theta$ for every $\theta$.** It holds only on the restricted domain, and in code `numpy.arcsin` and `math.asin` return exactly that principal value — recovering a full-turn angle needs `math.atan2(y, x)`.
- **Missing the second triangle in the SSA case.** The Law of Sines returns one angle; its supplement usually also fits, and the question is testing whether you checked.
- **Confusing amplitude with maximum, or midline with amplitude.** In $y = 3\sin(x) + 2$ the maximum is 5, the amplitude 3, the midline 2 — three different numbers.
- **Working in the wrong angle unit.** Every standard library's `sin` takes radians; a model fitted in degrees is silently wrong rather than loudly broken.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Sketch the circle before anything else. Most exact-value questions collapse the moment the terminal point is placed and its coordinates are named.
2. Separate reference angle from quadrant. The reference angle gives the magnitude from the two special triangles; the quadrant gives the sign. Answering both at once is where errors enter.
3. If an angle is not one of the sixteen, decompose it: $105^\circ = 60^\circ + 45^\circ$, $15^\circ = 45^\circ - 30^\circ$. A question naming an odd angle is asking for an addition identity.
4. For inverse questions ask two things — is the input inside $[-1, 1]$, and is the expected answer inside the principal range? Distractors are usually correct angles from the wrong branch.
5. For triangles, classify the given data as SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS or SSA *before* choosing a law. The pattern selects the law and flags the ambiguous case by itself.
6. For sinusoidal questions, compute maximum and minimum first, then amplitude and midline from them. It is faster than parsing the formula and immune to the amplitude/midline swap.
