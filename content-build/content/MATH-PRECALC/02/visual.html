1. Wrap Then Project

Composing functions and modeling wired one machine's output into the next. Invertible functions and restricting domains said an inverse exists only when no output is reused. Both arrive here at once: the wrap lands a point, cosine and sine are "read the x" and "read the y" composed onto it — and the wrap reuses every output forever. Drag the angle. Like a runner on a circular track: the angle is how far they have run, cos and sin are their two shadows on the walls.

Two facts are worth saying out loud. The identity row never moves off 1.000000, because it is the circle's own equation x^2 + y^2 = 1 with the coordinates renamed — no triangle, no restriction on theta. And the same-sine count is why the next two visuals exist: an output reached at several angles cannot be undone until you throw all but one of them away.

2. Adding Two Angles

Rotating by B and then by A is one rotation by A + B — so the identity is not a formula to drill, it is that composition written in coordinates. Move both angles and watch the formula and the direct value stay glued together, while "sin A + sin B" wanders off. Like walking 3 blocks north then 4 east: your displacement is one move, and it is never 7.

The judgment call is which decomposition to reach for. A question naming 105, 75 or 15 degrees is not asking for a calculator — it is telling you the angle splits into two of the sixteen. And the tangent denominator is not a special case to memorise: it vanishes exactly when the two angles sum to a right angle, where tangent has no value to report.

3. One Branch Only

Drag the horizontal line and count where it cuts. Every crossing is an angle with that value, and a function with several cannot be undone — so we keep one arch and discard the rest. That single surviving arch is the whole definition of the inverse. Like a lift that stops at floor 3 on every trip up and down: "where was it?" only has an answer if you agree to mean one trip.

The expert habit is to ask two questions of every inverse problem, not one: is the input inside the domain, and is the expected answer inside the principal range? A distractor is rarely a wrong angle — it is usually a perfectly correct angle from a branch we agreed to throw away, which is exactly why it looks right.

4. Any Triangle Now

Right triangles were never the point — they were the case where the correction term vanishes. Pick the shape of the data you were handed and the law picks itself; then drag the free quantity and watch the triangle answer. Like choosing a spanner by the bolt, not by preference: the shape of what you are given decides the tool.

One number decides the SSA case and it is worth naming: the altitude h = b sin(A). Below it the arc cannot reach the base and no triangle exists; at exactly h it just touches and gives the right triangle; between h and b it cuts twice; once the opposite side is as long as b it can only cut once. Every "the answer was 53.1, why is 126.9 also right" question is this picture.

5. Fit And Solve Waves

Four parameters, four things you can see. Set the wave, then drag the target line and watch the solutions arrive in pairs — because a horizontal line between the midline and the peak cuts every arch twice. Like tide tables: the height you care about happens twice a cycle, once on the way up and once on the way down.

Fit from the extremes, never from the symbols: amplitude is (max − min)/2 and the midline is (max + min)/2, so "maximum 9, minimum 1" gives amplitude 4 and midline 5 without ever seeing a formula. Solving then runs the other way — the inverse supplies one angle, the circle's mirror supplies its partner, and the period supplies the infinitely many copies of both.
