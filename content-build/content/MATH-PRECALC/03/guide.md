**The big idea**: **Special trig values and identities on the unit circle** put a point $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ on a circle of radius 1; **Angle addition identities** told you what two rotations do when you compose them; **Laws of sines and cosines** let you read a triangle from a side and an angle; and **Inverse trigonometric functions**, built on **Invertible functions and restricting domains** and **Verifying inverse functions**, showed that going backwards from a value to an angle only works on a restricted branch. This lesson gives that geometry an arithmetic. A complex number is *a point in the plane you can multiply*, and in polar form the multiplication rule is nothing but the angle addition identity you already derived: multiply the lengths, add the angles. The restricted-branch problem returns intact as the principal argument, and **Composing functions and modeling** with **Sinusoidal equations and models** is why engineers care — a rotating wave collapses into one number. The lesson then cashes the construction in with the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: this plane is finally large enough to hold every root of every polynomial.

**Key concepts**

- **The complex plane (Distance, Midpoint, Conjugates): $a + bi$ *is* the point $(a, b)$** — real part on the horizontal axis, imaginary part on the vertical, in that order. Accept that identification and every formula you know for the ordinary plane transfers unchanged, because adding complex numbers *is* adding coordinates. Distance is the ordinary distance formula wearing modulus notation: the distance between $1 + 2i$ and $4 + 6i$ is $|(4+6i) - (1+2i)| = |3 + 4i| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$. Midpoint is the ordinary average: the midpoint of $2 + 4i$ and $6 - 2i$ is $\frac{2+6}{2} + \frac{4 + (-2)}{2}i = 4 + i$. Without that dictionary you would memorise three new formulas; with it, none.
- **The conjugate $\bar z = a - bi$ is the reflection of $z$ across the real axis, and it is the tool that kills $i$.** Reflecting flips the sign of the imaginary part only, so the conjugate of $3 - 4i$ is $3 + 4i$. Two consequences carry the arithmetic. Adding a number to its conjugate cancels the imaginary parts — $(2 + 3i) + (2 - 3i) = 4$, real, and in general $z + \bar z = 2a$. Multiplying does better: $z\bar z = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 + b^2$, real and non-negative, which is exactly $|z|^2$. That is the whole reason division works in rectangular form: multiply top and bottom by the denominator's conjugate, as in $\frac{1}{3-4i} = \frac{3+4i}{(3-4i)(3+4i)} = \frac{3+4i}{25}$. Without conjugates, division has no procedure at all.
- **Modulus and argument are the polar coordinates of that same point.** The **modulus** $|z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ is the distance from the origin — a length, so never negative and never an angle. $|3 + 4i| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$, and $|-2i| = \sqrt{0 + 4} = 2$, not $-2$. The **argument** is the angle the ray from the origin through $z$ makes with the positive real axis, measured counterclockwise. Modulus is size, argument is direction, and the pair pins $z$ down as precisely as $a$ and $b$ do.
- **The argument is only defined up to $2\pi$, which is why there is a *principal* one.** The angles $\theta$ and $\theta + 2\pi k$ describe the same ray, so "the argument" is really a whole family; the principal argument is the representative chosen in $(-\pi, \pi]$ — the restricted-domain trick that turned $\tan$ into an invertible $\tan^{-1}$, applied verbatim. Read the easy ones off the axes: $1 + 0i$ lies on the positive real axis, so its principal argument is $0$; $i$ points straight up, so its principal argument is $\frac{\pi}{2}$; $-1 + 0i$ points straight left, so its principal argument is $\pi$ — the half-open convention picks $\pi$ rather than $-\pi$. Computing an argument as $\tan^{-1}(b/a)$ is correct only in quadrants I and IV, because $\tan^{-1}$ returns values in $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ and so can never point left: $-1 + i$ gives $\tan^{-1}(-1) = -\frac{\pi}{4}$ but has a true argument of $\frac{3\pi}{4}$. That gap is why every language ships `atan2(b, a)`.
- **Polar form of complex numbers is $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$, abbreviated $r\operatorname{cis}\theta$.** It is a direct rewrite of $a = r\cos\theta$ and $b = r\sin\theta$: substitute both into $a + bi$ and factor out $r$. So the two quantities that determine polar form are the modulus and the argument — not the real and imaginary parts, which are rectangular form, and never "$r + \theta i$", because polar form is a *product*, not a sum. Worked: for $1 + i$, $r = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$ and the equal positive parts put $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$, giving $\sqrt{2}\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$. Check it: $\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = 1$ for both components.
- **Multiplying/Dividing complex numbers in polar form: multiply the moduli, add the arguments — and the proof is the angle addition identities, line for line.** Expand the product and use $i^2 = -1$: $$r_1(\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)\cdot r_2(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta) = r_1 r_2\big[(\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta) + i(\sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta)\big].$$ The two brackets are exactly $\cos(\alpha+\beta)$ and $\sin(\alpha+\beta)$, so the product is $r_1 r_2\operatorname{cis}(\alpha+\beta)$. Concretely, $2\operatorname{cis}\alpha$ times $3\operatorname{cis}\beta$ is $6\operatorname{cis}(\alpha+\beta)$: the moduli give $2 \times 3 = 6$, never $2 + 3 = 5$, and the arguments give $\alpha + \beta$, never $\alpha\beta$.
- **Division runs the same machinery backwards: divide the moduli, subtract the arguments.** For $r_2 \neq 0$, $\frac{r_1\operatorname{cis}\alpha}{r_2\operatorname{cis}\beta} = \frac{r_1}{r_2}\operatorname{cis}(\alpha - \beta)$, proved by multiplying top and bottom by $\operatorname{cis}(-\beta)$ and reusing the product rule. So $\frac{6\operatorname{cis}(\pi/3)}{2\operatorname{cis}(\pi/6)} = 3\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = 3\operatorname{cis}\frac{\pi}{6}$. Repeating multiplication $n$ times gives De Moivre's theorem, $z^n = r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta)$ — the modulus is *raised to* the power while the argument is *multiplied by* it. If $z$ has modulus $5$ and argument $\frac{\pi}{4}$, then $z^2$ has modulus $5^2 = 25$ and argument $2 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}$. The same computation in rectangular form is a binomial expansion.
- **Geometrically, multiplying by $z$ means "rotate by $\arg z$, then scale by $|z|$".** Multiplying by $i$, modulus 1 and argument $\frac{\pi}{2}$, is a quarter turn: $i \cdot 1 = i$ and $i \cdot i = -1$. That is the rotation matrix behind the angle addition identities compressed into one number.
- **The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: every *nonconstant* polynomial has at least one complex root.** The hypothesis is nonconstant and nothing else — not real coefficients, not even degree, not prime degree. Its content is that $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed: enlarging the numbers once, just far enough to solve $x^2 + 1 = 0$, turns out to solve everything. Pair it with the factor theorem and induct — a root $z_1$ gives $p(x) = (x - z_1)q(x)$ with $\deg q = n-1$, and stripping one factor at a time exhausts the polynomial. So a degree-$n$ polynomial has **exactly $n$** complex roots counting multiplicity: exactly $n$, not "at most $n$" and not "$n$ distinct". Worked: $x^3 - 1$ has 3. Factor it as $(x-1)(x^2+x+1)$ to get $1$ and $-\frac{1}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i$, which in polar form are $\operatorname{cis}0$, $\operatorname{cis}\frac{2\pi}{3}$ and $\operatorname{cis}\frac{4\pi}{3}$ — three points evenly spaced around the unit circle, De Moivre run in reverse. By contrast $(x-2)^3$ also has 3 roots counting multiplicity, but only one distinct value.
- **When the coefficients are real, nonreal roots arrive in conjugate pairs.** Conjugation respects sums and products, so $\overline{p(z)} = p(\bar z)$ whenever every coefficient equals its own conjugate — that is, whenever every coefficient is real. Then $p(z) = 0$ forces $p(\bar z) = \bar 0 = 0$. A real-coefficient polynomial with $2 + 3i$ as a root therefore also has $2 - 3i$; not $-2-3i$ and not $3+2i$, because conjugation flips the sign of the imaginary part only. Two corollaries are worth carrying: nonreal roots come two at a time, so every odd-degree real polynomial has a real root; and each pair multiplies back to a real quadratic, $(x - (2+3i))(x - (2-3i)) = x^2 - 4x + 13$, which is why real polynomials factor into real linear and real quadratic pieces.

**Rules to remember**

- $a + bi$ is the point $(a, b)$. Distance, midpoint and reflection are the plane's ordinary formulas, unchanged.
- $\bar z = a - bi$; $z + \bar z = 2a$ and $z\bar z = a^2 + b^2 = |z|^2$, both real.
- $|z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ is a distance: never negative, never an angle.
- The principal argument lives in $(-\pi, \pi]$: $\arg(1) = 0$, $\arg(i) = \frac{\pi}{2}$, $\arg(-1) = \pi$.
- Polar form is $r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$ — a modulus times a bracket, fixed by modulus and argument alone.
- Multiplying: moduli multiply, arguments add. Dividing: moduli divide, arguments subtract.
- De Moivre: $z^n = r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta)$.
- Every nonconstant polynomial has a complex root, and a degree-$n$ one has exactly $n$ counting multiplicity.
- Real coefficients force nonreal roots into conjugate pairs.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Plotting $a + bi$ as $(b, a)$ or $(a, -b)$.** Real part first, on the horizontal axis; $(a,-b)$ is the conjugate, a genuinely different number.
- **Reporting a modulus that is negative or angular.** $|-2i| = 2$, not $-2$ and not $\frac{\pi}{2}$. Those two distractors are the modulus/argument swap in disguise.
- **Losing the modulus when converting to polar form.** $1 + i$ is $\sqrt{2}\operatorname{cis}\frac{\pi}{4}$; writing $1\operatorname{cis}\frac{\pi}{4}$ drops $r$ entirely and $2\operatorname{cis}\frac{\pi}{4}$ forgets the square root.
- **Writing polar form as $r + \theta i$.** A radius plus an angle is not a number here; polar form is a product.
- **Adding moduli or multiplying arguments.** $2\operatorname{cis}\alpha \cdot 3\operatorname{cis}\beta$ is $6\operatorname{cis}(\alpha+\beta)$, never $5\operatorname{cis}(\alpha+\beta)$ and never $6\operatorname{cis}(\alpha\beta)$.
- **Trusting $\tan^{-1}(b/a)$ in every quadrant.** It cannot return a leftward angle, so quadrants II and III come out $\pi$ off — and it is undefined outright when $a = 0$.
- **Reading the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra as a statement about real coefficients.** It covers every nonconstant polynomial, complex coefficients included.
- **Confusing "exactly $n$ roots" with "$n$ distinct roots".** $(x-2)^3$ has one distinct root and three counting multiplicity.
- **Conjugating the wrong sign.** The partner of $2+3i$ is $2-3i$; $-2-3i$ negates both parts and $-2+3i$ negates the wrong one.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Sketch the point before computing anything. Most complex-plane questions collapse the instant $a + bi$ is placed at $(a, b)$ and the axes are labelled.
2. Decide which form the question is in. Rectangular questions want conjugates, distance and midpoint; polar questions want modulus and argument. Converting needlessly is where arithmetic slips enter.
3. For modulus questions compute $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and then sanity-check the answer: a negative option or an option containing $\pi$ cannot be a modulus.
4. For polar form, get $r$ and $\theta$ on two separate lines, then assemble. Distractors are built by keeping one correct and corrupting the other.
5. For products and quotients, do modulus arithmetic and argument arithmetic as two independent lines. Multiply or divide the first; add or subtract the second. Powers are that rule repeated.
6. For Fundamental Theorem of Algebra questions, read the quantifier before the mathematics. "Every nonconstant", "exactly $n$ counting multiplicity" and "real coefficients" are three different claims, and the wrong options are usually correct sentences about a different one.

**Where this leads**: the next lesson, **04 Rational functions**, divides one polynomial by another, and everything here becomes its toolkit. **Advanced rational expression arithmetic** is factoring on both sides of the bar, and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra is the guarantee that both sides *can* be factored completely — conjugate pairs collapsing into the irreducible real quadratics that never touch the real axis. **End behavior and discontinuities (Asymptotes)** then sorts those roots by position: a denominator root with no matching numerator root is a vertical asymptote, a matched pair is a hole. **Graphs of rational functions** assembles the picture from the real roots alone — which is exactly why knowing how many roots left the real line, and in what pairs, is worth establishing first.
