1. Circle Becomes Plane

The unit circle from Special trig values and identities on the unit circle is one slice of a bigger picture. Set the angle, then grow the radius: the terminal point (cos t, sin t) becomes the complex number a + bi, and the two diagrams are literally the same diagram whenever r = 1. Like a clock hand: the direction was the whole story until someone let the hand change length.

Read the two diagrams as one claim: a complex number carries a direction and a size, and trigonometry only ever gave you the direction. The unit circle is exactly the set of complex numbers with modulus 1 — which is why every rule in this lesson that touches angles is a rule you already proved, and every rule that touches lengths is genuinely new.

2. Distance And Conjugate

The complex plane borrows every formula the ordinary plane already had. Move the two numbers and switch between the three things the questions ask for — distance, midpoint, conjugate — and watch each one turn out to be a formula you have used since coordinate geometry. Like changing the labels on a map's axes: the roads do not move.

The conjugate is the one of the three that is not merely borrowed geometry. Reflecting across the real axis is what lets you turn a complex denominator into a real one, because z times its conjugate is a^2 + b^2 — always real, always non-negative, and equal to the square of the modulus. Every division you will ever do in rectangular form runs through that single fact.

3. Modulus And Argument

Two numbers locate a point just as well as its coordinates do: how far out, and which way round. Move a and b and watch the modulus and the principal argument — and watch the naive arctangent formula quietly go a half-turn wrong the moment the point crosses to the left. Like giving directions as "3 km, north-east" instead of "3 east, 3 north".

The principal argument is the same idea as a principal branch of an inverse trig function: infinitely many angles name this ray, so one representative is chosen — here the one in (-pi, pi]. Half-open on purpose: the leftward direction is assigned +pi, never -pi, which is why arg(-1) = pi rather than -pi. A modulus, by contrast, needs no convention at all — it is a length.

4. Multiply By Rotating

In polar form multiplication stops being algebra and becomes motion: the moduli multiply and the arguments add, which is your angle addition identity wearing a different coat. Set two numbers, then switch between product, quotient and square and watch both halves of the arithmetic move. Like gears: one wheel scales the speed, the other turns the whole assembly.

Squaring is where the saving is obvious: De Moivre gives z^n = r^n cis(n t) in one line, while the same job in rectangular form is a binomial expansion. Set r1 = 5.00 and the argument to pi/4 and read the square off directly — modulus 25, argument pi/2. Note which operation happens to which quantity: the modulus is raised to the power, the argument is only multiplied by it.

5. Counting Every Root

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra makes a promise about counting, not about finding. Set the degree and switch between three polynomials of that same degree: the roots move, stack and pair up, but the total counting multiplicity never once differs from the degree. Like a guest list: seats can be shared or scattered, but the head count is fixed.

Read the theorem's quantifiers carefully, because the wrong answers are usually true sentences about a different claim. It applies to every nonconstant polynomial — not only those with real coefficients, not only even or prime degrees. It gives exactly n roots counting multiplicity — not "at most n", and not n distinct ones. And the conjugate-pair rule is a separate, extra hypothesis that needs the coefficients to be real.
