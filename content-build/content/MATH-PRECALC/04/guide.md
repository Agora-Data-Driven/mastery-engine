**The big idea**: **The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra** closed the last lesson by promising that every polynomial splits completely into linear factors, and **Invertible functions and restricting domains** taught you to ask which inputs a formula is forbidden to take. A rational function fuses those two habits into one object: a ratio $f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ of two polynomials, where the factored forms carry all the information and the zeros of $Q$ decide what leaves the domain. Almost every question here — combine these expressions, where is the asymptote, sketch this graph — is answered by the same opening move: **factor the numerator, factor the denominator, then see which factors cancel and which survive.** A surviving denominator factor is a wall the graph can never cross; a cancelled one is a single missing point. **Composing functions and modeling** built new functions by chaining old ones; this lesson builds them by dividing, and division is the operation that punches holes and walls into an otherwise smooth picture.

**Key concepts**

- **Advanced rational expression arithmetic begins by factoring every denominator, because that is the only way to find the *least* common denominator.** The LCD is the product of each distinct factor raised to the highest power in which it appears anywhere. Skip the factoring and you fall back on multiplying the denominators together, which still works but drags in redundant factors you must cancel later — and it hides shared factors entirely. Worked: $\frac{1}{x^2 - 1} + \frac{1}{x + 1}$. Factored, the denominators are $(x-1)(x+1)$ and $(x+1)$, so the LCD is $(x-1)(x+1)$ — *not* $(x^2-1)(x+1)$. Multiply the second term by $\frac{x-1}{x-1}$: $\frac{1 + (x-1)}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \frac{x}{(x-1)(x+1)}$. Check at $x = 3$: $\frac18 + \frac14 = \frac38$, and $\frac{3}{2 \cdot 4} = \frac38$.
- **With like denominators you add the numerators and leave the denominator alone.** This is nothing but the distributive law with $\frac1d$ factored out: $\frac{a}{d} + \frac{b}{d} = (a + b)\cdot\frac1d$. Denominators are never added. Worked: $\frac{x}{x-1} + \frac{1}{x-1} = \frac{x+1}{x-1}$, which at $x = 3$ reads $\frac32 + \frac12 = 2$ and $\frac{4}{2} = 2$. Subtraction behaves identically but you must distribute the minus sign over the *whole* numerator: $\frac{2}{x+1} - \frac{1}{x+1} = \frac{1}{x+1}$ — one denominator, kept as it is, so the answer is neither $0$ nor $\frac1x$.
- **Multiplying multiplies straight across; only division inverts.** $\frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}$, and $\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{d}{c}$. "Invert the second fraction and multiply" is the division rule and has no business in an addition problem — it is the classic distractor. Worked: $\frac{x}{x+2} \div \frac{x-3}{x+2} = \frac{x}{x+2}\cdot\frac{x+2}{x-3} = \frac{x}{x-3}$.
- **Cancel whole factors, never terms — and only after factoring, never before combining.** $\frac{(x-1)(x+3)}{(x-1)}$ loses the $(x-1)$ because it multiplies everything above and below. In $\frac{x+3}{x}$ nothing cancels, because $x$ is a *term* of the numerator, not a factor of it. Cancelling before finding an LCD is also wrong on a sum: there is nothing to cancel until the fractions are over one denominator.
- **Every cancellation leaves a domain restriction behind.** $\frac{x^2-1}{x-1} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x+1$ **for every $x \neq 1$**, and the original was never defined at $x = 1$. Simplifying changes the formula, not the domain — which is exactly why the graph keeps a scar there.
- **End behavior is decided by the leading terms, and nothing else.** As $|x|$ grows, divide top and bottom by the highest power present: $\frac{3x+1}{x-4} = \frac{3 + \frac1x}{1 - \frac4x} \to \frac{3}{1} = 3$, so the horizontal asymptote is $y = 3$. Every non-leading term is multiplied by a power of $\frac1x$ that vanishes. Check at $x = 1000$: $\frac{3001}{996} \approx 3.013$. The three cases: denominator degree wins $\Rightarrow y = 0$ (so $\frac{1}{x-2}$ flattens onto $y = 0$); degrees equal $\Rightarrow y$ = ratio of leading coefficients; numerator exceeds the denominator by one $\Rightarrow$ no horizontal asymptote but a **slant asymptote**, the quotient of the polynomial division.
- **Discontinuities: a vertical asymptote occurs where the denominator is zero *and* the factor does not cancel.** For $f(x) = \frac{1}{x-2}$ the denominator vanishes at $x = 2$ while the numerator stays $1$, so near there a fixed number is divided by something arbitrarily tiny and $|f|$ explodes: $f(2.001) = 1000$, $f(1.999) = -1000$. The asymptote is the *line* $x = 2$ — a vertical line has an $x$-equation, so $y = 2$ and $x = -2$ are wrong on sight.
- **A cancelling factor gives a removable discontinuity — a hole — instead.** $f(x) = \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ has denominator zero at $x = 1$, but the factor cancels, so the graph is the line $y = x + 1$ with the single point $(1, 2)$ removed. Same trigger, opposite verdict, and the difference is decided entirely by cancellation. It is not an $x$-intercept — the curve does not cross $x = 1$, it is absent there — and $y = 1$ is not an asymptote of anything.
- **Graphs of rational functions are read in a fixed order: domain restrictions and asymptotes first, intercepts second.** The asymptotes are the skeleton; plotting points before you have them produces a curve that strolls through a wall. Then: $x$-intercepts occur where **the numerator is zero and the denominator is not** (a zero of both is a hole or an asymptote, never a crossing), and the $y$-intercept is simply $f(0)$, provided $0$ is in the domain. Worked on $f(x) = \frac{x-1}{x+2}$: domain $x \neq -2$; vertical asymptote $x = -2$; equal degrees give horizontal asymptote $y = 1$; numerator zero at $x = 1$ with denominator $3 \neq 0$, so the $x$-intercept is $x = 1$; and $f(0) = \frac{-1}{2} = -\frac12$ is the $y$-intercept.
- **$f(x) = \frac1x$ is the reference picture worth knowing cold.** Its asymptotes are $x = 0$ and $y = 0$; it has **two** branches, one in each of quadrants I and III, and it crosses neither axis — $\frac1x = 0$ has no solution, and $x = 0$ is not in the domain. It is also, pleasingly, its own inverse, which **Verifying inverse functions** lets you confirm in one line: $f(f(x)) = 1 \div \frac1x = x$.
- **A graph may cross a horizontal asymptote; it can never cross a vertical one.** A horizontal asymptote is a claim about the far left and far right only, so $f(x) = \frac{x}{x^2+1}$ passes through $y = 0$ at $x = 0$ and still approaches $y = 0$ at both ends. A vertical asymptote is a gap in the domain — there is no value there to cross with.

**Rules to remember**

- Factor first, always. Every other step in the lesson reads off the factored form.
- Unlike denominators: find the common denominator *before* anything else. Like denominators: add or subtract numerators only.
- Invert-and-multiply is the *division* rule. Never apply it to a sum.
- Denominator zero, factor survives $\Rightarrow$ vertical asymptote. Denominator zero, factor cancels $\Rightarrow$ hole.
- Vertical asymptotes are named $x = a$; horizontal asymptotes are named $y = b$.
- Compare degrees for end behavior: lower on top $\to y = 0$; equal $\to$ ratio of leading coefficients; one higher $\to$ slant asymptote.
- $x$-intercept: numerator zero, denominator nonzero. $y$-intercept: evaluate $f(0)$.
- Simplifying never restores a domain point that was excluded in the original.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Adding the denominators.** $\frac{x}{x-1} + \frac{1}{x-1}$ is $\frac{x+1}{x-1}$, not $\frac{x+1}{2x-2}$ and not $x + 1$.
- **Cancelling before combining.** With a sum on the page there is nothing yet to cancel; the fractions must sit over one denominator first.
- **Cancelling terms instead of factors.** In $\frac{x+3}{x}$ the $x$ values do not cancel, and testing $x = 1$ ($4$ versus a claimed $3$) settles it instantly.
- **Not distributing a subtraction across the whole numerator.** $\frac{a}{d} - \frac{b+c}{d} = \frac{a-b-c}{d}$; the dropped second minus sign is the single commonest arithmetic slip here.
- **Writing the asymptote of $\frac{1}{x-2}$ as $x = -2$.** The denominator is zero where $x - 2 = 0$, and that is $x = 2$; the sign flips relative to the one printed in the factor.
- **Reading the horizontal asymptote off the constants.** For $\frac{3x+1}{x-4}$ it is $y = 3$, from the leading coefficients — not $\frac{1}{-4}$, and not $x = 3$.
- **Calling a hole an intercept, or an asymptote.** At a cancelled factor the curve has a missing point with an ordinary neighbourhood around it; nothing blows up and nothing crosses.
- **Assuming a horizontal asymptote is never touched.** It constrains the ends of the graph, not its middle.
- **Sketching from a table of points.** Without the asymptotes drawn first, the two branches get joined into one impossible curve.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Factor both polynomials before reading the question's options. Most distractors are answers to the unfactored form.
2. For any arithmetic question, ask which operation you are actually performing: addition needs a common denominator, division needs a reciprocal. The distractor list usually contains the rule for the *other* operation.
3. Sanity-check a simplification numerically. Substitute a legal value such as $x = 3$ into the original and into your answer; two different numbers end the argument.
4. For asymptote questions, solve *denominator* $= 0$, then check whether that same factor appears on top. That single check separates the wall from the hole.
5. For end behavior, look only at the leading terms and compare degrees. Constants and middle terms never affect the answer.
6. For graph questions, run the checklist in order — domain, vertical asymptotes, end behavior, then $x$- and $y$-intercepts — and read $f(0)$ directly rather than manipulating the formula.
