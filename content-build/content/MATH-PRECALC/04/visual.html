1. Factor Then Read

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra promised that both polynomials split into linear factors; Invertible functions and restricting domains taught you to throw out illegal inputs. Put the two together and the denominator's zeros are the whole story. Slide the numerator's root onto one of them and watch a wall turn into a single missing point. Like a road with two washed-out bridges: patch one and it becomes a pothole you step over, but the map still says do not drive there.

Notice the one line that never changes: the domain. Both x = 2 and x = -1 are excluded no matter where p sits, because the original formula divides by zero at each of them. Cancelling a factor rewrites the expression, and a rewritten expression cannot hand back a point the first one never had — which is exactly why the graph keeps a visible scar there instead of quietly healing.

2. Common Denominator First

Advanced rational expression arithmetic is one ordered routine, and the tempting shortcuts are all testable. Pick a problem, then drag x: the original and the correct combination move as one number while the shortcut drifts away from both. Like checking a recipe conversion by actually weighing the flour — one number that disagrees ends the argument.

Two of these shortcuts are real rules pointed at the wrong operation. Invert and multiply is how you divide, and it is worthless on a sum. Cancel the common factors is how you simplify a single fraction, and there is nothing to cancel until the terms sit over one denominator. The distractor is rarely nonsense — it is usually the right move from a neighbouring problem.

3. Leading Terms Win

End behavior asks one question: what does f do when x is enormous? Build any ratio you like, then push the test point out to a million and watch the exact value collapse onto the ratio of the leading terms. Like judging two cities by population: once the numbers are in millions, nobody cares who has an extra dozen residents.

Read the degrees, never the constants. For (3x + 1)/(x - 4) the horizontal asymptote is y = 3 because 3 and 1 are the leading coefficients — not y = -1/4 from the constants, and never x = 3, since a horizontal line is named by a y-equation. And a horizontal asymptote is a statement about the two ends of the graph only: the curve is perfectly free to cross it somewhere in the middle.

4. Hole Or Wall

Both discontinuities start the same way — the denominator hits zero — and the values themselves tell you which one you have. Squeeze the test points toward the bad x and read the table: a hole settles down, a wall runs away by a factor of ten each time. Like walking toward a gap in the floor: either you find a single missing tile or the ground drops away.

A hole is invisible in a table of "nice" x-values and invisible on most plotters — the curve looks continuous because only one point is gone. That is why the algebra outranks the picture here: factor first, and a cancelled factor tells you a point is missing before any graph could. A wall announces itself; a hole never does.

5. Sketch In Order

Graphs of rational functions are drawn in a fixed sequence, and the order is the whole technique. Build (ax + b)/(cx + d) with the sliders, then step through: domain and asymptotes go down first, intercepts second, the curve last. Like framing a house before hanging a door — the skeleton decides where everything else is allowed to go.

The two branches are the tell. A rational function with one vertical asymptote is cut into two independent pieces, and the sketch is finished only when both are drawn — joining them across the wall is the classic wrong picture. Note too that the branches never touch the vertical asymptote and are free to cross the horizontal one; only the vertical line is a genuine barrier.
