**The big idea**: **The complex plane (Distance, Midpoint, Conjugates)** handed you the distance between two points and the midpoint between them; **Special trig values and identities on the unit circle** handed you $x^2 + y^2 = 1$; **End behavior and discontinuities (Asymptotes)** and **Graphs of rational functions** taught you to read what a curve does far from the origin straight off its algebra. This lesson points all three at one family of curves. **Introduction to ellipses and hyperbolas** begins with a *distance condition*: pin two points, then demand something of the two distances from a moving point to them. Demand a constant **sum** and you get an ellipse; demand a constant **difference** and you get a hyperbola. **Foci, vertices, and equations of conics** is what that condition becomes once you square, simplify and divide until the right-hand side is 1 — and every relation among $a$, $b$ and $c$ that falls out is a right triangle you can redraw in ten seconds.

**Key concepts**

- **The two definitions differ by one word.** An ellipse is the set of points whose distances to two fixed points — the **foci** — have a constant *sum*; a hyperbola is the set whose distances have a constant *difference*. The mechanism is what each constraint permits. A fixed sum means a point can never get far from both foci at once, so the curve closes and is bounded. A fixed difference is satisfiable arbitrarily far out, because there both distances grow together and their gap stays put — so the curve escapes to infinity in two branches. Check it with numbers: foci $(\pm 4, 0)$, sum $10$. The point $(5,0)$ gives $9 + 1 = 10$; the point $(0,3)$ gives $5 + 5 = 10$. Both lie on $\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$.
- **The standard equation is the unit circle, stretched.** Take $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ and replace $x$ by $x/a$ and $y$ by $y/b$. Every point $(\cos t, \sin t)$ moves to $(a\cos t,\, b\sin t)$, and the equation becomes $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$. That is why a plus sign between squared terms means ellipse, and why the right side must be $1$ before you read the denominators. $x^2 + y^2 = 9$ is the case $a = b = 3$ written unhelpfully; divide by $9$ for $\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$, a circle.
- **Vertices are what you get by setting the other variable to zero.** For $\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$, putting $y = 0$ gives $x^2 = 25$, so the **vertices** are $(\pm 5, 0)$; putting $x = 0$ gives the co-vertices $(0, \pm 3)$. The longer axis is the **major axis**, and the larger denominator says which variable it runs along: in $\frac{x^2}{16} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$ the $16$ sits under $x^2$, so the major axis is oriented **horizontally**, ends $(\pm 4, 0)$. No graphing required.
- **The constant sum is $2a$, and a vertex proves it in one line.** At the vertex $(a, 0)$ the two focal distances are $a + c$ and $a - c$, summing to $2a$ — the full length of the major axis. The string in the gardener's construction is exactly that long, and $a$ earns its name: the semi-major axis.
- **$c^2 = a^2 - b^2$ is a right triangle, not a formula.** At the co-vertex $(0, b)$ symmetry makes the two focal distances equal, and they must still add to $2a$, so each is exactly $a$. That distance is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $b$ (up the minor axis) and $c$ (out to a focus), so $a^2 = b^2 + c^2$ and $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$. Two consequences come free: $a > b$ for every ellipse, and $c < a$, so the foci sit *inside* the curve on the major axis. For $\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$, $c^2 = 25 - 9 = 16$, so $c = 4$ and the foci are $(\pm 4, 0)$.
- **The hyperbola flips one sign and every consequence follows.** In $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$, setting $y = 0$ gives vertices $(\pm a, 0)$ — so $\frac{x^2}{9} - \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$ has vertices $(\pm 3, 0)$ — while $x = 0$ gives $y^2 = -b^2$, no real solution: the curve never crosses the other axis. The constant difference is again $2a$, since at a vertex the distances are $c + a$ and $c - a$. But the foci now lie *beyond* the vertices, so $c > a$, and the right-triangle bookkeeping runs the other way: $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$. For $\frac{x^2}{16} - \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$, $c^2 = 16 + 9 = 25$, so $c = 5$.
- **For a hyperbola, $a$ is the one under the positive term — not the bigger one.** This is the sharpest contrast with the ellipse, where $a$ is by definition the larger. $\frac{x^2}{4} - \frac{y^2}{25} = 1$ is an ordinary hyperbola with $a = 2$ and $b = 5$; $\frac{y^2}{9} - \frac{x^2}{16} = 1$ opens up and down, vertices $(0, \pm 3)$. The variable carrying the plus sign is the direction the branches open, and its denominator is $a^2$.
- **Asymptotes are end behaviour, computed exactly as for a rational function.** Solve $\frac{x^2}{9} - \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$ for $y$: $y = \pm\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{x^2 - 9} = \pm\frac{2}{3}\,x\sqrt{1 - 9/x^2}$. As $|x|$ grows the square root tends to $1$ and the branch closes on $y = \pm\frac{2}{3}x$ without ever reaching it — at $x = 30$ the curve is at $19.90$ against the line's $20.00$. Geometrically: draw the $2a$ by $2b$ central rectangle and extend its diagonals, slopes $\pm b/a$. An ellipse has no asymptotes, because it is bounded.
- **Centre, translation and completing the square.** The centre is the midpoint of the two foci and of the two vertices — the midpoint formula from **The complex plane (Distance, Midpoint, Conjugates)**, unchanged. Sliding it to $(h, k)$ replaces $x$ by $x - h$ and $y$ by $y - k$: $\frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1$. So $9x^2 + 4y^2 - 18x + 16y - 11 = 0$ becomes $9(x-1)^2 + 4(y+2)^2 = 36$, and dividing by $36$ gives $\frac{(x-1)^2}{4} + \frac{(y+2)^2}{9} = 1$ — centre $(1, -2)$, major axis vertical, $a = 3$, $b = 2$, $c = \sqrt{5}$.
- **Eccentricity $e = c/a$ indexes the whole family with one number.** $e = 0$ is a circle, where $c = 0$ and the foci have merged at the centre; $0 < e < 1$ is an ellipse, flatter as $e$ approaches $1$; $e = 1$ is a parabola; $e > 1$ is a hyperbola. So $\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$ has $e = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8$, and $\frac{x^2}{16} - \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$ has $e = \frac{5}{4} = 1.25$.
- **Naming a conic from its equation takes three checks, in order.** Are both variables squared? If only one is, it is a parabola: $y = x^2 + 9$ has a vertex and exactly *one* focus, being defined by a focus and a directrix, and no asymptotes. If both are squared, do their coefficients share a sign? Same sign is the ellipse family, opposite signs a hyperbola. Are those coefficients equal? Equal and same-sign is a circle. Only then divide through so the right side is $1$ and read the denominators.

**Rules to remember**

- Ellipse = constant **sum** of distances to two foci. Hyperbola = constant **difference**. One fixed point at a fixed distance is a circle.
- Standard form has $1$ on the right. Divide through first; denominators read before that mean nothing.
- Plus between squared terms is an ellipse, minus a hyperbola, one squared variable a parabola.
- Ellipse: $a > b$ always, the larger denominator marks the major axis, $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$, $c < a$.
- Hyperbola: $a^2$ sits under the positive term whether or not it is larger, $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, $c > a$.
- For both, the axis through the foci has length $2a$ — also the constant sum or difference.
- Foci lie on the major or transverse axis, never off it, and the centre is their midpoint.
- Hyperbola asymptotes have slopes $\pm b/a$, from the $2a$ by $2b$ central rectangle. Ellipses have none.
- $e = c/a$: $0$ circle, between $0$ and $1$ ellipse, $1$ parabola, above $1$ hyperbola.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Swapping sum and difference.** The sum is the closed curve, because a bounded total keeps the point near both foci; the difference is the pair of branches. Every definition question is built on this exchange.
- **Reading $a$ as "whatever is under $x^2$" for an ellipse.** In $\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{25} = 1$ the major axis is vertical: $a = 5$, vertices $(0, \pm 5)$, foci $(0, \pm 4)$.
- **Using $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$ on an ellipse, or $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$ on a hyperbola.** Anchor it geometrically, not by symbol: ellipse foci are inside, so $c$ must come out smaller than $a$; hyperbola foci are outside the vertices, so larger.
- **Reporting $c^2$ as $c$.** If $c^2 = 16$ then $c = 4$; if $c^2 = 25$ then $c = 5$. The un-rooted value is the standard distractor.
- **Quoting vertices as $(\pm a^2, 0)$.** For $\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$ they are $(\pm 5, 0)$ — not $(\pm 25, 0)$, and not the co-vertices $(0, \pm 3)$.
- **Assuming $a > b$ for a hyperbola.** There is no such rule; only the position of the plus sign matters.
- **Treating a circle as a different kind of object.** It is the $a = b$, $c = 0$ ellipse — but asked which equation represents an ellipse, with a genuine $a \ne b$ form offered alongside $x^2 + y^2 = 9$, take the one with unequal denominators.
- **Expecting an ellipse to have asymptotes, or a hyperbola to be bounded.** The sign between the terms decides that and nothing else.
- **Reading $a$, $b$ and the centre off an equation that still has linear terms.** Complete the square first, or the centre silently defaults to the origin.
- **Thinking a constant *product* of the two distances belongs here.** It gives a Cassini oval, not a conic.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Classify before computing. Signs alone separate the four cases, and many questions ask for nothing more.
2. For an ellipse, take $a$ from the larger denominator and let its position fix the orientation: larger under $x^2$ gives a horizontal major axis and vertices $(\pm a, 0)$; larger under $y^2$ gives vertical, $(0, \pm a)$.
3. For a hyperbola, take $a$ from the positive term and ignore which number is larger. The branches open along that variable's axis.
4. Say the $c$ relation with its geometry attached — "foci inside, so subtract" or "foci outside, so add" — then substitute. That habit survives pressure; two similar formulas do not.
5. Take the square root, then answer. Vertices are $\pm a$ and foci are $\pm c$, never $\pm a^2$ or $\pm c^2$.
6. On definition questions, scan the options for four words: *sum*, *difference*, *product*, *one* fixed point. Sum is the ellipse, difference the hyperbola, one point at fixed distance a circle, product not in the family.
