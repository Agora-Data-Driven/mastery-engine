1. From Circle To Ellipse

You already own the unit circle x^2 + y^2 = 1 and its point (cos t, sin t). Stretch it by a in the x direction and by b in the y direction and you have written every ellipse there is. Drag a, b and the angle, and watch one point travel from the circle to the curve. Like a photograph pulled wider on one side: nothing new is drawn, the same picture is scaled.

already taught — the unit circle
x^2 + y^2 = 1 point (cos t, sin t) distance from centre = 1 always
this lesson — the ellipse
x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 = 1 point (a cos t, b sin t) two distances, to two foci

One warning the stretch hands you for free: the right-hand side must be 1 before any denominator means anything. x^2/9 + y^2/4 = 4 is a perfectly good ellipse, but its semi-axes are 6 and 4, not 3 and 2 — divide through first and read second.

2. Sum Or Difference

Both curves are defined by the two distances from a moving point to two fixed foci. The ellipse pins their sum; the hyperbola pins their difference. Slide the point around and watch which of the two numbers refuses to move. Like a loop of string round two pins versus two ropes whose lengths always differ by the same amount.

Two words are all that separate the definitions, and the wrong ones are always on offer. A constant distance to one fixed point is a circle. A constant product of the two distances is a Cassini oval, which is not a conic at all. Sum closes the curve; difference splits it in two.

3. Foci Vertices Axes

Set the two denominators and read everything else off the picture: which axis is major, where the vertices are, and where the foci land. The right triangle drawn on the curve is the c formula. Like a ladder against a wall: the wall, the ground and the ladder are always one Pythagorean triple away from each other.

The pattern that survives every question: for an ellipse a is the larger of the two, and c comes out smaller than a because the foci are trapped inside a closed curve. For a hyperbola a is whichever sits under the plus sign, larger or not, and c comes out bigger than a because the foci sit beyond the vertices. Get the geometry and the two formulas stop being confusable.

4. Name That Conic

Three questions about the coefficients name the curve before any algebra. Then zoom out and watch the thing that actually separates them: whether the curve stays put, hugs a line, or outruns every line there is. Like identifying an animal from its tracks, then watching where it runs.

The asymptote calculation is the one you already ran on rational functions: divide out the dominant term and see what survives at large x. y = +/-(b/a) sqrt(x^2 - a^2) becomes +/-(b/a) x once the a^2 stops mattering — the same end-behaviour argument, on a curve that is not a function.
