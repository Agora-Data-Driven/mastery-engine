**The big idea**: **The complex plane (Distance, Midpoint, Conjugates)**, **Modulus and argument**, and **Polar form of complex numbers** already trained you to hold a point in the plane as one object carrying two facts — how far, and which way. A vector is that same object with the multiplication taken away. Where $z = a + bi$ had a modulus, an argument, and a product rule that rotated, a vector $\langle a, b \rangle$ keeps the pair, keeps the length, keeps the heading, and asks only to be scaled, added and subtracted. The lesson is three moves. Read one arrow two ways — components on one side, magnitude and direction on the other. Combine arrows — scalar multiplication, addition, subtraction, all of it componentwise. Then notice that velocity, force and displacement genuinely *are* arrows, so **Vector word problems** are the first two moves with units bolted on. The habit worth building now is that every question here is answerable in components, and components are just two ordinary numbers doing ordinary arithmetic side by side.

**Key concepts**

- **Vector components, magnitude, and direction — one arrow, two readings.** A vector in the coordinate plane is represented by an **ordered pair of components**, written $\langle a, b \rangle$: $a$ is how far across, $b$ is how far up, and both are *displacements*, not locations. The alternatives all destroy information. A single real number giving the length says a plane flew 400 km and refuses to say where; a complex number's modulus alone is that same amputation performed on $z$. A pair of endpoints describes one drawn arrow rather than the vector, because $\langle 3, 4 \rangle$ is the same vector drawn from $(0,0)$ to $(3,4)$ or from $(1,1)$ to $(4,5)$. Components are the canonical form: two numbers, in order, fixing both facts at once.
- **Magnitude is the Pythagorean theorem in vector notation.** For $\langle a, b \rangle$ the magnitude is $\|\langle a, b \rangle\| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ — the hypotenuse of the right triangle whose legs are the components. It is not $a + b$, which walks along the legs instead of cutting across, and it is not $a^2 + b^2$, which is the square of the answer with the root dropped. Worked: $\|\langle 3, 4 \rangle\| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$, against $a + b = 7$ and $a^2 + b^2 = 25$ — both wrong answers you will be offered. Magnitude is never negative, and it is zero only for the zero vector $\langle 0, 0 \rangle$. This is literally the modulus formula $|a + bi| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ under a new name.
- **Direction is the angle measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis, and it converts to components by trigonometry you already own.** Magnitude $r$ at direction $\theta$ has components $\langle r\cos\theta,\; r\sin\theta \rangle$ — the polar-to-rectangular conversion from **Polar form of complex numbers**, unchanged. Worked: magnitude 6 at $0^\circ$ gives $\langle 6\cos 0^\circ,\, 6\sin 0^\circ \rangle = \langle 6, 0 \rangle$, pointing straight along the positive $x$-axis. At $90^\circ$ the terminal point on the unit circle is $(0,1)$, so the unit vector there is $\langle 0, 1 \rangle$ — straight up, not $\langle 1, 0 \rangle$ and not $\langle 0, -1 \rangle$, which is $270^\circ$. Going the other way, $\theta = \tan^{-1}(b/a)$, adjusted by the quadrant exactly as **Special trig values and identities on the unit circle** taught: $\langle -1, 1 \rangle$ has $\tan^{-1}(-1) = -45^\circ$ from the calculator, and the true direction is $135^\circ$.
- **Scalar multiplication stretches along a line and never turns.** $k\langle a, b \rangle = \langle ka, kb \rangle$, both components scaled, and $\|k\mathbf{v}\| = |k|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|$. A positive scalar greater than 1 **lengthens the magnitude** and leaves the direction alone; a scalar between 0 and 1 shortens it; a negative scalar reverses the direction, a $180^\circ$ turn, never a $90^\circ$ rotation; and $k = 0$ collapses the arrow to the zero vector, a point. Worked: $3\langle 1, -2 \rangle = \langle 3, -6 \rangle$ — notice that $\langle 3, -2 \rangle$ (only the first component scaled) and $\langle 1, -6 \rangle$ (only the second) are the two ways to get this wrong. The magnitude goes from $\sqrt{5}$ to $3\sqrt{5}$, tripled, and the arrow stays on the same line through the origin.
- **Addition is componentwise, and drawn it is tip-to-tail.** $\langle a_1, b_1 \rangle + \langle a_2, b_2 \rangle = \langle a_1 + a_2,\; b_1 + b_2 \rangle$. Worked: $\langle 2, 3 \rangle + \langle 4, -1 \rangle = \langle 2+4,\; 3+(-1) \rangle = \langle 6, 2 \rangle$. Geometrically, slide the second arrow so its tail sits on the first arrow's tip; the sum runs from the first tail to the second tip. The horizontal and vertical parts never interfere: 2 across then 4 across is 6 across, whatever happened vertically. This is exactly $(2 + 3i) + (4 - i) = 6 + 2i$.
- **Subtraction is adding the opposite, and the order matters.** $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}$ means $\mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{v})$, where $-\mathbf{v}$ is $\mathbf{v}$ scaled by $-1$. Worked: $\langle 5, 1 \rangle - \langle 2, 4 \rangle = \langle 5-2,\; 1-4 \rangle = \langle 3, -3 \rangle$. Reversing the order gives $\langle -3, 3 \rangle$, the exact negative — a distractor on almost every subtraction question. Drawn from a common tail, $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}$ is the arrow *from the tip of $\mathbf{v}$ to the tip of $\mathbf{u}$*, which is why difference vectors answer "what change gets me from here to there".
- **Magnitudes do not add — the arithmetic is componentwise, the geometry is not.** $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\| \le \|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|$, with equality only when both point the same way. Worked: $\langle 3, 0 \rangle$ and $\langle 0, 4 \rangle$ have magnitudes 3 and 4, but their sum $\langle 3, 4 \rangle$ has magnitude 5, not 7. In general $\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 + 2\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$ — the **Laws of sines and cosines** applied to the triangle the two arrows close.
- **Vector word problems: name the axes first, then it is the same three moves with units.** Fix a convention — east is $+x$, north is $+y$ — and every phrase becomes components. "3 units east and 4 units north" is $\langle 3, 4 \rangle$, not $\langle 4, 3 \rangle$; the order of the words is not the order of the components, the axes decide. Velocities compose by addition: a boat with velocity $\langle 6, 2 \rangle$ in a current of $\langle 1, -1 \rangle$ has resultant velocity $\langle 7, 1 \rangle$. Displacements compose the same way: 5 units east then 2 units west is $\langle 5, 0 \rangle + \langle -2, 0 \rangle = \langle 3, 0 \rangle$, a net 3 units east. And a force given as $\langle 3, 4 \rangle$ has magnitude $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$ — "how strong" is the magnitude, "which way" is the direction. Vectors suit motion problems precisely because they **encode both magnitude and direction** in one object; a scalar speed of 40 km/h cannot say where you end up, and the units never leave the problem.

**Rules to remember**

- A vector is an ordered pair of components $\langle a, b \rangle$. Length alone is not a vector; it is half of one.
- $\|\langle a, b \rangle\| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$. Always the square root, never $a+b$, never negative.
- Components from magnitude and direction: $\langle r\cos\theta,\, r\sin\theta \rangle$. Direction back from components: $\tan^{-1}(b/a)$, then fix the quadrant.
- $0^\circ$ is $\langle 1, 0 \rangle$, $90^\circ$ is $\langle 0, 1 \rangle$, $180^\circ$ is $\langle -1, 0 \rangle$, $270^\circ$ is $\langle 0, -1 \rangle$ — counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis.
- $k\langle a, b \rangle = \langle ka, kb \rangle$: **both** components. $|k| > 1$ lengthens, $|k| < 1$ shortens, $k < 0$ reverses, $k = 0$ collapses. Scaling never rotates by $90^\circ$.
- Add and subtract componentwise: $\langle 2,3 \rangle + \langle 4,-1 \rangle = \langle 6,2 \rangle$; $\langle 5,1 \rangle - \langle 2,4 \rangle = \langle 3,-3 \rangle$.
- $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{v})$, and $\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{u}$ is its negative. Addition commutes; subtraction does not.
- $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|$ is generally less than $\|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|$. Add the components, then take the magnitude — never the reverse.
- In a word problem, write the axis convention down before the arithmetic. Every sign error starts there.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Answering a magnitude question with $a + b$ or $a^2 + b^2$.** For $\langle 3, 4 \rangle$ those give 7 and 25; the magnitude is 5. Both appear as distractors on purpose.
- **Scaling only one component.** $3\langle 1, -2 \rangle$ is $\langle 3, -6 \rangle$; $\langle 3, -2 \rangle$ and $\langle 1, -6 \rangle$ are the two half-done answers.
- **Thinking a negative scalar rotates.** It reverses, a $180^\circ$ flip along the same line. Nothing in this lesson rotates a vector by $90^\circ$.
- **Subtracting in the wrong order.** $\langle 5,1 \rangle - \langle 2,4 \rangle = \langle 3,-3 \rangle$, while $\langle -3, 3 \rangle$ is the reverse difference. Check the sign of each component separately.
- **Reading $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}$ as "add $\mathbf{u}$ to $\mathbf{v}$", a midpoint, or a product of magnitudes.** It is $\mathbf{u}$ plus the opposite of $\mathbf{v}$, and nothing else.
- **Adding magnitudes instead of vectors.** Two forces of 3 and 4 give 7 only if they point the same way; perpendicular they give 5.
- **Swapping the components in a word problem.** "3 east, 4 north" with east as $+x$ is $\langle 3, 4 \rangle$. Writing $\langle 4, 3 \rangle$ answers a different question.
- **Dropping the direction of a reversal.** "2 units west" is $\langle -2, 0 \rangle$, so 5 east then 2 west nets 3 east, not 7.
- **Trusting $\tan^{-1}$ blindly.** Its principal range covers only two quadrants, so a vector pointing into quadrant II or III needs $180^\circ$ added — the same restriction issue you met with **Inverse trigonometric functions**.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Convert to components before doing anything else. Magnitude, direction, sum and difference questions all become arithmetic on two numbers once you are there.
2. For magnitude, square, add, then take the root — and sanity-check that the answer is larger than each component and smaller than their sum.
3. For direction questions, place the arrow on the unit circle rather than computing. $90^\circ$ is straight up, so the answer must have a positive second component and a zero first one.
4. For scalar multiples, ask two questions in order: did both components change, and did the sign pattern change? That eliminates three distractors immediately.
5. For sums and differences, compute each component separately and out loud. The distractors are usually one component right and one wrong, so a partial check catches them.
6. For word problems, write the axis convention, translate each phrase into a vector, then add. Only convert back to magnitude or compass direction at the very end, when the question asks for it.

**Where this leads**: the next lesson, **07 Matrices**, takes the arrows you have just built and asks what can *act* on them. Matrix arithmetic — addition, subtraction and scalar multiplication — is this lesson's componentwise rules run on a rectangle of numbers instead of a pair. Matrix multiplication is genuinely new: it combines a whole grid with a vector to produce another vector, which is what makes **matrices as transformations of the plane** possible, and rotation finally becomes an operation you can apply rather than an angle you compute. Inverses and solving systems with matrices then answer the question this lesson cannot: given where an arrow landed, which arrow went in?
