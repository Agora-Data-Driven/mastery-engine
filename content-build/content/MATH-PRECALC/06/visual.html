1. Modulus Becomes Magnitude

You already carry a point in the plane as one object with a length and a heading — that was The complex plane, Modulus and argument and Polar form of complex numbers. Move the two numbers and watch the same arrow answer to both names. Then pick an operation and see where the two stories agree, and the one place they part. Like the same street address written as a postcode or as "two blocks north, one east".

Keep one difference in view. Two complex numbers can be multiplied, and in polar form that multiplies the moduli and adds the arguments — a rotation. Two vectors have no such product in this lesson: they are scaled, added and subtracted, and that is the whole toolkit. A vector is the arrow without the rotation rule attached.

2. Length And Heading

One arrow, two readings, and a round trip between them. Set a magnitude and a direction and watch the components appear; the panel then recomputes the magnitude and direction back out of those components, so you can see the two descriptions are the same information. Like giving a taxi a distance and a compass bearing, or giving it street-grid steps — same corner.

The four quarter-turns are worth knowing cold, because questions ask for them by angle and answer in components: 0° is <1, 0>, 90° is <0, 1>, 180° is <-1, 0>, 270° is <0, -1>, all times the magnitude. Counterclockwise from the positive x-axis is the convention; read it clockwise and every answer is reflected.

3. Stretch Flip Collapse

A scalar has exactly three things it can do to an arrow, and rotating by a quarter turn is not one of them. Drag the multiplier through positive, zero and negative and watch the copy slide along one fixed line through the origin. Like a volume knob: it makes the same song louder, quieter, or silent — it never changes the song.

The arithmetic trap is scaling one component. Watch the readout: at k = 3 the vector <1, -2> becomes <3, -6>, and the two half-done answers <3, -2> and <1, -6> would both leave the dotted line the arrow is confined to. If your answer is off the line, you scaled half the vector.

4. Tip To Tail

Add the components and the picture takes care of itself: slide the second arrow's tail to the first arrow's tip, and the answer runs from start to finish. Switch to subtraction and watch it become "add the opposite". Like two legs of a walk: the total is where you ended up, not how far you trudged.

Order is the whole difference between the two subtractions. u − v and v − u have the same length and opposite signs in every component, so a question that swaps them is not testing arithmetic — it is testing whether you read which vector came first. Addition has no such trap: u + v and v + u are the same arrow.

5. Boat And Current

A word problem is the last three visuals with units attached. Pick a scenario, move the second vector, and watch the sentence, the components and the resultant stay in step — including the moment a helpful current becomes a hostile one. Like walking up a moving escalator: your speed and its speed only combine once you agree which way is up.

Everything here rests on one line written before any arithmetic: east is +x, north is +y. Fix that and "2 units west" becomes <-2, 0> without thinking, signs stop being guesswork, and the answer at the end is converted back to plain English only when the question asks for it — a magnitude if it wants "how fast" or "how strong", a direction if it wants "which way".
