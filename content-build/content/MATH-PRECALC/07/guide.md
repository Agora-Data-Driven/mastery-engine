**The big idea**: **Vector components, magnitude, and direction** gave you a column of numbers you could point at, and **Scalar multiplication, addition, and subtraction** gave you the rule for combining two such columns one component at a time. A matrix is the next object up — several of those columns side by side, so a $2 \times 2$ matrix is two plane vectors and a $2 \times 3$ is three. Almost everything here follows from that picture. Matrix *arithmetic* is that vector arithmetic done column by column, which is why it inherits a shape restriction. Matrix *multiplication* is not arithmetic at all: it is **Composing functions and modeling** written in numbers, so order matters as violently as it did for $f \circ g$. The *inverse* is **Verifying inverse functions** applied to a whole system at once — a genuine round trip $A^{-1}A = I$, existing exactly when the map is one-to-one, the same criterion you used in **Invertible functions and restricting domains**. And the geometry closes the loop: a $2 \times 2$ matrix acting on column vectors *is* a linear transformation of the plane, and its rotation matrix is **Polar form of complex numbers** wearing different notation.

**Key concepts**

- **Dimensions come first: a matrix is $m \times n$, rows before columns, and every rule below is a shape rule before it is an arithmetic rule.** `[[1,2],[3,4]]` is $2 \times 2$; `[[1,2,3],[4,5,6]]` is $2 \times 3$ — two rows, three columns. Entry $a_{ij}$ sits in row $i$, column $j$. Reverse that order and every dimension question flips.
- **Addition and subtraction are entrywise, and defined only when the two matrices have identical dimensions.** Each position needs exactly one partner. `[[1,2],[3,4]] + [[2,0],[-1,5]] = [[3,2],[2,9]]`, position by position: $1+2=3$, $2+0=2$, $3+(-1)=2$, $4+5=9$. Subtraction is the same with the signs watched: `[[4,1],[0,3]] - [[2,-1],[5,1]] = [[2,2],[-5,2]]`, since $1-(-1)=2$ and $0-5=-5$. Mismatched dimensions leave some position with no partner, so the operation is undefined — "both square" is not the condition ($2 \times 2$ and $3 \times 3$ are both square and cannot be added), and determinants are irrelevant to it. This is exactly column-by-column vector addition: the first column of that sum is `<1,3>` $+$ `<2,-1>` $=$ `<3,2>`.
- **Scalar multiplication multiplies every entry by $k$ — every single one, including the zeros.** The scalar multiple `2[[1,-3],[4,0]]` is `[[2,-6],[8,0]]`: the $-3$ doubles to $-6$, and the $0$ stays $0$ because $2 \cdot 0 = 0$, not because it was skipped. Unlike addition, scaling needs no shape agreement — any matrix of any dimensions can be scaled. Three distractors here each name a *different* operation: adding $k$ to every entry, scaling only the main diagonal, and interchanging the rows with the columns — that last is the **transpose**.
- **Matrix multiplication is row-times-column, and $AB$ is defined exactly when the column count of $A$ equals the row count of $B$.** The inner dimensions must match, and they vanish: $(m \times n)(n \times p) = m \times p$. So a $2 \times 3$ times a $3 \times 4$ is $2 \times 4$ — the threes meet and cancel, leaving the outer pair. The mechanism explains the condition: entry $(i,j)$ of $AB$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$, and two lists can only be dotted if they are the same length.
- **A worked product, and the mistake it rules out.** For `[[1,0],[2,1]] [[3,4],[5,6]]`: row 1 · col 1 $= 1(3)+0(5) = 3$; row 1 · col 2 $= 1(4)+0(6) = 4$; row 2 · col 1 $= 2(3)+1(5) = 11$; row 2 · col 2 $= 2(4)+1(6) = 14$ — giving `[[3,4],[11,14]]`. Multiplying entrywise would have produced `[[3,0],[10,6]]` — a different operation, never the product. A matrix times a column vector is the same rule with $p = 1$: `[[1,2],[0,1]] [[3],[4]]` has entries $1(3)+2(4) = 11$ and $0(3)+1(4) = 4$, so the answer is `[[11],[4]]` — a $2 \times 2$ times a $2 \times 1$ gives a $2 \times 1$.
- **Commutativity is the property that generally fails; associativity, distributivity and an identity all survive.** With the same two matrices, $AB =$ `[[3,4],[11,14]]` but $BA =$ `[[11,4],[17,6]]`, because row 1 of $B$ against the columns of $A$ gives $3(1)+4(2) = 11$ and $3(0)+4(1) = 4$. Shape alone can already kill the reverse product: a $2 \times 3$ times a $3 \times 4$ is defined while $3 \times 4$ times $2 \times 3$ is not. What does hold is $A(BC) = (AB)C$, $A(B+C) = AB + AC$, and $AI = IA = A$. Particular pairs may still commute — any scalar multiple of the identity commutes with everything — but that is a fact about those matrices, exactly as two pure scalings commuted by coincidence in **Composing functions and modeling**.
- **The identity matrix is the multiplicative 1, and it is its own inverse.** $I =$ `[[1,0],[0,1]]` leaves every vector unchanged, since $I$ applied to `<x,y>` gives $\langle 1x + 0y,\ 0x + 1y \rangle = \langle x, y \rangle$. Because $I \cdot I = I$, the round trip passes with $I^{-1} = I$ — while `[[-1,0],[0,-1]]` is the half-turn, a different matrix that is also its own inverse.
- **The determinant $ad - bc$ decides whether an inverse exists, and for $2 \times 2$ there is a formula.** For $A =$ `[[a,b],[c,d]]`, $\det A = ad - bc$ — not $ad + bc$, not $ab - cd$ — and when it is non-zero, $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc}$ `[[d,-b],[-c,a]]`: swap the main diagonal, negate the other two, divide by the determinant. Worked: $A =$ `[[1,1],[1,-1]]` has $\det = (1)(-1) - (1)(1) = -2$, so $A^{-1} = -\frac{1}{2}$ `[[-1,-1],[-1,1]]` $=$ `[[0.5,0.5],[0.5,-0.5]]`. When $\det = 0$ that division is impossible and no inverse exists: the transformation crushes the plane onto a line, so different inputs land on the same output and there is no single-valued return trip — precisely the one-to-one failure you learned to spot with the horizontal line test.
- **A system of linear equations is one matrix equation $AX = B$, and $A^{-1}$ isolates $X$ because $A^{-1}A$ is the identity.** Take `x + y = 5` and `x - y = 1`. The coefficients form $A =$ `[[1,1],[1,-1]]`, the unknowns $X =$ `[[x],[y]]`, the constants $B =$ `[[5],[1]]`. Left-multiplying gives $A^{-1}(AX) = (A^{-1}A)X = IX = X$, so $X = A^{-1}B =$ `[[0.5,0.5],[0.5,-0.5]] [[5],[1]]` $=$ `[[3],[2]]`: $x = 3$, $y = 2$, checked in two seconds by $3 + 2 = 5$ and $3 - 2 = 1$. Two details carry weight: the identity is what makes $A$ disappear — nothing "cancels" and $B$ is not converted into anything — and you must multiply on the **left of both sides**, since $BA^{-1}$ is a different product, here not even defined.
- **A $2 \times 2$ matrix acting on column vectors is a linear transformation of the plane, and its columns say where the basis goes.** Applying $A$ to `<x,y>` produces $x \cdot (\text{column } 1) + y \cdot (\text{column } 2)$. Worked: `[[2,0],[0,2]]` applied to `<1,3>` gives $\langle 2(1)+0(3),\ 0(1)+2(3) \rangle =$ `<2,6>` — uniform scaling by 2, the vector's own scalar multiplication carried out by a matrix. It is not a lookup table of values, not a polynomial equation and not a complex conjugate — it is a rule sending every vector somewhere, pinning the origin and keeping straight lines straight.
- **Read the standard transformations of the plane straight off the entries.** `[[1,0],[0,1]]` leaves every vector unchanged. `[[1,0],[0,-1]]` sends `<x,y>` to `<x,-y>` — reflection across the $x$-axis — while `[[-1,0],[0,1]]` gives `<-x,y>`, reflection across the $y$-axis. `[[0,1],[1,0]]` sends `<x,y>` to `<y,x>`, swapping the coordinates, which is reflection across the line $y = x$. `[[k,0],[0,k]]` scales everything by $k$. Rotation by $\theta$ about the origin is $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$, and multiplying two of them gives the rotation by $\theta + \phi$: expand the product and its entries are literally the **Angle addition identities** — the same fact you met as **Multiplying/Dividing complex numbers in polar form**, where arguments add.

**Rules to remember**

- Dimensions are rows $\times$ columns. $A + B$ needs identical dimensions; $AB$ needs the column count of $A$ to equal the row count of $B$.
- $(m \times n)(n \times p) = m \times p$: the inner dimensions must match, then disappear.
- Addition, subtraction and scalar multiplication are entrywise; multiplication never is, and a scalar hits every entry of a matrix of any shape.
- Entry $(i,j)$ of $AB$ is row $i$ of $A$ dotted with column $j$ of $B$.
- $AB \neq BA$ in general; associativity, distributivity and the identity all hold.
- $I$ leaves every vector unchanged, $AI = IA = A$, and $I^{-1} = I$.
- $\det$ `[[a,b],[c,d]]` $= ad - bc$; the inverse exists exactly when that is non-zero, and equals $\frac{1}{ad-bc}$ `[[d,-b],[-c,a]]`.
- Solve $AX = B$ by left-multiplying: $X = A^{-1}B$, valid because $A^{-1}A = I$.
- The columns of a $2 \times 2$ matrix are the images of `<1,0>` and `<0,1>`, and the origin never moves.

**Common pitfalls**

- Adding matrices of different dimensions, or believing the condition is that both be square.
- Adding the scalar instead of multiplying by it, or scaling only the main diagonal.
- Confusing the transpose — rows becoming columns — with a scalar multiple or with the inverse.
- Multiplying entrywise: `[[1,0],[2,1]] [[3,4],[5,6]]` is `[[3,4],[11,14]]`, never `[[3,0],[10,6]]`.
- Reading a product's dimensions as the outer pair reversed, or as the dimensions of $A$. A $2 \times 3$ times a $3 \times 4$ is $2 \times 4$.
- Computing $BA$ when the question said $AB$, or assuming they agree because they did in one example.
- Naming associativity or distributivity as the property that fails. It is commutativity.
- Using $ad + bc$ or $ab - cd$ for the determinant, or dropping the two minus signs in the inverse formula.
- Dividing by a zero determinant instead of concluding that no inverse exists.
- Right-multiplying to solve $AX = B$, or explaining the step as "the inverses cancel" as though $A$ were a number.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Shape first: write the dimensions under each matrix before computing anything. For a sum they must be identical; for a product the inner pair must match and the answer is the outer pair.
2. Compute one *strategic* position rather than the whole answer — a negative, a zero, a subtraction, or on a product the bottom-left entry — because that is where the options separate. In `[[4,1],[0,3]] - [[2,-1],[5,1]]`, the entries $1-(-1) = 2$ and $0-5 = -5$ settle it alone.
3. When a question asks which property fails, answer commutativity and have the counterexample ready: `[[1,0],[2,1]]` and `[[3,4],[5,6]]` give `[[3,4],[11,14]]` in one order and `[[11,4],[17,6]]` in the other.
4. For inverses and systems, compute $ad - bc$ first. Zero ends the question; non-zero hands you the formula. Then solve and substitute back — the check is faster than redoing the algebra.
5. For a transformation, feed it `<1,0>` and `<0,1>` or just read its columns. To test a claimed effect, push a lopsided vector such as `<1,3>` through it — `<1,1>` is left alone by the coordinate swap, so it cannot tell that matrix from the identity.

**Where this leads**: matrices gave you a language for many quantities moving under one rule, and all of it was deterministic — the same input always lands in the same place. The next lesson, **08 Probability and combinatorics**, keeps the bookkeeping and drops the determinism: Venn diagrams with the addition and multiplication rules, permutations and combinations for counting arrangements, and discrete random variables with their expected value. The connection is closer than it looks: a table of transition probabilities is a matrix whose rows are conditional distributions, and multiplying it by a state vector advances the system exactly the way you multiplied `[[1,0],[2,1]]` by a column today.
