1. From Vectors To Matrices

You already add vector components one at a time, and already scale a vector by a number. Stand two of those columns side by side and you have a matrix — the arithmetic does not change, only the amount of it. Switch the operation, drag the scalar, then break the shapes on purpose. Like adding two shopping lists line by line: it only works while every line has a partner.

The shape rule is not bureaucracy — it is the definition. Every position needs exactly one partner, so "both square" is not the condition: a 2 × 2 and a 3 × 3 are both square and still cannot be added, and determinants have nothing to do with it. Scaling is the one operation here that never asks about shape, which is your clue that it is vector scaling applied entry by entry and nothing more.

2. Rows Meet Columns

Multiplication is the one operation that is not entrywise. Set the four dimensions and watch the product appear or vanish, then tap any entry of a real product to see which row met which column. Like a handshake across a table: every hand on one side needs exactly one hand on the other, or nobody shakes.

shape lab — A is m × n, B is q × p

Row-times-column is not a convention someone chose — it is what makes AB the composition of the two transformations, "do B, then do A". That is also why the inner dimensions must agree: whatever B produces has to be something A can consume. Read the rule as a sentence and the dimension questions stop needing memory.

3. AB Versus BA

One property of ordinary numbers does not survive the move to matrices. Pick a pair, then test each property in turn and watch which two sides agree. Like getting dressed: socks then shoes and shoes then socks are not the same morning.

Non-commutativity is geometry, not arithmetic. Rotate-then-reflect and reflect-then-rotate are two different reflections, and no amount of care with the numbers will merge them. Notice what does survive: you may regroup a triple product and expand over a sum freely — you simply may not reorder. And shape alone can settle it: a 2 × 3 times a 3 × 4 is defined while the reverse is not.

4. Solve By Inverse

Two equations are one matrix equation A X = B, and the inverse is the undo button — when there is one. Drag the coefficients: the determinant, the inverse, the solution and the two lines all move together. Like a lock and its key: turn the determinant to zero and the key stops existing, not just stops working.

A zero determinant is not a computational accident, it is the plane collapsing onto a line: the two rows describe parallel or identical lines, so there is no single crossing point to return to. That is the same collapse as a function failing to be one-to-one — different inputs share an output, so no single-valued undo can exist. Non-zero determinant, one intersection, one inverse.

5. Moving The Plane

A 2 × 2 matrix is a rule that moves every vector of the plane at once, and its two columns are the whole rule: where <1,0> goes and where <0,1> goes. Pick a transformation, spin the angle, then move the test vector. Like a stamp pressed on a rubber sheet: one press, and every point on the sheet has moved.

Two habits pay for themselves. Read the columns: column 1 is the image of <1,0>, column 2 the image of <0,1>, so you can name a matrix without computing anything. And test with a lopsided vector: <1,1> is left exactly where it is by the coordinate swap, so it can never tell that matrix apart from the identity. The origin, note, never moves under any of these.
