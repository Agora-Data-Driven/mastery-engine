**The big idea**: **Composing functions and modeling** taught you to read a formula as a machine with a domain and a codomain, and a random variable is exactly that — a function whose domain is a set of outcomes. **Vector components, magnitude, and direction** and **Matrix multiplication and properties** gave you the weighted sum, a row of weights against a column of values, and an expected value is exactly that. **Advanced rational expression arithmetic** taught you to cancel a quotient before multiplying it out, which is the entire computational content of a combination. Almost nothing here is new machinery; what is new is one constraint on machinery you already own — the weights are *probabilities*, so each is at least $0$ and together they sum to exactly $1$. Every result below is that constraint plus one discipline: **count every outcome exactly once**. The addition rule repairs a double count, the multiplication rule assembles a joint outcome from stages, combinations divide out an over-count of $r!$, and expected value collapses the weighting into a single number.

**Key concepts**

- **A Venn diagram draws the sample space, and the overlapping region is the intersection.** The rectangle is the sample space $S$; each circle is an event, a subset of $S$; where the circles overlap is $A \cap B$, the outcomes in *both* events at once. The union $A \cup B$ is everything inside either circle, and the region outside both is the complement of the union. The picture turns event algebra into region arithmetic. Take 30 engineers: 18 write Python (event $A$), 12 write SQL (event $B$), 7 write both. The overlap is 7, so Python-only is $18 - 7 = 11$, SQL-only is $12 - 7 = 5$, and neither is $30 - (11 + 7 + 5) = 7$. Four disjoint regions, every engineer in exactly one.
- **The addition rule subtracts $P(A \cap B)$ to remove the outcomes counted twice.** $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$. The mechanism is bookkeeping, not shrinkage. $P(A)$ already contains every outcome of the overlap, and so does $P(B)$, so adding the two terms counts the overlap **twice** while the union needs it once; subtracting one copy restores the count to one. With the engineers, $\frac{18}{30} + \frac{12}{30} - \frac{7}{30} = \frac{23}{30}$, matching the direct count $11 + 7 + 5 = 23$. Drop the subtraction and you get $\frac{30}{30} = 1$ — the claim that every engineer writes at least one of the two, which is false, because 7 write neither. When the events are **mutually exclusive** the overlap is empty, $P(A \cap B) = 0$, and the rule collapses to $P(A) + P(B)$: a special case, not a second rule.
- **The multiplication rule assembles a joint event from stages: $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A)$.** Read it as area: lay the sample space out as a unit square, take a strip of width $P(A)$, then keep the fraction $P(B \mid A)$ of *that strip's* height; the shaded rectangle's area is the joint probability. **Independence** says knowing $A$ occurred does not change $B$'s chance, $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$, and only then does the rule simplify to $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. With $P(A) = 0.3$ and $P(B) = 0.4$ independent, $P(A \cap B) = 0.3 \times 0.4 = 0.12$ — smaller than either factor, because requiring both conditions can only narrow the outcome set.
- **Independent and mutually exclusive are near opposites, not synonyms.** Mutually exclusive means the events cannot co-occur, so $P(A \cap B) = 0$; independent means they co-occur at exactly the product rate. Two mutually exclusive events with positive probability are in fact strongly *dependent*: learning $A$ happened drops $P(B \mid A)$ to $0$. Same two numbers, $0.3$ and $0.4$: exclusive gives $P(A \cap B) = 0$ and $P(A \cup B) = 0.7$; independent gives $0.12$ and $0.3 + 0.4 - 0.12 = 0.58$.
- **Permutations count ordered arrangements, and the factorial ratio is just cancellation.** Fill $r$ labelled slots from $n$ distinct items: $n$ choices for the first, $n-1$ for the second, $r$ factors in all, giving $_nP_r = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$. With $n = 10$, $r = 3$ that is $10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720$, and $\frac{10!}{7!}$ cancels to those same three factors without ever evaluating $10!$ — the move you make on a rational expression. Gold, silver and bronze from 10 runners is a permutation: swapping two medallists is a different outcome.
- **Combinations divide the orderings out: $_nC_r = \frac{_nP_r}{r!} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}$.** Every unordered set of $r$ items appears among the ordered lists exactly $r!$ times — once per internal rearrangement — so dividing by $r!$ is exact, never an approximation. From the same numbers, $_{10}C_3 = \frac{720}{6} = 120$: the 720 ordered podiums collapse to 120 three-person committees, each counted 6 times. The symmetry $_nC_r = {}_nC_{n-r}$ is free, since choosing 3 to include is the same act as choosing 7 to exclude: $_{10}C_7 = 120$ too.
- **Choosing between them is one question: does swapping two chosen items change the outcome?** If yes — podium places, passwords, seat assignments — it is a permutation. If no — a committee, a hand of cards, a subset of features — it is a combination. Since $r! \geq 1$, permutations are always at least as numerous as combinations.
- **Counting becomes probability the moment outcomes are equally likely.** Then $P = \frac{\text{favourable}}{\text{total}}$, with the same counting convention top and bottom. Draw 3 items from a batch of 10 containing 4 defective: total ways $_{10}C_3 = 120$; exactly one defective means choosing 1 of the 4 defective and 2 of the 6 good, $_4C_1 \times {}_6C_2 = 4 \times 15 = 60$ — the multiplication rule again. So $P = \frac{60}{120} = 0.5$.
- **A discrete random variable is a function from outcomes to numbers, and its distribution must sum to 1.** $X$ assigns a number to each outcome; the distribution is the list of distinct values $x_i$ with probabilities $p_i = P(X = x_i)$. Validity has two conditions: every $p_i \geq 0$, and $\sum_i p_i = 1$ — because the values partition the sample space, so their probabilities must account for *all* of it. A table reading $0.5, 0.2, 0.2$ is not a distribution; the missing $0.1$ is an outcome you failed to list. The sum is $1$, never $0$, never the mean, never the standard deviation.
- **Expected value is the probability-weighted sum $E[X] = \sum_i x_i\,p_i$, and it is the long-run average of outcomes.** It is literally the dot product of the value vector with the probability vector — the row-times-column product from matrices, with the sum-to-one constraint making it an *average* rather than just a weighted total. Roll a fair die: $E[X] = \frac{1}{6}(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) = 3.5$. That is the number the running mean of repeated rolls settles toward, and no single roll ever produces it — **an expected value need not be an attainable outcome**.
- **Expected value is not the most likely value and not the middle value.** Take $X = 0$ with $p = 0.7$, $X = 10$ with $p = 0.2$, $X = 100$ with $p = 0.1$. Then $E[X] = 0(0.7) + 10(0.2) + 100(0.1) = 12$, while the most likely value (the mode) is $0$ and the middle value (the median) is also $0$. Only the mean lets a rare large outcome pull it — which is why it is the right summary for long-run totals and the wrong one for "what will probably happen next".
- **In decisions, put the cost inside the sum.** A \$2 ticket paying \$500 with probability $0.001$ has expected profit $500(0.001) - 2 = -1.5$ dollars per ticket, so a thousand tickets lose about \$1,500 on average. That scales because expectation is linear: $E[aX + b] = a\,E[X] + b$, from factoring constants out of the sum and using $\sum p_i = 1$.

**Rules to remember**

- $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$; the subtracted term is the overlap counted twice, nothing more.
- On a Venn diagram: overlap $=$ intersection, both circles together $=$ union, outside $=$ complement.
- $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B \mid A)$ always; $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$ only when the events are independent.
- Mutually exclusive $\Rightarrow P(A \cap B) = 0$. Independent $\Rightarrow P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. Both at once only if a probability is $0$.
- $_nP_r = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$ (order matters); $_nC_r = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}$ (order does not), and $_nC_r = \frac{_nP_r}{r!}$.
- $_nC_r = {}_nC_{n-r}$, and $_nC_0 = {}_nC_n = 1$.
- A valid discrete distribution has $p_i \geq 0$ and $\sum p_i = 1$.
- $E[X] = \sum x_i p_i$: the long-run average, not the mode, not the median, and not necessarily an attainable value.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Reading the subtraction in the addition rule as a fudge factor.** It is not there to shrink a probability, to force independence, or because shared outcomes cannot happen; it removes a double count, and equals $0$ exactly when the overlap is empty.
- **Calling the overlap the union.** The overlap is the intersection — the *smaller* region, requiring both.
- **Adding when the question says "and".** With $P(A) = 0.3$ and $P(B) = 0.4$ independent, $0.7$ is the union, not the intersection; the answer is $0.12$.
- **Treating mutually exclusive as independent.** Exclusive events give $P(A \cap B) = 0$, which is a product only if one probability is $0$.
- **Using $P(A)P(B)$ when the draws are without replacement.** Then the second stage is conditional and $P(B \mid A) \neq P(B)$.
- **Using a permutation for a committee.** It over-counts by $r!$ — the single most common counting error.
- **Cancelling factorials wrongly.** $\frac{10!}{7!} = 720$, not $\frac{10}{7}$; factorials do not divide term by term.
- **Mixing ordered and unordered counts in one fraction.** Use the same convention above and below the bar, or the probability comes out nonsense.
- **Accepting a probability table that does not sum to 1.** Check the sum before computing anything from it.
- **Calling the expected value the most likely outcome.** In the $0/10/100$ example the mean is $12$, a value the variable can never take.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Name the region before you compute. "Both", "and", "overlap" mean intersection; "either", "or", "at least one" mean union. Most Venn questions turn on that translation alone.
2. For any $P(A \cup B)$, write the full addition rule and *then* ask whether the intersection term is $0$. Reaching for $P(A) + P(B)$ first is how the double count survives.
3. For "and" questions, ask whether the stages are independent. If yes, multiply the marginals; if not, multiply by the conditional. Given $0.3$ and $0.4$, decide deliberately between $0.12$ (product) and $0.7$ (sum).
4. For counting, ask the swap test — does reordering the chosen items give a new outcome? — then write $_nP_r$ or $_nC_r$ before touching arithmetic, and cancel the factorials rather than expanding them.
5. For distribution questions, sum the probabilities first. Any option other than $1$ for that sum is wrong, and a missing entry is usually recovered as $1$ minus the rest.
6. For expected value, build the $x_i p_i$ column and add it. Distractors are almost always the mode, the median, an unweighted average, or a single probability.

**Where this leads**: the next lesson, **09 Series**, gives that summation its own machinery — summation notation makes $\sum x_i p_i$ a first-class object, arithmetic and geometric series give closed forms for the infinite sums that appear once a random variable takes infinitely many values, and the Binomial Theorem shows $_nC_r$ was never merely a counting device: the combinations you computed here are precisely the coefficients of $(a + b)^n$, which is why the same numbers organise both a committee count and a polynomial.
