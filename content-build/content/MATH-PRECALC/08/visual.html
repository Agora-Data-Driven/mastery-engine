1. Dot Product Average

Vector components, magnitude, and direction and Matrix multiplication and properties already gave you a row of weights times a column of values. An expected value is that same product with one extra rule bolted on: the weights must sum to 1. Drag the three probabilities and watch the rule bite. Like a class average: multiply each mark by its share of the class, and the shares had better add up to the whole class.

The sum-to-one rule is not decoration. Without it the row-times-column product is only a weighted total; with it, the total becomes an average, because the weights now describe how a whole is divided up. That is also why a table whose probabilities add to 0.9 is not "slightly off" — it is a table with an outcome missing.

2. Counted Twice

30 engineers: 18 write Python, 12 write SQL. Drag how many write both and watch P(A) + P(B) stay stubbornly at 1.000 while the true union shrinks. The gap between the two bars is exactly the region you counted twice. Like two guest lists for one party: add the headcounts and everyone invited by both hosts gets a chair they cannot sit in.

Read the rule as bookkeeping and its edge cases stop being special. The subtracted term is not a fudge to make the number smaller, not a way to force independence, and certainly not a claim that shared outcomes cannot happen — it is one copy of a region that got counted twice. When the overlap is empty that copy is 0, and the rule already says P(A) + P(B) without anyone adding a second rule.

3. Multiply The Stages

P(A and B) = P(A) x P(B given A) is an area: take a strip of the sample space of width P(A), then keep a fraction of that strip's height. Switch the relationship and watch the same two numbers, 0.30 and 0.40, produce three different answers. Like taking 40% of the 30% of a field you already fenced off — the second percentage is of the fenced part, not the whole field.

Independent and mutually exclusive are near opposites, and the square shows why. Exclusive means the two shaded blocks never share a column — so learning that A happened forces P(B given A) to 0, which is as dependent as two events can get. Independent means the shaded fraction of A's strip is the same as the shaded fraction of everything else; the strips do not care about each other at all.

4. Order Or Not

Permutations and combinations count the same choices; only one of them cares what order they came in. Toggle order on and watch every unordered set explode into exactly r! ordered lists — the factor you divide back out. Like a photo of three friends: one group of friends, six ways to line them up for the camera.

One question decides every counting problem: does swapping two of the chosen items give a different outcome? Gold, silver and bronze — yes, so permutation. A three-person committee — no, so combination. Because r! is never below 1, the ordered count is always at least the unordered one, which makes a quick sanity check: an answer bigger than nPr or smaller than nCr is wrong before you check it.

5. Long Run Average

"Expected value" is a promise about the long run, not about the next trial. Spin these variables and watch the running mean wander wildly, then settle onto E[X] — a value neither of them can actually produce. Like a batting average: meaningless after one at-bat, and hard to shift after a thousand.

Two habits come out of this panel. First, compute E[X] by building the x·p column and adding it — never by averaging the values, which silently assumes every outcome is equally likely. Second, keep the three summaries apart: the mean is the balance point, the mode is the tallest bar, and the median is the middle of the order. Only the mean is what a long run of trials converges to, and only the mean can be a number the variable never takes.
