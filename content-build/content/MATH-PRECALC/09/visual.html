1. Sigma Was Already There

Discrete random variables and Expected Value made you write x0*p0 + x1*p1 + x2*p2 + ... , and Scalar multiplication, addition, and subtraction made you add matched components one index at a time. Both are the same shape: a rule, applied to every index in a range, added up. Sigma is the name for that shape. Drag the bounds and watch the long form and the short form stay equal. Like a recipe that says "repeat for each guest" instead of writing the step out eight times.

Sigma is an instruction, not a number: it says add, it is not a repeated product, not a running difference, and not the limit of a sequence. And notice what the rename button does not change — the index is a counter that disappears when the sum is evaluated, so its letter can never be part of the answer.

2. Difference Or Ratio

Two families, one diagnostic. Both rows below are always computed — the differences between consecutive terms and their ratios — and exactly one of them holds still. Whichever one does names the sequence, and the next term is then a single operation. Like a staircase versus a photocopier: one adds the same rise every step, the other multiplies by the same factor every copy.

Two terms never settle it. From 3, 6 alone the list continues 9, 12 with a constant difference of 3, or 12, 24 with a constant ratio of 2 — both are perfectly good sequences. Always test a third term before you name the family, and run both tests rather than the one you expected to win.

3. Summing Without Adding

A sequence is the list; a series is its sum. Both closed forms below are derived on screen, not quoted: the arithmetic one folds the list against itself, the geometric one subtracts a shifted copy. The brute-force total is computed alongside so you can watch them agree. Like counting a stadium by rows and seats instead of pointing at every seat.

The excluded case is the one to internalise: at r = 1 the geometric formula divides by zero, and the honest answer is that every term equals a1, so the sum is n*a1. A formula with a hole in it is not a broken formula — the hole is where a different, simpler argument takes over.

4. Pascal Builds The Coefficients

The numbers in front of the terms of (a + b)^n are not invented and not memorised: they are the combinations you already know, and the triangle builds each one by adding the two directly above it. Pick a row and an entry, and watch the same number arrive three ways — by addition, by factorials, and by symmetry. Like a family tree run backwards: every entry is born from the two immediately above it.

What the coefficients are not: successive powers of 2, successive primes, or an arithmetic run with one fixed gap. Each row does add up to 2^n — a different claim, and the source of the confusion. The reliable check is symmetry: because choosing k things to include is the same act as choosing n-k to leave out, every row must read the same backwards.

5. One Term Without Expanding

"What is the coefficient of x^2?" is not a request to expand anything. One term of the sum answers it: C(n,k) a^(n-k) b^k, with the single k that puts the right power on x. Pick a binomial, a power and a term, and check the extracted term against the full expansion below it. Like pulling one book off a shelf instead of emptying the shelf to find it.

Two habits survive every question on this topic. Count the terms first — (a + b)^n always gives n+1 of them with exponents adding to n, which kills any two-term option instantly. Then check the coefficient list reads the same backwards, and remember that a negative b flips the sign of every odd-numbered term while leaving the magnitudes untouched.
