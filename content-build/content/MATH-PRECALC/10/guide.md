**The big idea**: **End behavior and discontinuities (Asymptotes)** already had you describe a curve by where it is *heading* rather than where it lands, and **Graphs of rational functions** had you cancel a factor and punch a hole in a graph at a point the function never reaches — the cancellation itself coming straight from **Advanced rational expression arithmetic**. **Arithmetic and Geometric series** went further: an infinite geometric sum is a number no finite partial sum ever produces. Each was a limit, unnamed. This lesson names it and gives it rules. $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ says $f(x)$ stays as close to $L$ as you please once $x$ is close enough to $a$ on **both** sides, with $x = a$ itself excluded from the inspection on purpose. Continuity is the fortunate case where where-it-is-heading and what-it-is agree, $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$, and the Intermediate Value Theorem is the existence guarantee you buy with that agreement.

**Key concepts**

- **Defining and estimating limits (Graphs and Tables): the limit reads the neighbourhood, never the point.** $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ is the value $f(x)$ nears as $x$ nears $a$ — not $f(a)$, not the slope at $a$, not an average of nearby values. The mechanism is the deletion: the definition inspects $x$ near $a$ with $x = a$ itself removed, so whatever happens *at* $a$ gets no vote. Without that deletion the idea would fail exactly where it is needed. $f(x) = \frac{x^2-9}{x-3}$ has no value at $3$ whatsoever, yet the table $f(2.9)=5.9$, $f(2.99)=5.99$, $f(3.01)=6.01$, $f(3.1)=6.1$ closes on $6$ from both sides, so $\lim_{x \to 3} f(x) = 6$. Reading graphs works identically: a curve with a hole punched at $(1,5)$ that approaches $5$ from both sides has $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = 5$, and a table of values settling on $7$ from either side as $x \to 2$ gives $7$.
- **Estimating means reading the two one-sided limits and comparing them.** $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ is what $f$ approaches from below $a$, $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ from above. The two-sided limit exists **exactly when both exist and are equal**, and equals their common value. So if the left-hand limit at $x = 3$ is $4$ and the right-hand limit is $9$, the two-sided limit **does not exist** — it is not $13$ (that is adding them), not $9$, not $4$, and not the average $6.5$. Shrinking the sampling window from $h = 0.1$ to $h = 0.001$ moves both columns of the table but never closes a gap of $5$; a gap that refuses to close *is* the non-existence.
- **Three things can happen at $a$, and the limit is indifferent to all three.** $f(a)$ can be undefined, defined with a different value, or defined and equal to the limit. Only the third is continuity. Patch $\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}$ with the extra rule $f(3)=2$ and the limit is still $6$; patch it with $f(3)=6$ and the function becomes continuous there. Same neighbourhood, same limit, three different functions — which is why "the limit exists" and "the function is defined" are separate questions on every quiz.
- **Limit properties and algebraic manipulation start with the properties: they break a big limit into small ones.** If $\lim f = L$ and $\lim g = M$ **both exist**, then $\lim(f+g) = L+M$, $\lim(f-g) = L-M$, $\lim(fg) = LM$, $\lim(f/g) = L/M$ **provided $M \neq 0$**, $\lim(cf) = cL$, and $\lim f^{\,n} = L^{\,n}$. That is what lets you assemble $\lim_{x \to 2}(x^2+3x-4)$ term by term as $4 + 6 - 4 = 6$. The look-alikes are the whole difficulty: $\lim(f+g)$ is **not** $\lim f \cdot \lim g$, $\lim(f/g)$ is **not** $\lim f - \lim g$, and $\lim(cf)$ is **not** $c + \lim f$. A bogus rule can be accidentally true at one input — with $f(x)=x+1$ and $c=3$, the false $\lim(cf)=c+\lim f$ holds at $a = 0.5$ and nowhere else. One agreement is a coincidence; a law holds at every $a$.
- **Direct substitution is a theorem about continuity, not a shortcut.** Substituting is legal precisely when $f$ is continuous at the point, which is why polynomials always yield ($4+6-4=6$ above), rational functions yield wherever the denominator is non-zero, and roots yield on their domain. Reach for substitution first every time: it either answers the question or hands you the diagnostic $\frac{0}{0}$.
- **Algebraic manipulation is what you do when substitution returns $\frac{0}{0}$.** That form is *indeterminate* — it reports a competition between a numerator and a denominator both collapsing, not a value, and it never means the limit is $0$. Factoring resolves it by cancelling the common factor that caused it: $\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x+3$ for every $x \neq 3$. The two expressions differ only at $3$, the limit never looks at $3$, so they have the same limit — substitute into the simplified form to get $6$. Identically, $\frac{x^2-1}{x-1} = x+1 \to 2$ as $x \to 1$. Note what cancelling does *not* do: it does not enlarge the domain of the original function, still undefined at $3$, and it does not remove substitution — it makes substitution work.
- **Not every $\frac{0}{0}$ cancels, and $\frac{c}{0}$ with $c \neq 0$ is a different animal.** For $\frac{1}{x-3}$ as $x \to 3$ the numerator stays at $1$ while the denominator collapses through opposite signs, so the values run to $-\infty$ on the left and $+\infty$ on the right: the limit does not exist, and the discontinuity is a vertical asymptote — non-removable, the case you met in **End behavior and discontinuities (Asymptotes)**. Expressions with roots need the conjugate rather than a factor: $\frac{\sqrt{x}-2}{x-4} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+2} \to \frac14$ as $x \to 4$.
- **Continuity and the Intermediate Value Theorem: continuity at a point is three boxes ticked in order.** $f$ is continuous at $x=a$ when (1) $f(a)$ exists, (2) $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists, and (3) the two are equal. All three are required — a value with no limit is a jump, a limit with no value is a hole. So $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 5$ together with $f(2) = 5$ makes $f$ continuous at $2$. It does **not** make $f$ differentiable there: $|x-2|$ is continuous at $2$ and has no derivative at $2$.
- **"Continuous for every real number" quietly demands "defined for every real number".** $x^2+1$ qualifies: polynomials are continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$. $\frac{1}{x}$ fails at $0$. $\frac{x-2}{x-2}$ equals $1$ everywhere except $x=2$, where it is $\frac00$ — a hole, so it fails too. $\sqrt{x}$ is perfectly continuous on its domain $[0,\infty)$ but is undefined for negative $x$, so it is not continuous for *every* real number. Continuity is always relative to a domain, and the qualifier is where the marks are.
- **The Intermediate Value Theorem is the payoff: continuity on a closed interval buys existence.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $N$ is any value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then $f(c) = N$ for at least one $c$ in $(a,b)$ — the function attains *every* value between its endpoint values. The corollary you will use most: $f$ continuous with $f(1) = -2$ and $f(3) = 4$ must have a root in $(1,3)$, because $0$ lies between $-2$ and $4$. Take $f(x) = 3(x-2)^3 - 3(x-2)^2 + 4$, which does have those endpoint values; bisection locates its root near $x \approx 1.16$. Existence is all you get: not uniqueness, not location, not that $f$ is increasing, linear or differentiable, and not that $f$ has a maximum and a minimum — that last is the Extreme Value Theorem, a different result.
- **Remove continuity and the guarantee evaporates.** $\frac{1}{x}$ on $[-1,1]$ has $f(-1) = -1$ and $f(1) = 1$, yet is never $0$: the sign change happens *across* the break rather than at a crossing. Every bisection root-finder you have written rests on the IVT and inherits its hypothesis.

**Rules to remember**

- A limit describes approach; $f(a)$ describes arrival. They are separate facts and either can exist without the other.
- Two-sided limit exists $\iff$ both one-sided limits exist and agree. A gap that will not close means the limit does not exist — never split the difference.
- $\frac{0}{0}$ is a signal that the algebra is unfinished, not an answer, and never a licence to write $0$.
- Cancel a common factor to get a function that agrees everywhere except the one point the limit ignores; then substitute.
- $\frac{c}{0}$ with $c \neq 0$ is an asymptote, not a hole: no cancellation exists and the limit does not exist.
- Limit laws distribute over sums, differences, products, constant multiples and powers; quotients need a non-zero denominator limit. Nothing converts $+$ to $\times$.
- Continuity at $a$ = value exists **and** limit exists **and** they match.
- Continuous everywhere requires defined everywhere; $\sqrt{x}$ and $\frac{1}{x}$ both fail that test.
- IVT needs continuity on a **closed** interval and gives existence only, somewhere in the open interval.

**Common pitfalls**

- Reading $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ off the graph at $x = a$ instead of on either side of it — the hole is the one point that does not count.
- Averaging or adding mismatched one-sided limits. Left $4$, right $9$ gives neither $6.5$ nor $13$; it gives "does not exist".
- Announcing that a limit cannot exist because $f(a)$ is undefined. That is the ordinary case.
- Treating $\frac{0}{0}$ as $0$, or as $1$, or as proof the limit fails.
- Cancelling and then claiming the original function is now defined at the point. Only the simplified expression is.
- Using the quotient law when the denominator's limit is $0$, where the law simply does not apply.
- Calling a function continuous everywhere when its domain has gaps.
- Reading IVT as a promise of monotonicity, linearity, uniqueness, differentiability, or of a maximum and minimum.
- Applying IVT to a function with a break inside the interval, which is exactly what $\frac{1}{x}$ on $[-1,1]$ punishes.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Substitute first. A number means you are done; $\frac{0}{0}$ means factor or use a conjugate; $\frac{c}{0}$ with $c \neq 0$ means asymptote, so the limit does not exist.
2. For any graph or table question, cover the point itself and read the two sides separately. Then ask only whether they agree.
3. When a question mentions the value at the point, check whether it changes anything — usually it is there to see if you will let it.
4. For a "which property is valid" item, test the candidate on concrete numbers such as $\lim f = 3$, $\lim g = 4$. Any rule swapping $+$ for $\times$, or $/$ for $-$, dies immediately.
5. For continuity, tick the three boxes out loud in order; the first that fails names the discontinuity as a hole, a jump, or an asymptote.
6. For IVT items, confirm continuity on the closed interval, then confirm the target sits between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. If both hold, the answer is "there exists at least one" — and nothing stronger.
7. Distrust every absolute-sounding option: "always", "guaranteed differentiable", "must be increasing". IVT and continuity are modest theorems.

**Where this leads**: this closes Precalculus. The next course, Calculus, opens with **01 Limits and continuity**, which re-treads this ground at higher resolution and then leaves it behind: the Squeeze Theorem, limits of trigonometric functions such as $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$, a full taxonomy of discontinuities, and conjugate techniques as standard equipment.
