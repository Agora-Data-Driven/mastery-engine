1. Hole Or Value

In Graphs of rational functions you cancelled a factor, marked a hole, and moved on. That hole is where this lesson starts. Choose what happens at x = 3, then close in on it from both sides and watch which of the two answers actually changes. Like a missing stair: you can see exactly where the step should be without ever standing on it.

The neighbourhood of 3 is byte-for-byte identical in all of them, so the limit is 6 in all of them; the only thing that moves is the single number f(3), and it decides continuity alone. That is why "the limit exists" and "the function is defined there" are two separate questions on every quiz — and why answering one with the other is the most common way to lose a mark on this topic.

2. Both Sides Agree

A two-sided limit is a claim that two independent approaches land on the same number. Drag the two arrival heights apart and watch the shrinking window fail to close the gap — that failure is what "does not exist" means. Like two people walking toward each other down a corridor: if they stop at different doors, there is no meeting point to name.

Both branches are drawn with open circles, so f(3) is undefined in every configuration here — and yet the two-sided limit exists whenever the two heights match. That is the second half of visual 1 stated the other way round: the limit and the value are independent facts. When they disagree by a fixed amount the graph has a jump, which is exactly the discontinuity that no algebraic cancellation can ever repair.

3. Cancel The Zero

Substitution is always the first move. It either answers the question or hands you a diagnosis — and 0/0 is a diagnosis, not an answer. Pick an expression, watch the substitution attempt, then watch the table find the value the algebra was hiding. Like a scale reading zero on both pans: it tells you the sides balance, not what either one weighs.

Notice what cancelling never does. It does not enlarge the domain — the original expression is still undefined at the point afterwards. It does not make the limit 0. And it does not replace substitution; it is what makes substitution work. The last two cases are the boundary of the technique: a numerator that refuses to vanish gives an asymptote no factoring can remove, and a root needs a conjugate rather than a factor.

4. Which Law Is Real

Six statements about f(x) = x + 1 and g(x) = x^2. Three are limit laws and three are impostors — and one impostor is true at exactly one value of a, which is the whole trap. Drag a and watch which claims survive everywhere. Like a stopped clock: right twice a day, and still not a clock.

Every number on this panel is measured, not asserted: the left-hand column samples the combined function either side of a, and the right-hand column combines the two limits separately. A law is a claim that those two procedures always agree. Note the quotient row going grey at a = 0 — the quotient law carries the condition lim g != 0, and a law with an unmet condition is not a law that failed, it is a law that does not apply.

5. Every Value Between

The Intermediate Value Theorem trades one hypothesis — continuity on a closed interval — for one guarantee: every height between the two endpoint heights is actually reached. Pick a function, drag the target height, and watch the guarantee appear, vanish, or stay silent. Like driving from a basement to a roof: you cannot skip the ground floor unless there is a hole in the building.

Read what the theorem does not promise, because that is where the marks are: not uniqueness (x^2 + 1 reaches most of its values twice), not a location, not that f is increasing, linear or differentiable, and not that f has a maximum and a minimum — that last one is the Extreme Value Theorem, a different result with the same hypothesis. IVT is an existence statement and nothing more; every bisection root-finder you have written is that statement turned into an algorithm.
