**The big idea**: This is the first lesson of the course, so the ground it stands on is your own. Recall from **probability** that a distribution is nothing but a set of outcomes carrying weights that sum to 1; recall from **linear algebra** that a rectangle of numbers has row sums $A\mathbf{1}$ and column sums $\mathbf{1}^\top A$, and that a matrix built as an outer product has rank one; recall from **machine learning** that a confusion matrix is a 2×2 grid of counts out of which you pull recall along the rows and precision down the columns. Analyzing categorical data is those three facts applied to data whose values are *labels* rather than measurements, and nearly all of it follows from a single move: **count individuals into cells, then choose which total to divide by.** Divide a cell by the grand total and you have a joint proportion; divide a margin by the grand total and you have a marginal distribution; divide a cell by its own group's total and you have a conditional distribution. That last choice carries the lesson, because comparing conditional distributions across groups is how you decide whether two categorical variables are related at all.

**Key concepts**

- **The values of a categorical variable are labels or group names, so the only arithmetic they support is counting.** A categorical value indexes a bucket; it does not sit on a number line, so differences, orderings and means of the values are undefined. Why that matters: every summary you already own — mean, median, standard deviation — silently assumes a metric that labels do not have. If a survey codes region as `1`, `2`, `3`, the mean 2.0 is arithmetic on bucket names, and it changes if you renumber them.
- **Analyzing one categorical variable means building a frequency table and drawing a bar chart.** Tally each label, then draw one bar per category whose height is its count. The bars carry gaps precisely because the horizontal axis carries no distance: you can permute the categories and the display is equally correct. Worked example: a favourite-fruit survey records apple 12, banana 8, orange 10. Apple has the highest frequency at 12 — three bars of heights 12, 8 and 10, in whatever order you like.
- **Relative frequency divides the count by the total, and that is what makes two surveys comparable.** A count alone confounds "how popular" with "how many were asked". Worked example: if 30 of 120 surveyed students choose theater, the relative frequency of theater is $30/120 = 0.25$; the same 30 out of 40 would be $0.75$. The numerator did not move — the denominator decided the answer.
- **The percentages of one categorical variable always total 100%, whatever the sample size.** The categories are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, so the relative frequencies partition 1 and the percentages partition 100 — which is the pie chart's whole licence to exist, since a slice's angle is $360° \times$ relative frequency. Worked example: the fruit survey gives $12/30 = 40.0\%$, $8/30 \approx 26.7\%$, $10/30 \approx 33.3\%$, and $40.0 + 26.7 + 33.3 = 100.0$. A total of 118% means someone was counted twice or the categories overlap; a total that drifts with sample size means you divided by the wrong thing.
- **Histograms, box plots and scatterplots are the wrong displays here, for one shared reason.** A histogram needs bin boundaries, a box plot needs quartiles, a scatterplot needs two numeric coordinates — and none of the three is even definable on labels. That is why the bar chart is *the* display for a single categorical variable: the distinction lives in what the axis is made of.
- **A two-way (contingency) table organizes two categorical variables jointly.** Rows are the categories of one variable, columns the categories of the other, and cell $n_{ij}$ counts the individuals who are in row $i$ *and* column $j$. Worked example, 100 students by athlete status × exam result: athletes 15 passed and 5 failed; non-athletes 40 passed and 40 failed. Every individual is counted exactly once, under two labels at the same time.
- **The margins are the row and column totals, and the grand total is the sum of every cell.** Formally $r_i = \sum_j n_{ij}$, $c_j = \sum_i n_{ij}$, and $\sum_i r_i = \sum_j c_j = n$. In a 2×2 the grand total is the sum of all four cells — not one row, not one column, never a difference. Worked example: the rows give 20 and 80, the columns give 55 and 45, and both routes reach $n = 100$; that agreement is a free arithmetic check every time you build a table. In a table where 12 girls prefer cats and 18 prefer dogs, the row total is $12 + 18 = 30$ girls.
- **Margins are useful because they are the denominators.** A margin divided by $n$ is that variable's own one-variable distribution — the **marginal distribution**, identical to the frequency table you would have built had you ignored the other variable ($20/100$ and $80/100$). And a margin is the denominator of every conditional proportion: among the 20 athletes, 15 passed, so the proportion who passed among the athletes is $15/20 = 0.75$. Margins are counts, so they add to the grand total, not to 100.
- **Distributions in two-way tables come in three kinds, and only the denominator differs.** Joint: cell ÷ grand total, $15/100 = 15\%$. Marginal: margin ÷ grand total. Conditional: cell ÷ that group's own total. All three are honest readings of the same cell and they answer different questions — 15% of all students are athletes who passed, 75% of athletes passed, and $15/55 \approx 27\%$ of those who passed were athletes. Choosing the wrong denominator is the single most common error in this topic.
- **A conditional distribution can be computed by row or by column, and the two are different numbers.** By row means dividing each cell by its row total, so it reports percentages *within each row* and every row sums to 100%; by column means dividing by column totals, and then every column sums to 100%. To get the conditional distribution of the outcome given a particular group, the quantity you need is that group's total — not the grand total, not a mean, not a range. Worked example: among 20 boys, 8 prefer soccer, so within the boys' row the conditional percentage is $8/20 = 40\%$.
- **When the groups are different sizes, compare conditional distributions rather than raw counts.** A raw cell count mixes the outcome rate together with the size of the group; dividing by the group total takes the size back out. Worked example: 40 non-athletes passed against only 15 athletes, yet the pass rate is $40/80 = 50\%$ for non-athletes and $15/20 = 75\%$ for athletes — the count gap is a size gap. This is not a claim that counts are misleading in general; they are the data, and they are exactly right for "how many people are in this cell". They are simply not comparable across totals that differ.
- **If the conditional distributions are nearly the same across the categories of the other variable, that suggests little or no association.** Independence says $P(\text{outcome} \mid \text{group}) = P(\text{outcome})$ for every group, so under independence each row-conditional distribution equals the column marginal, and the counts satisfy $n_{ij} = r_i c_j / n$ — the outer product of the margins, a rank-one table. Worked example: 8 of 20 boys and 12 of 30 girls prefer soccer, both exactly 40%, and the expected count $20 \times 20 / 50 = 8$ hits the observed cell on the nose: no association. The athlete table expects $20 \times 55 / 100 = 11$ against an observed 15, with conditionals of 75% versus 50%: association. A gap between conditionals is evidence of a relationship and nothing more — no percentage in a two-way table can establish that one variable causes the other.

**Rules to remember**

- Categorical values are labels; the only summaries they support are counts and proportions.
- One categorical variable → frequency table plus bar chart. Histogram, box plot and scatterplot all require a quantitative axis.
- Relative frequency $=$ count ÷ total. The percentages of one variable total 100%, whatever the sample size and however many categories there are.
- Grand total $=$ the sum of every cell $=$ the sum of the row totals $=$ the sum of the column totals.
- Joint ÷ grand total; marginal ÷ grand total; conditional ÷ the group's own total.
- "By row" means percentages within each row, and each row sums to 100%. "By column" is a different distribution of the same table.
- Groups of different sizes are compared by conditional distribution, because it divides the size back out.
- Nearly identical conditional distributions ⇒ little or no association. Clearly different ones ⇒ association — never causation.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Doing arithmetic on coded labels.** Averaging region codes produces a number that changes when you renumber them.
- **Reading a joint percentage when the question asked for a conditional one.** $15/100$ and $15/20$ describe the same cell and answer different questions.
- **Dividing by the grand total to get a conditional distribution.** The group's own total is the denominator; the grand total, the mean of the cells and the range of the data are all the wrong quantity.
- **Swapping the row and column conditionals.** "What percent of athletes passed" is $15/20$; "what percent of the passers were athletes" is $15/55$.
- **Comparing raw counts across groups of unequal size.** $40 > 15$ while $50\% < 75\%$ — both statements are true about the same table.
- **Overcorrecting into "raw counts are inherently misleading".** They are not. Percentages are for comparability, not a replacement for the counts.
- **Expecting the margins to add to exactly 100.** They are counts and add to the grand total; only distributions add to 100%.
- **Upgrading an association to a cause.** Margins and conditional distributions describe a relationship; they never identify its direction or its mechanism.
- **Reaching for "nonlinear" or "correlation" language.** Categorical variables have no shape to be linear or nonlinear about — the only question available is whether the conditional distributions differ.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Count the variables first. One variable → frequency, relative frequency, bar chart, percentages totalling 100%. Two variables → a two-way table and its three distributions.
2. Before computing any percentage, say the denominator out loud. Most distractors in this topic are the right numerator over the wrong total.
3. When a question names a group — "among the 20 athletes", "within the boys' row" — it is asking for a conditional distribution and has already handed you the denominator.
4. On a display question, ask what the horizontal axis would be made of. Labels ⇒ bar chart; anything requiring bins, quartiles or coordinates is a quantitative display and does not apply.
5. On an association question, compute both conditional distributions and compare them. Nearly equal ⇒ little or no association; clearly different ⇒ association. Never compare the raw cell counts.
6. Distrust options promising more than counting supports: margins adding to exactly 100, percentages establishing causation, a categorical relationship being "perfectly correlated".
