1. Table You Already Know

You have read this table hundreds of times under another name: the confusion matrix. It is a two-way table of two categorical variables — what a message actually is, and what the filter called it. Recall reads along a row; precision reads down a column. Drag the class balance and watch which one moves. Like a scoreboard you can read across or down: same numbers, two different stories.

The filter never changes across this slider — it always catches 90% of spam and wrongly flags 10% of the rest. Everything that moves, moves because the margins moved. That is the whole lesson in one picture: a two-way table holds several different percentages of the same four counts, and which one you get is decided entirely by the total you divide by.

2. One Variable, Counted

One categorical variable, and only two operations are defined on it: count, then divide by the total. Move the counts and watch the relative frequencies. Then press reorder — the picture is just as correct in any order, which is exactly what "categorical" means. Like a jar of coloured marbles: you can count them, but you cannot average their colours.

Notice what is not on offer. A histogram needs bin boundaries, a box plot needs quartiles, a scatterplot needs two numeric coordinates — and none of the three is definable when the horizontal axis is made of names. The bar chart is not merely the conventional choice for one categorical variable; it is the only one of the four that is even well defined.

3. Margins And Grand Total

Build a two-way table cell by cell. The margins are sums of cells, the grand total is the sum of every cell, and the two routes to it must agree. Watch the conditional proportion underneath rebuild itself from whichever row total you just changed. Like a receipt: the line items have to reach the same total whichever way you add them up.

The margins are counts, so they add to the grand total — never to 100. Only after you divide does anything add to 100%. Keep that separation and a whole family of distractors dissolves: the grand total is not a row total, not a column total, and never the difference of two margins.

4. Three Ways To Divide

One frozen table, four readings. Switch the denominator and watch the same cell report 15%, 75% or 27% — every one of them true, every one of them answering a different question. Tap a cell to see all of its readings side by side. Like a slice of cake: a share of the whole cake, of one layer, or of one plate — same slice, three fractions.

The marginal distribution is the fourth member of this family and it is hiding in plain sight: divide a margin by the grand total (20/100 and 80/100) and you have recovered the one-variable frequency table you would have built had you never recorded the exam result at all. Joint, marginal and conditional are one table under three denominators.

5. Spotting Association

Two hundred people, two groups, one yes/no outcome. Slide the group sizes apart and the raw counts go wild while nothing about the groups changes. Slide the gap and the conditional distributions separate. Only the second one is association. Like two classrooms of different sizes: the bigger one always has more hands up, which tells you nothing until you divide.

Expected count is the whole idea in one formula: under independence a cell should hold (row total × column total) ÷ grand total, because the joint table is then just the outer product of its own margins — a rank-one matrix. Observed close to expected in every cell is what "little or no association" looks like arithmetically. And an association, however large, still says nothing about which variable drives the other, or whether anything does.
