1. Labels Or Ruler

Analyzing one categorical variable and Two-way tables put categories on an axis, where order was arbitrary and distance meant nothing. Quantitative values will not sit still for that. Drag one page-load time away from the pack and watch which axis notices. Like seat numbers versus a tape measure: both are in order, but only one tells you how far apart things are.

read as labels — equal slots, text order
read as numbers — position is the value

This is the whole reason a new family of displays exists. A bar chart, a pie chart and a two-way table all take a categorical axis; dot plots, stem-and-leaf plots, histograms and box plots all take a numerical one. Pick from the variable type first and the rest of the lesson is bookkeeping.

2. Binning A Histogram

Each bar of a histogram is one numerical interval and its frequency — never a category, never a single value. Drag the bin width and watch the same 20 load times tell three different stories. Tap any bar to read its interval. Like the zoom on a map: too close and it is all street names, too far and the whole city is one dot.

The bin width is a decision you make, and the reader of your chart cannot see it. That is the honesty problem with histograms: two analysts can hand the same data to the same audience and show a smooth single peak or a jagged three-peaked mess. Say what width you used, and check the shape survives a couple of nearby widths before you believe it.

3. Same Box, Different Data

Four displays, one data set — then swap the data set. Three of the four displays change completely. The box plot does not move a pixel, because it only ever saw five numbers. Like a passport: name, height, date of birth — enough to identify you, nowhere near enough to describe you.

The judgment: the five-number summary is a lossy summary, and the loss is not random. It is blind to modality, to gaps, and to everything inside the box — exactly the features that usually mean two populations got mixed together. Reach for the box plot to compare centre, spread and outliers across groups; reach for a dot plot, a stem-and-leaf plot or a histogram before you believe a distribution has one peak.

4. Naming The Shape

Shape is two questions: how many peaks, and which way does the tail run. Choose a modality, then drag the tail. Every word in the verdict is read back off the bars and the numbers — never off the buttons you pressed. Like describing a hill: first how many summits, then which side is the long slow slope down.

Two habits worth keeping. Name the skew after the tail, never after the bulk — "most values are small" describes a right-skewed distribution. And when the mean and the median pull apart, that gap is itself a measurement: it is the distribution telling you it is not symmetric, and it is why reporting an average alone on skewed data quietly misleads.

5. Two Groups Compared

Comparing distributions means one shared axis and four sentences: shape, center, spread, outliers. Shift group B's whole distribution, then drop one straggler into it and watch which statistics move and which refuse to. Like a class photo: one tall student changes the tallest person in the room and nothing about the typical one.

Both rows are drawn against the same horizontal axis — that is not decoration, it is the whole validity of the comparison. Two box plots or two histograms on different scales can be individually correct and jointly show the opposite of the truth, and it is the single easiest way to mislead with a chart that contains no false numbers at all.
