**The big idea**: **Displaying quantitative data with graphs** and **More on data displays** taught you to *see* a distribution, and **Describing and comparing distributions** taught you to say what you saw — shape, centre, spread, outliers — in words; **Analyzing one categorical variable**, **Two-way tables** and **Distributions in two-way tables** did that same job for counts and proportions. This lesson turns the words into numbers. Every statistic below is a deliberate act of information destruction: you hold $n$ values and you keep one, so the only question worth asking is *which* information you agreed to lose. Two axes organise all nine Khan Academy topics here — centre and spread — and each axis carries a squared-error member (mean, variance, standard deviation) and an order-based member (median, IQR, range). Covariance and correlation then run the identical machinery on two variables at once.

**Key concepts**

- **Measuring center in quantitative data: the mean balances, the median counts.** The mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$ is the point where signed deviations cancel — $\sum (x_i - \bar{x}) = \sum x_i - n\bar{x} = 0$ by construction — so a weightless ruler carrying equal weights at each value balances exactly there. That is why the mean is *the balance point*, not the middle value. The median is positional: sort, then take the middle entry (odd $n$) or the average of the two middle entries (even $n$). For $1, 3, 8, 10, 12$ the median is the third of five, $\mathbf{8}$ — not the midrange $6.5$, not the mean $6.8$. For $2, 5, 7, 9$ it is $(5+7)/2 = 6$. Mean of $2, 4, 6$ is $12/3 = 4$; mean of $1, 1, 4, 8$ is $14/4 = 3.5$. **Mean and median are the two standard measures of centre**; the mode answers a different question and the range is not a centre at all.
- **More on mean and median: resistance is a loss function in disguise.** The mean is the $c$ minimising $\sum (x_i - c)^2$; the median is the $c$ minimising $\sum |x_i - c|$ — squared error against absolute error, the same MSE-versus-MAE choice you already make when fitting a model. A squared penalty is unbounded, so one distant point can buy an arbitrarily large shift; an absolute penalty is linear, so sliding a point that is already on one side of the middle changes nothing. Take $5, 6, 7, 100$: median $6.5$, mean $29.5$. **Adding one unusually large value pulls the mean upward** (never down) while the median holds. Push the $100$ to $1000$: mean $254.5$, median still $6.5$. This is why strongly right-skewed quantities — income, latency, session length — are reported by their median.
- **Interquartile range (IQR): the middle half, measured.** $Q_1$ is the median of the lower half of the sorted data and $Q_3$ the median of the upper half, so $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$. With $Q_1 = 10$ and $Q_3 = 18$ the IQR is $8$. Given median $20$, $Q_1 = 15$, $Q_3 = 27$, the middle 50% of the data lies between **15 and 27** — an interval claim; the median is not an endpoint of it. A larger IQR means more variability inside that middle half and nothing about how high the centre sits. Resistance comes free: $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ are order statistics, so any value beyond them can run off to infinity without moving them at all, which is exactly why the IQR is less affected by extremes than the range.
- **Box and whisker plots are the five-number summary drawn to scale.** The five numbers are minimum, $Q_1$, median, $Q_3$, maximum. The box runs from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$ — the middle 50% — so **the width of the box is the IQR** ($Q_1 = 12$, $Q_3 = 20$ gives a box of width $8$), and the line inside the box marks the *median*, never the mean. The whiskers reach the extremes (or the furthest point within $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ of the box, outliers drawn separately). Whisker length is where tail information survives, so **a much longer right whisker means a right-skewed distribution** — the reading you gave a histogram, now recovered from five numbers instead of fifty bins.
- **Variance and standard deviation of a population: square first, then undo the squaring.** Because $\sum (x_i - \mu) = 0$ identically, the average raw deviation is zero for every data set — deviations must stop cancelling, and squaring is how. That gives $\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum (x_i - \mu)^2$. For the population $2, 4, 6$: $\mu = 4$, deviations $-2, 0, 2$, squares $4, 0, 4$, so $\sigma^2 = 8/3$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{8/3} \approx 1.633$. Variance lives in squared units (ms², dollars²) and cannot be compared with the data; the square root restores the scale, so **the standard deviation is the spread measure expressed in the original units**, readable as the typical distance of a value from the mean.
- **Variance and standard deviation of a sample: $n-1$ is a bias correction, not a convention.** The sample mean is fitted to the very data you are measuring, so it sits closer to those points than the unknown $\mu$ does: $\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \le \sum (x_i - \mu)^2$, always. Taking expectations gives $E\!\left[\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2\right] = (n-1)\sigma^2$, so dividing by $n$ underestimates by the factor $(n-1)/n$ at *every* sample size, and dividing by $n-1$ makes $s^2$ unbiased. One degree of freedom was spent estimating $\bar{x}$. For the sample $1, 3, 5$: $\bar{x} = 3$, squared deviations $4, 0, 4$ summing to $8$, so $s^2 = 8/2 = 4$ and $s = 2$. Compare that with the population $2, 4, 6$ above: identical spacing, identical sum of squares, and the entire difference between $8/3$ and $4$ is the divisor. (Note $s^2$ is unbiased; $s$ itself is biased slightly low — concavity — and nobody corrects it.)
- **More on standard deviation: shifts do nothing, scaling multiplies.** In one line, $(ax_i + b) - (a\bar{x} + b) = a(x_i - \bar{x})$, so $\text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\text{Var}(X)$ and $\text{SD}(aX+b) = |a|\,\text{SD}(X)$. **Add $5$ to every value and the standard deviation is unchanged; multiply every value by $3$ and the standard deviation is multiplied by $3$** (the variance by $9$). Range, IQR and MAD behave the same way, because all of them are built from differences and a constant cancels in a difference. A small standard deviation means values tightly clustered near the mean, and $s = 0$ occurs exactly when every value is identical: $1, 1, 1$ has $s = 0$, while $0, 1, 0$ does not.
- **Other measures of spread: range and MAD sit at the two ends of resistance.** The range is $\max - \min$; a minimum of $3$ and a maximum of $14$ gives $11$. Built from two numbers only, it is the most outlier-sensitive statistic in the lesson — one bad reading moves it without limit — and two data sets sharing a range agree on nothing except that $\max - \min$ matches. The median absolute deviation, $\operatorname{median}(|x_i - \operatorname{median}(x)|)$, is its fully resistant opposite. Ranked by resistance to a single extreme value: range (none), standard deviation (weak — it squares the offence), IQR (strong), MAD (strongest). IQR and range both measure *spread*; mean and median both measure *centre*.
- **Covariance and Correlation (Pearson and Spearman): centring, applied twice.** $\operatorname{cov}(x,y) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$. Each term is positive when both coordinates land on the same side of their own means, so **a negative covariance says that when one variable rises the other tends to fall**. Its magnitude is meaningless — it carries the units of $x$ times the units of $y$, so switching seconds to milliseconds multiplies it by 1000. Pearson's $r = \operatorname{cov}(x,y)/(s_x s_y)$ divides those units out; recall from linear algebra that this is the cosine of the angle between the two mean-centred vectors, so Cauchy–Schwarz hands you $-1 \le r \le 1$ for free. It is *not* covariance divided by the sample size. **$r$ measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship only**: for $y = x^2$ over symmetric $x$, $r = 0$ while the relationship is perfect. Spearman's $\rho$ is Pearson's $r$ computed on ranks, so it detects any monotone relationship and inherits the resistance of order statistics. And the standing warning: **correlation does not imply causation**.

**Rules to remember**

- Mean = balance point (deviations cancel). Median = middle address (position only).
- Right-skewed or outlier-carrying data: report the median. Symmetric data: mean and median agree, so use the mean.
- $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$, the width of the box, the spread of the middle 50%.
- Five-number summary: minimum, $Q_1$, median, $Q_3$, maximum. The line in the box is the median.
- Longer whisker = longer tail on that side = skew in that direction.
- Deviations are squared so they cannot cancel; the square root exists to restore the units.
- Population divides by $N$; a sample divides by $n-1$ because $\bar{x}$ was estimated from the same data.
- $+b$ leaves every spread measure alone; $\times a$ multiplies every spread measure by $|a|$ and the variance by $a^2$.
- $r$ is unitless and linear-only; covariance keeps units and its size means nothing on its own.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Calling the mean "the middle value".** It is the balance point; the middle value is the median, and on skewed data they can be far apart.
- **Reading the median as an outlier fix.** It is resistant, not corrective — the outlier is still in your data, and it may be the most important row there.
- **Using variance where the units matter.** Variance is in squared units, so it can never be "the typical distance from the mean"; only the standard deviation can.
- **Quoting a range as evidence of similarity.** Equal ranges are compatible with wildly different medians, means and IQRs.
- **Assuming the box plot's centre line is the mean.** It is the median, so a box plot never shows you the mean at all.
- **Dividing by $n$ on a sample.** A silent default, not an error: NumPy's `np.std` uses `ddof=0`, pandas' `.std()` uses `ddof=1`, so one column yields two answers.
- **Reading $r \approx 0$ as "no relationship".** It means no *linear* relationship; plot the data, and reach for Spearman when the pattern is monotone but bent.
- **Treating a strong $r$ as a causal claim,** or comparing raw covariances across variables measured in different units.

**How to approach the questions**

1. First classify the statistic: centre or spread? That split kills most distractors: the wrong-axis option — a mean offered as a spread, a range as a centre — is the standard trap.
2. On small numeric sets, sort before you do anything. Median, quartiles, IQR and range are defined on the sorted order; a mis-sorted list is the commonest arithmetic loss.
3. For any sample-versus-population wording, find the divisor. "Sample" plus "$n-1$" in the stem means the answer uses $n-1$; the same sum of squares over $n$ is the distractor beside it.
4. For transformation questions, separate the shift from the scale. Adding leaves every spread untouched; multiplying scales the standard deviation by the factor and the variance by its square.
5. For resistance questions, imagine dragging one value to infinity and ask which statistic follows it. Range and mean follow; median, IQR and MAD do not.
6. Beware absolute words. Correct answers here say "less affected than", "tends to", "typical"; "always", "exactly equal to" and "identical to the population" are the wrong option.
