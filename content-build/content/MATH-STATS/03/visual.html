1. Balance Point Drag

Displaying quantitative data with graphs and Describing and comparing distributions taught you to look at a dot plot and say "skewed right, one outlier". Here is the same picture with the two numbers attached. Drag the fourth value and watch which summary follows it. Like a seesaw with three children on one end: the mean is where the plank balances, the median is just whoever is sitting in the middle.

The mechanism is a loss function you already use. The mean minimises squared error sum (x - c)^2 and the median minimises absolute error sum |x - c| — MSE against MAE. A squared penalty is unbounded, so one far value buys an arbitrarily large shift; an absolute penalty is linear, so sliding a point that is already past the middle changes nothing at all. Resistance is not a property of the median so much as a property of the norm you chose to minimise.

2. Five Number Box

Minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum — five numbers, drawn to scale. Pick a starting shape, then push the largest value out and watch which parts of the picture move and which refuse to. Like a train: the carriage holds the middle half of the passengers, and only the couplings stretch.

Read the whiskers, not the box, for shape: the box is deliberately blind to the tails, which is exactly what makes it resistant. And note what the plot cannot show you — the mean is never drawn on a box plot, so two data sets with identical five-number summaries can have very different means.

3. Squares Not Signs

Spread means "how far from the centre", but distances have signs and signs cancel — by construction, not by accident. Switch what you total and drag the third value to see which candidate survives. Like measuring how far a class wandered from the teacher: walking left and walking right both count as wandering.

Squaring is not the only way to kill a sign — absolute values work too, and the median absolute deviation in visual 6 is built that way. Squares win because they are differentiable everywhere and add cleanly over independent parts, which is why variance and not mean absolute deviation sits underneath every downstream result you know. Try x = 0 and then x = 6: different data, identical spread.

4. Why n Minus One

Take a population whose variance you actually know, enumerate every sample of size n, and average the two candidate formulas over all of them. No simulation and no luck — these are exact expected values. Like measuring a room with a tape you cut to fit that room: it always comes out a little short.

The correction is for estimating the centre, not for "being a sample". Switch the centring button and the winner switches with it: knowing mu really does buy you back that degree of freedom. And note what is not fixed — s^2 is unbiased, but s is the square root of an unbiased quantity, which makes it slightly biased low. Nobody corrects that in practice, and now you know why it is not a contradiction.

5. Tilt The Cloud

Covariance centres two variables at once and multiplies; correlation divides the units back out. Tilt the cloud, bend it, and change the units of y, then watch which of the three numbers actually mean anything. Like asking whether two dancers move together: the answer must not change when you measure their steps in centimetres instead of inches.

And the warning that outranks all of the arithmetic: correlation does not imply causation. A confounder driving both variables, causation running the other way, or a filter that selected the rows you are looking at will each manufacture a strong r out of nothing. Correlation is a description of this sample's geometry, never a claim about what would happen if you intervened.

6. Spread Under Stress

Five measures of spread on one data set of response times. Drag the slowest request out to disaster, then shift and scale the whole set, and watch which numbers follow and which refuse. Like a room full of people and one basketball player: the average height moves, the person standing in the middle does not.

Two consequences worth carrying out of here. The range is computed from the maximum and the minimum and nothing else, which makes it the most outlier-sensitive statistic on the list — and it also means two data sets sharing a range agree on nothing except that max minus min matches. And variance is the one row measured in ms², so it can be compared with another variance and never with the data itself; that is the job the square root does.
