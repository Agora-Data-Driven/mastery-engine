**The big idea**: **Displaying quantitative data with graphs** gave you histograms and dot plots, **Describing and comparing distributions** taught you to read shape, centre and spread off one, **Measuring center in quantitative data** located the middle, and **Variance and standard deviation of a population** together with **Interquartile range (IQR)** and **Box and whisker plots** measured the scatter. All of them describe a sample you actually counted. This lesson swaps the bars for a *model*: a smooth curve whose **area** is proportion, plus one curve — the normal — regular enough that a single ruler makes any two measurements comparable. The seven topics under this lesson in your progress tree (each tagged `khanacademy.org` there) — Density curves, Normal distributions and the empirical rule, Percentiles, Z-scores, Normal distribution calculations, More on normal distributions, and Effects of linear transformations — are one chain of reasoning: idealise the histogram, name the shape, locate a value by rank, locate it by distance, compute with it, and know exactly what survives when the data is rescaled.

**Key concepts**

- **A density curve is a relative-frequency histogram with the bin width taken to zero.** Draw the histogram so each bar's *area* — not its height — is the proportion of data in that bin, then keep halving the bin width. The bars converge to a smooth curve, and exactly two properties survive that limit and define it: the curve is never below the horizontal axis (a negative height would be a negative proportion), and the total area under it is exactly 1, because the whole population is 100%. Nothing else is required. It need not be symmetric or bell-shaped, and it may lie flat *on* the axis: the uniform density on $[2, 7]$ has height $0.2$ inside and exactly 0 outside, and its area is still $0.2 \times 5 = 1$.
- **Area is the probability; height is not.** For a continuous variable $X$, $P(a \le X \le b)$ is the area under the curve between $a$ and $b$ — which is why the total being 1 is not a convention but the whole point. If the area from 2 to 5 equals 0.3, that says $P(2 \le X \le 5) = 0.3$: 30% of the population lies in that window. It does not say the curve is 0.3 tall there, that the mean is 0.3, or that exactly 30 data points landed there. Heights are unbounded — the uniform density on $[0, 0.5]$ is 2 tall — only the area is capped.
- **The normal distribution is one specific density curve: symmetric, single-peaked and bell-shaped.** Because it is symmetric about its centre, the balance point (mean), the half-way rank (median) and the peak (mode) coincide — in real, approximately normal data they come out approximately equal. Write it Normal(mean, standard deviation), the convention these questions use, so $X \sim \text{Normal}(50, 4)$ has mean 50 and standard deviation 4; its variance is 16. The rival notation $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ puts the variance in that second slot, so check which one is in play.
- **Two dials, doing different jobs.** Raising the mean slides the whole curve along the axis and changes nothing else — same shape, same spread, same total area. The standard deviation is what changes shape: the peak of a normal density is $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$, so moving $\sigma$ from 2 to 10 drops the peak from about 0.199 to about 0.040. **Larger standard deviation means wider and flatter**, and the two go together precisely because the area is pinned at 1 and has to go somewhere.
- **The empirical rule compresses that shape into three numbers: 68–95–99.7.** About 68% of values lie within 1 standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within 2, about 99.7% within 3. Worked: for Normal(100, 15), $[85, 115]$ holds about 68%, $[70, 130]$ about 95%, and $[55, 145]$ about 99.7%. The tails follow by symmetry: the area above $z = 1$ is $(100 - 68)/2 = 16\%$, and above $z = 2$ is $(100 - 95)/2 = 2.5\%$. So a value more than 2 standard deviations above the mean is **somewhat unusual** — roughly 1 in 40 — and not impossible.
- **Those percentages are a property of the normal shape, not of data in general.** A uniform distribution keeps only about 57.7% within 1 standard deviation and a full 100% within 2; strongly skewed or clearly bimodal distributions miss in either direction. The rule is a shortcut for approximately normal distributions and a source of confident wrong answers everywhere else.
- **A percentile locates a value by rank.** The $p$-th percentile is the value at or below which about $p\%$ of the data lie, which makes the quartiles you already know percentiles in disguise: **Q1 is the 25th percentile, the median is the 50th, and Q3 is the 75th**, so the IQR is the middle 50% by definition. Worked: among 100 sorted test scores the 30th smallest sits at the 30th percentile — 30 scores are at or below it, so it beats about 30% of the field, not 70%. A score at the 90th percentile has about 90% of values at or below it, which says nothing about its raw size.
- **A z-score locates the same value by distance instead, measured in standard deviations: $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$.** Subtract the mean to re-centre, divide by the standard deviation to change the unit. Worked: $x = 80$ drawn from mean 70 and standard deviation 5 gives $z = 10/5 = 2$, two standard deviations above the mean; $x = 85$ gives $z = 15/5 = 3$. The sign carries the side — $z = -1.5$ is 1.5 standard deviations *below* the mean, not 1.5 raw units below zero — and the magnitude carries the surprise: among $z = 0.2$, $-0.5$, $1.0$ and $2.7$, the most unusual is $2.7$, because $|z|$ is largest. A positive z-score means only that the value is above the mean, never that it is automatically an outlier.
- **Normal distribution calculations are two moves: standardise, then look up.** Tables (and `scipy.stats.norm.cdf`) are published for one curve only — the standard normal, mean 0 and standard deviation 1 — so raw values are converted to z-scores first, and the table returns the area to the left, which is exactly the percentile. It runs backwards too: in Normal(100, 15) the value exactly 1 standard deviation below the mean is $100 - 15 = 85$. Forwards, $z = 1$ leaves about 16% of the area above it by the empirical rule, and 15.87% by the table.
- **A linear transformation $y = ax + b$ moves centre and spread by different rules — derive it once and you never memorise it.** Centre: $\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum(ax_i + b) = a\bar{x} + b$, so adding 7 to every value adds 7 to the mean, and $y = 2x + 1$ gives a new mean of $2(\text{old mean}) + 1$. The median and every percentile move by the same formula, because for $a > 0$ the transformation preserves order. Spread: the deviations become $y_i - \bar{y} = a(x_i - \bar{x})$, so the standard deviation is multiplied by $|a|$ and the variance by $a^2$ — multiply every value by 4 and the standard deviation is multiplied by 4, not 16.
- **The effects of the two halves are asymmetric, and that is the examinable part.** Adding $b$ has no effect on spread: a rigid shift changes no distance between two points, so the histogram is picked up and set down elsewhere with an identical shape. Add 3 to every value and the median rises by 3 while the standard deviation, IQR and shape are untouched.
- **Standardising is itself a linear transformation, which is why z-scores behave.** Take $a = 1/\sigma$ and $b = -\mu/\sigma$: the new mean is $\mu/\sigma - \mu/\sigma = 0$ and the new standard deviation is $|1/\sigma| \cdot \sigma = 1$. Since a linear map leaves shape alone, a skewed distribution stays exactly as skewed after standardising. Z-scores make values comparable across units; they do not make data normal, and converting a z-score into a percentile is only legitimate when the distribution was approximately normal to begin with.

**Rules to remember**

- A density curve satisfies exactly two conditions: never below the axis, and total area exactly 1 — never more, never less. Symmetry, bell shape and "never touches the axis" are not required.
- Probability is area, never height. The area over an interval is $P(a \le X \le b)$.
- 68–95–99.7 within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations, for approximately normal distributions only.
- One-sided tails: subtract the two-sided percentage from 100 and halve. Above $z = 1$ is 16%; above $z = 2$ is 2.5%.
- Q1 = 25th percentile, median = 50th, Q3 = 75th. A percentile counts values *at or below*.
- $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$. Sign is the side, magnitude is the surprise.
- Standardise before any table lookup; the table describes mean 0 and standard deviation 1 only.
- Normal(mean, standard deviation): here the second slot is the standard deviation, not the variance.
- The mean shifts the curve, the standard deviation shapes it, and the area stays 1 either way.
- For $y = ax + b$: mean, median and percentiles become $a(\cdot) + b$; standard deviation, IQR and range are multiplied by $|a|$; variance by $a^2$; $b$ alone changes no spread and no shape.

**Common pitfalls**

- Reading the height of a density curve as a probability. Densities can be taller than 1; only the area is capped at 1.
- Assuming every density curve is bell-shaped or symmetric, or that it can never touch the axis. All three are false.
- Quoting the empirical rule for skewed, bimodal or uniform data, where it is simply the wrong model.
- Treating "more than 2 standard deviations above the mean" as impossible rather than somewhat unusual — about 1 in 40.
- Flipping a percentile. The 30th percentile beats about 30% of the field, not 70%.
- Losing the sign of a z-score, or reading $z = -1.5$ as 1.5 raw units below zero.
- Scaling the standard deviation by $a^2$ (that is the variance) or adding $b$ to it (nothing happens).
- Believing that standardising makes a distribution normal. It sets mean 0 and standard deviation 1; the shape is unchanged.
- Blaming a wider, flatter curve on a larger mean or a smaller sample size. Width is the standard deviation.

**How to approach the questions**

1. When asked what *must* be true of every density curve, only two answers ever qualify: it stays on or above the x-axis, and its total area is exactly 1.
2. Convert to a z-score before anything else. Most normal-model questions are one subtraction, one division, then a symmetry argument.
3. For a tail percentage, take the two-sided empirical number, subtract it from 100 and halve — 68 gives 16 per side, 95 gives 2.5.
4. For percentile wording, force the phrase "at or below". If an option quotes the complement, that is the intended trap.
5. For transformation questions, ask separately what $+b$ does and what $\times a$ does. Centres take both; spreads take only $|a|$; shape takes neither unless $a$ is negative.
6. When a question compares z-scores for unusualness, rank by $|z|$ and ignore the sign entirely.
