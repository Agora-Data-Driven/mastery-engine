1. Histogram Into Curve

Displaying quantitative data with graphs gave you the histogram and Describing and comparing distributions taught you to read shape off one. Shrink the bins and the bars become a density curve: area is proportion, the total is pinned at 1, and nothing ever dips below the axis. Like a mosaic you keep stepping back from until the tiles vanish and only the picture is left.

Only two things are required of a density curve, and every shape above obeys both: it never drops below the axis, and the total area is exactly 1. Symmetry, a bell shape and "it never touches the axis" are not on the list — the uniform block is exactly 0 outside its own range and is a perfectly legal density.

2. Empirical Rule Bands

68 - 95 - 99.7 is not a fact about data. It is a fact about one shape. Pick a shape, then slide the band width in standard deviations and watch the real percentages agree — or refuse to. Like a shoe size that fits everyone in one family and nobody in the next.

Read the tail column, not just the middle. "More than 2 standard deviations above the mean" is about 2.5% of a normal distribution — roughly 1 observation in 40. That is somewhat unusual, which is a long way from impossible, and it is the reading the questions want.

3. The Percentile Ladder

100 exam scores, all different, sorted. Slide to any one of them and the percentile is just a count: how many sit at or below. The quartiles from Box and whisker plots turn out to be three particular rungs on this ladder. Like a queue where your position, not your height, is the answer.

A percentile is a statement about rank and says nothing about raw size. The 90th percentile of one exam and the 90th of another can be 300 points apart. That is exactly the gap visual 4 closes: a z-score measures the same value in distance instead, and for an approximately normal distribution the two are convertible.

4. Standardize Any Value

One picture, two rulers. The top axis is the raw scale you measured in; the bottom is the standard scale, mean 0 and SD 1, that every normal table is printed for. Move x, the mean or the SD and watch the value slide between them. Like reading the same thermometer in Celsius and in Fahrenheit.

The percentile printed here assumes the distribution really is approximately normal. Standardizing is a linear transformation and linear transformations never change shape, so a skewed variable stays skewed after standardizing — its z-score is still meaningful as a distance, but reading it off a normal table is not.

5. Two Dials, One Bell

A normal distribution has exactly two parameters and they do completely different jobs. The dashed curve is a fixed reference, Normal(50, 4). Move the mean and only the position changes; move the standard deviation and the shape does. Like a tent: carry it somewhere else and it is the same tent; widen the base and the roof has to come down.

Notation matters and the two conventions disagree. Written Normal(mean, standard deviation) — the form these questions use — Normal(50, 4) has SD 4 and variance 16. Written N(mu, sigma^2) the second slot is the variance instead, so the same curve is N(50, 16). Check which one a source is using before you trust the number.

6. Shift Then Stretch

Nine values, one transformation y = a·x + b. Every statistic you learned in Measuring center in quantitative data and Variance and standard deviation of a population reacts to a and b differently — and the panel checks the rule against the recomputed data every time. Like moving a ruler versus rescaling it: sliding it changes every reading, stretching it changes every gap.

This is the machinery behind visual 4. Standardizing is the linear transformation with a = 1 / SD and b = -mean / SD: the new mean is mean/SD - mean/SD = 0 and the new standard deviation is (1/SD) × SD = 1. Shape is untouched, which is exactly why a z-score is a distance and not a promise of normality.
