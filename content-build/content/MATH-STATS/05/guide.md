**The big idea**: **Two-way tables** and **Distributions in two-way tables** taught you bivariate thinking for two *categorical* variables — cross-tabulate the counts, then compare conditional distributions. This lesson asks the same question, do two variables move together, for two *quantitative* variables, where binning into a table would throw the numbers away. Most of the machinery is already yours: **Covariance and Correlation (Pearson and Spearman)** introduced $r$, **Z-scores** supplied the standardisation that makes $r$ unitless, **Variance and standard deviation of a sample** supplied $s_x$ and $s_y$, and **Effects of linear transformations** already explains why changing units moves a slope but leaves $r$ alone. The six units here — **Introduction to scatterplots**, **Introduction to trend lines**, **Correlation coefficients**, **Least-squares regression equations**, **Assessing the fit in least-squares regression**, and **More on regression** (khanacademy.org) — assemble those pieces into one pipeline: plot the pairs, describe the pattern in words, quantify it with $r$, fit the line that minimises the sum of squared residuals, then audit that line with residuals and $r^2$. Every number downstream inherits one caveat: these are summaries of *linear* pattern in *observed* data. They predict; they never by themselves establish that $x$ causes $y$.

**Key concepts**

- **A scatterplot displays the relationship between two quantitative variables, and each plotted point is a single paired observation $(x, y)$.** The pairing is the mechanism: both numbers are measured on the *same* individual, so one dot at $(3200, 24)$ says "this car weighs 3,200 lb and does 24 mpg". Break the pairing and the plot means nothing. Twenty cars give twenty points, never forty — which is why a scatterplot is not one variable tracked over time, not a category with its count, and not a display of a centre or a distribution's shape.
- **Describe any scatterplot with four words: form, direction, strength, outliers.** Form is linear or curved. Direction: points that rise from left to right are a **positive** association, points that fall are **negative**, and a cloud with no clear upward or downward pattern shows **little or no association**. Strength is how tightly the points hug that pattern. An **outlier** is one point lying far from the general pattern of the rest — not the same as a **cluster**, which is a group of points sitting together apart from the others. With $(1,2),(2,3.9),(3,6.1),(4,8)$ the direction is positive and tight; adding $(3.5,-4)$ adds an outlier, not a cluster.
- **A trend line summarises the general direction of the points; it does not join them.** A good one follows the pattern with roughly as much data above it as below, so the positive and negative gaps balance out. Three false requirements to discard: it need not pass exactly through each plotted point (impossible unless the points are collinear), it need not begin at the origin $(0,0)$, and it is not horizontal by default — a flat line asserts that $y$ does not change with $x$, which is a finding, not a formatting default. A trend line sloping upward from left to right means the association is positive.
- **Interpolation is supported by data in a way extrapolation is not.** Estimating $y$ at an $x$ *inside* the observed range is **interpolation**; going outside it is **extrapolation**. The line is evidence only where there are points, and nothing in the fit knows whether the pattern continues beyond them. With study hours observed from 1 to 6 and $\hat{y} = 50 + 7x$, predicting at $x = 4$ is fine; at $x = 20$ the equation happily returns 190 on a 100-point test. The arithmetic is not wrong — the assumption behind it is.
- **The correlation coefficient $r$ measures the direction and strength of a *linear* relationship, and nothing else.** Mechanism: standardise both variables and average the products of their z-scores, $r = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum z_x z_y$. Points in the upper-right and lower-left quadrants of the mean lines contribute positive products, upper-left and lower-right negative ones. Because z-scores carry no units, $r$ is unitless and always lies between $-1$ and $1$; $|r| = 1$ only when every point sits exactly on a line. So $r = -0.9$ is a strong negative association — stronger than $r = -0.2$, and negative where $r = 0.9$ is positive.
- **$r$ is not the slope, and $r = 0$ does not mean "no relationship".** Re-express $y$ in cents rather than dollars: every $z_y$ is unchanged, so $r$ is unchanged, while the slope is multiplied by 100. The two are linked but distinct, by $b = r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x}$. And $r = 0$ guarantees only that there is no *linear* relationship: the symmetric points $(-2,4),(-1,1),(0,0),(1,1),(2,4)$ give $r = 0$ on top of a perfect curved relationship. Nor does $r$ care about signs in the data — it is defined for negative values just as happily.
- **The least-squares regression line is defined by what it minimises: the sum of the squared residuals.** Not the sum of the residuals, which any balanced line drives to zero; not the correlation, which the data already fixed. Squaring punishes big misses and makes the criterion differentiable, and setting its derivatives to zero gives $b = r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x}$ and $a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}$ — so the line always passes through the point of averages $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$. With $\bar{x} = 3$, $\bar{y} = 8$, $s_x = 1.58$, $s_y = 4.74$, $r = 0.9$: $b = 0.9 \times 4.74 / 1.58 = 2.7$, and $a = 8 - 2.7 \times 3 = -0.1$.
- **Read $\hat{y} = a + bx$ literally.** The coefficient $b$ is the predicted change in $y$ per 1-unit rise in $x$, so a 2-unit rise moves the prediction by $2b$ — slope 4 predicts $+8$. The coefficient $a$ is the predicted $y$ when $x = 0$, which is only meaningful if $x = 0$ is anywhere near the data. Signs belong to the coefficient: in $\hat{y} = 5 - 0.5x$ the slope is $-0.5$. Prediction is plain substitution: $\hat{y} = 2 + 3x$ at $x = 4$ gives $2 + 12 = 14$.
- **A residual is observed minus predicted, and the order is not negotiable.** Observed $y = 15$ against a predicted 12 is a residual of $+3$ — the point sits *above* the line. Observed 18 against a predicted 20 is $-2$, below. Reversing the subtraction flips every sign and is the most common slip on these questions.
- **Assess the fit on the residual plot, not the scatterplot.** Plot each residual against $x$ and look for structure. Random scatter around zero means the line has taken out the pattern and left only noise, so a linear model is appropriate. A clear curved pattern means the model is wrong in a way no choice of straight line can repair, and a nonlinear model may fit better; a fan of steadily growing spread is a different warning again.
- **$r^2$, the coefficient of determination, is the fraction of the variation in $y$ explained by $x$.** Mechanism: $r^2 = 1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST}$, comparing squared error around the line with squared error around $\bar{y}$. It is not the slope squared, not the mean of the residuals, and not the probability the model is correct. Bigger means a tighter linear fit, so $r^2 = 0.81$ beats $0.35$ and buries $0.05$. Squaring discards the sign: $r = -0.8$ gives $r^2 = 0.64$, exactly as $r = +0.8$ would.
- **One influential outlier can strongly pull the line and change $r$.** Because the criterion squares residuals, a far-off point contributes disproportionately and drags the fitted line toward itself; an extreme $x$ (high leverage) makes it worse still. For $(1,1),(2,2),(3,3),(4,4)$, $r = 1$ and $b = 1$; move the last point to $(4,-6)$ and $r$ falls to about $-0.42$ with the slope turning negative. Refit with and without such a point and report both.

**Rules to remember**

- One point per paired observation; scatterplots are for two quantitative variables.
- Say form, direction, strength, outliers before you compute anything.
- $-1 \le r \le 1$ always. Sign gives direction, magnitude gives strength of the *linear* pattern.
- $r$ is unitless and is not the slope; $b = r\,s_y/s_x$ converts between them.
- $r = 0$ rules out a linear relationship, not every relationship.
- Least squares minimises $\sum(\text{observed} - \text{predicted})^2$ and passes through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.
- Residual $=$ observed $-$ predicted. Positive means the point is above the line.
- $r^2$ is the share of variation in $y$ explained by $x$, and it throws the sign away.
- Interpolate inside the data; treat every extrapolation as an unverifiable assumption.
- No value of $r$ or $r^2$ upgrades association into causation.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Reading a large $r$ as proof that $x$ causes $y$.** Correlation measures linear association; a lurking third variable explains a great many strong correlations.
- **Reporting $r = 0$ as "no relationship".** It is the guarantee of no *linear* relationship, and a perfect parabola satisfies it.
- **Quoting $r$ where the slope is wanted.** They differ by the factor $s_y/s_x$, so they are not even in the same units.
- **Getting the residual backwards.** Predicted minus observed is a distractor on nearly every residual question.
- **Judging fit from the scatterplot alone.** The curvature that a scatterplot hides is exactly what a residual plot exposes.
- **Insisting a trend line hit points or start at the origin.** Both constraints damage a fit that was supposed to balance the points.
- **Extrapolating.** The model returns a number for any $x$ you give it, including impossible ones, and reports no discomfort.
- **Leaving an influential outlier unexamined.** Fit, $r$ and $r^2$ can all be a single point's opinion.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For "what does a scatterplot or a point show", answer with pairing and *two quantitative variables* — distractors offer one variable, a count, or a distribution.
2. For direction, read the tilt: up to the right is positive, down is negative, no tilt is little or no association.
3. For $r$, check the bound and the sign before the magnitude. "Strongest negative" wants the most negative value, not the largest absolute one among positives.
4. For slope questions, carry the sign out of the equation, then multiply by the stated change in $x$.
5. For residuals, write observed $-$ predicted in that order, then read the sign as above or below the line.
6. For $r^2$, square $r$ (dropping the sign) and phrase the answer as the fraction of variation in $y$ explained by $x$.
7. Any option containing "proves", "causes", or "always" is almost certainly the distractor.
