1. Table To Scatterplot

Two-way tables and Distributions in two-way tables handled two categorical variables by counting cells. Two quantitative variables cannot be counted that way without destroying the numbers, so each row becomes one point instead. Choose a pattern, add spread, and read what the cloud actually says — the verdict below is computed from the points on screen, not from the button you pressed. Like swapping a tally of "tall / short" for everyone's real height marked on a wall.

An outlier is one point far from the general pattern of the rest; a cluster is a group of points sitting together away from the others. They look similar and mean different things — one is a suspect observation, the other is a hint that two populations got mixed. Notice also what the two-way table cannot do: once a pair is filed as "high, high", the pair itself is gone, and with it every regression on this page.

2. Trend Line And Prediction

A trend line summarises the direction of the points — it does not join them. Drag the slope and the intercept until the gaps above and below cancel, then push the prediction marker past the edge of the data and watch the label change. Like drawing a line through footprints in sand: fine where the footprints are, a guess the moment they stop.

Balance is necessary and nowhere near sufficient: the flat preset balances perfectly and is still a terrible summary, because it claims y does not change with x. Balancing fixes only the sum of the gaps. Visual 4 picks the one line that fixes the sum of their squares, and there is exactly one of those.

3. Correlation Versus Slope

r is the average product of z-scores, so it carries no units — which is why changing the units of y moves the slope and leaves r untouched. Drag r itself, switch the leftover part of y from noise to a curve, and change the units. Like a photo: rescaling it changes every measurement on it and changes nothing about what it shows.

Two facts that look alike and are not: r is bounded by -1 and 1 because it is an average of z-score products, while a slope is unbounded because it carries the units of y over the units of x. A correlation of -0.9 is stronger than one of +0.2 and points the other way — magnitude is strength, sign is direction, and neither is causation.

4. Minimizing Squared Residuals

"Least squares" is not a slogan, it is the definition: of every line through the point of averages, exactly one makes the total area of these squares as small as it can be. Drag the slope and watch the total. Like tuning a radio by the hiss: you are not aiming at the station, you are minimising the noise, and the station is wherever the noise bottoms out.

Why squares and not plain distances? Squaring makes big misses expensive and the total smooth enough to differentiate, and setting those derivatives to zero is what produces b = r × s_y / s_x and a = y-bar - b × x-bar in closed form. It is also why one far-off point can dominate the whole fit — which is the next visual.

5. Residuals And One Outlier

The scatterplot hides what the residual plot shows. Move the one loose observation and watch the line, r and r-squared follow it; switch the underlying data to a curve and watch the residuals arch even though nothing looks wrong above. Like a spirit level: the wall looks straight until you lay the bubble against it.

Nothing here licenses a causal claim, and a high r-squared least of all: r-squared says how much of the variation in y a straight line in x accounts for in this sample, which is a statement about fit, not about mechanism. Regression earns its keep as prediction when a linear model fits — checked here, on the residual plot — and never as proof.
