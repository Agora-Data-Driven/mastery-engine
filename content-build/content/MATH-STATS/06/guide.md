**The big idea**: every lesson so far handed you a finished table and asked what it says. **Analyzing one categorical variable** gave you frequency tables and bar charts; **Displaying quantitative data with graphs** and **More on data displays** gave you histograms and dot plots; **Two-way tables** and **Distributions in two-way tables** gave you conditional distributions; **Correlation coefficients** and **Least-squares regression equations** gave you $r$ and a fitted line. Study design asks the question that comes *before* all of them and decides what they may mean: where did these rows come from? Two choices do nearly all the work. **How the individuals were selected** decides whether a statistic computed on your sample estimates anything about a population at all. **How the treatment was assigned** decides whether an association you measure may be read as a cause. Get either wrong and the arithmetic downstream is flawless and worthless — an $r$ of $0.86$ from a self-selected sample is a real number about nobody in particular.

**Key concepts**

- **Individuals and variables are the two axes of every data set, and "variable" has an exact meaning.** A **variable** is any characteristic that can take different values across the individuals being studied. Rows are the individuals, columns the variables, a cell one individual's value. Keep it apart from three neighbours: a *constant* does not vary, an *outcome* is one observed result, and a *parameter* describes an entire population, not a column. Worked example: 40 students organized into four columns — preferred school subject, number of siblings, height in centimetres, hours studied per week. Each varies across the 40; "the school they all attend" does not, and is therefore not a variable.
- **Categorical or quantitative is decided by what the values *are*, not by whether they look numeric.** A **categorical (qualitative)** variable places individuals into named groups; a **quantitative** one records a measured or counted numeric amount on which arithmetic means something. The test: does the difference or the average of two values mean anything? Worked example: `number of siblings` takes 0, 1, 2, 3 and a mean of 1.4 siblings is meaningful, so it is quantitative — as are height in centimetres and time spent studying. Preferred school subject, eye colour, a lunch menu selection and a favourite colour sort individuals into named groups, so they are categorical. A numeral is not a promise: a zip code is a name written in digits, and averaging them is arithmetic on labels.
- **The type of the variable picks the display, and the wrong display is not merely ugly — it is undefined.** For a single quantitative variable the general-purpose display is a **histogram**: cut the number line into equal-width bins, let bar height be the count in each, and shape, centre and spread become readable at a glance. A single categorical variable wants a bar chart, or a pie chart of shares. Two categorical variables are organized into a two-way frequency table whose row and column headings are the two variables' categories. Worked example: 40 study times from 0.4 to 11.4 hours, binned at width 2, give counts 8, 8, 5, 11, 4, 4 — centred near 5 hours with a long right tail. A pie chart of the same numbers gives 40 slivers, because a share of a total is not a distribution.
- **Population, sample, parameter, statistic — a research question is not statistical until it names them.** The question fixes the **population**; a census measures all of it, a **sample** a subset. The number you want is a **parameter**, the number you compute is a **statistic**, and this lesson is about the gap between them. Worked example: the mean commute of all 8,400 employees is the parameter $\mu$; the mean of 200 sampled employees, $\bar{x} = 34.6$ minutes, is the statistic estimating it.
- **Random selection is what makes a sample representative — on average, not on any one draw.** In a **simple random sample** every group of $n$ individuals is equally likely, so no characteristic can systematically over-populate it. **Stratified** sampling draws an SRS inside each of several homogeneous strata, which cuts variability sharply; **cluster** sampling takes whole naturally occurring groups at random and measures everyone in them — cheaper, but far noisier when a cluster's members resemble each other; **systematic** sampling takes every $k$th individual after a random start. Worked example: 8,400 employees at sites of 5,000, 2,400 and 1,000. Proportional stratification of $n = 200$ takes 119, 57 and 24; a simple random sample could easily draw 15 from the smallest site, or 35.
- **Convenience and voluntary response are the two failure modes, and bias is a shift, not noise.** A convenience sample takes whoever is easiest to access; a voluntary response sample takes whoever chooses to answer, over-representing strong opinions. Three named biases: **undercoverage** (the sampling frame never reaches part of the population), **nonresponse** (the selected individual does not answer) and **response bias**, including the wording of the question. Worked example: an intranet poll returns 900 of 8,400 with a mean commute of 51 minutes against a true 34.6 — long commuters had the motive to reply. Push the replies to 5,000 of the same kind and the estimate does not drift toward 34.6; it becomes *more precisely wrong*. **Raising $n$ shrinks variability and does nothing whatever to bias.**
- **Observational study or experiment: the difference is assignment, and nothing else.** In an **observational study** you record the explanatory variable as you find it, so the exposed and unexposed groups formed themselves. In an **experiment** the investigator imposes the treatments on the experimental units. Worked example: joggers average 62 bpm resting heart rate against 71 for non-joggers. The 9-bpm gap is real data; what it is *evidence for* is open, because the joggers also chose to be joggers.
- **Confounding is the exact name for that failure.** A **confounding variable** is associated with the explanatory variable *and* affects the response, so its contribution cannot be separated from the treatment's. Worked example: suppose 80% of the joggers but only 30% of the non-joggers are under 35, and being under 35 lowers resting heart rate by 8 bpm on its own. That imbalance alone shifts the group means by $8 \times (0.80 - 0.30) = 4.0$ bpm — nearly half the observed gap — even if jogging does exactly nothing. A **lurking variable** is the same thing never recorded, and there is the sting: adjustment reaches the confounders you measured and never the ones you did not.
- **Random assignment breaks confounding, including the confounders nobody thought of.** A chance device makes treatment independent of every pre-existing characteristic at once, measured or not, so the arms balance on all of them on average. Worked example: 100 volunteers split 50/50 by coin flip land near 50% under-35 in both arms instead of 80% against 30%; the leftover imbalance is chance, of order $1/\sqrt{n}$, shrinking from roughly 0.22 at $n = 20$ to 0.05 at $n = 400$. Matching subjects on age instead fixes age and nothing else.
- **Control, replication and blinding are the other three pillars.** A **control group**, often on a **placebo**, supplies the baseline the effect is measured against and absorbs the improvement from being treated at all. **Replication** means enough units per treatment for a real effect to stand clear of unit-to-unit variability: with a per-person spread of 9 bpm the standard error of a difference is $9\sqrt{2/n}$ — 5.7 bpm at $n = 5$, 0.9 bpm at $n = 200$ — so a true 3-bpm effect is invisible in the first and obvious in the second. **Blinding** keeps subjects from knowing their arm; **double-blind** keeps whoever delivers the treatment and measures the response in the dark too, so expectation cannot leak into the measurement.
- **Blocking is stratification applied to assignment.** Split the units into blocks homogeneous in a known nuisance variable, then randomize *within* each block. Worked example: 60 subjects, 30 who do regular cardio and 30 who do not; randomizing 15/15 inside each block forces the cardio share to 50% in both arms by construction, where one overall randomization leaves it to chance. A **matched pairs** design is the limiting case — blocks of two, or each subject as their own control.
- **Scope of inference: two random acts, two separate licences.** Random *selection* licenses generalizing to the population. Random *assignment* licenses a causal conclusion about the individuals studied. Memorize the four cells: both, and the causal claim covers the population; assignment only, on volunteers, and it covers just those volunteers; selection only, and you have a generalizable association with no cause; neither, a description of the sample and nothing more.

**Rules to remember**

- A variable is a characteristic that varies across individuals. Categorical values name a group; quantitative values are amounts.
- One quantitative variable → histogram. One categorical variable → bar chart or pie chart. Two categorical variables → two-way table.
- A parameter describes the population, a statistic the sample; the design decides how close they get.
- Bias is a systematic shift; variability is spread. A larger $n$ shrinks the second and never the first.
- Observational versus experimental turns on one thing: did the investigator assign the treatment?
- A confounder is linked to the explanatory variable *and* affects the response. Adjustment reaches only the ones you measured.
- Random assignment balances everything on average; blocking balances one known nuisance variable exactly.
- Random selection buys generalization, random assignment buys causation, and neither buys the other.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Calling a variable quantitative because its values are digits.** Zip codes, jersey numbers and coded survey answers still place individuals into named groups.
- **Reaching for a pie chart or a category bar chart to show a quantitative distribution.** Both need categories; a distribution needs the number line the histogram's bins live on.
- **Believing a bigger sample repairs a biased one.** It sharpens the wrong answer. Only the selection method fixes a selection problem.
- **Calling a large observational study an experiment.** Size, budget and care, however carefully applied, do not create random assignment.
- **Treating "we controlled for age and income" as equivalent to randomizing.** Adjustment reaches the recorded variables; randomization reaches all of them.
- **Confusing stratifying with blocking.** Strata partition the population for *selection*; blocks partition the units for *assignment*.
- **Reading a well-run experiment on volunteers as a statement about everyone.** Random assignment was there; random selection was not.
- **Assuming a control group makes a study blind.** Control supplies a baseline, blinding removes expectation; a study can have either without the other.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For a variable question, ask what a single value *is*: a name for a group, or an amount. Then check the options for a numeral masquerading as a measurement.
2. For a display question, ask what the horizontal axis is made of. A number line means histogram; labels mean bar chart or pie chart; two sets of labels mean a two-way table.
3. Find the sentence saying how individuals reached the study and the one saying how the groups were formed. Nearly everything else is scenery.
4. If nobody assigned anything, it is observational, and the answer is an association plus a plausible confounder — never a cause.
5. When an option offers a causal claim, look for random assignment; when it offers "all customers", look for random selection. The 2×2 settles most scope questions alone.
6. Distrust options that fix bias with sample size, or that promote any careful comparison to "controlled".
