**The big idea**: **Distributions in two-way tables** already had you divide a cell by its own row total and call the answer a conditional distribution; **Analyzing one categorical variable** had you divide a count by a grand total and call it a relative frequency; **Density curves** had you read the area between two values as the fraction of the data lying there. Probability changes almost none of that arithmetic and all of its status — the same ratios now describe a process you are *about to run* rather than a dataset you already hold. So every rule in this lesson is one of two questions asked carefully: *what is the complete list of things that could happen*, and *which total am I dividing by*. Get the outcome list right and the denominator right, and set operations, the addition rule, conditional probability, independence and both multiplication rules are all consequences of counting.

**Key concepts**

- **Randomness, probability, and simulation.** A random process is one whose single outcomes are uncertain but whose long-run pattern is not: repeat it enough and the relative frequency of an event settles down. Probability *is* the number it settles on. That is why the mechanism matters — without long-run stability there would be nothing for a probability to be. A **simulation** imitates the process with a **random number generator**, runs many trials, and reports the observed proportion as an estimate: 270 successes in 1,000 trials gives $270/1000 = 0.27$. Increasing the number of trials makes the estimate *more stable*; it never removes the randomness and never guarantees the exact theoretical value. Simulation earns its keep exactly when direct calculation is hard but a random model is easy to imitate — a queue, a shuffled deck, a retry policy. Two things randomness is not: a promise that short runs are balanced, and a claim that nothing causes anything.
- **Basic theoretical probability.** Theoretical probability is obtained by *reasoning about equally likely outcomes*, not by collecting data and not by opinion: $P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}$. On a fair six-sided die every face is equally likely, so $P(4) = 1/6$; on a fair coin $P(\text{heads}) = 1/2$. Because a favourable count can be no smaller than 0 and no larger than the total, **every probability is a number between 0 and 1, inclusive** — an impossible event has probability 0, a certain event has probability 1. A "probability" of 2, of $-0.3$, or of 27 is not a small error; it means the denominator was wrong.
- **Experimental probability.** Experimental (empirical) probability is computed from the *actual observed results* of many trials: successes ÷ trials. A coin showing heads 47 times in 100 tosses has experimental probability $47/100 = 0.47$ — not $0.53$, which is the complement, and not $0.50$, which is the theoretical model. A spinner landing on red 18 times in 50 spins gives $18/50$. The contrast to hold: **experimental probability comes from data, theoretical probability comes from a model**, and the two are never guaranteed to be equal on any finite run. As the number of trials grows, the experimental value tends to settle near the true probability rather than drifting away from it — which is what licenses simulation in the first place, and is also how a suspiciously unfair die is eventually caught.
- **Probability using sample spaces.** The **sample space** is the set of *all* possible outcomes of an experiment — not the likeliest outcome, not the number of trials. For one toss of one fair coin it is $\{H, T\}$; for two fair coins tossed together it is $\{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$, **4 distinct outcomes**, because $HT$ and $TH$ are different outcomes even when they look alike in a summary. Writing the space out is worth the effort because it lets you count favourable and total outcomes directly instead of guessing a ratio: rolling one fair die, "even" is $\{2, 4, 6\}$, so $P(\text{even}) = 3/6 = 1/2$. Listing does not prove independence and does not restrict you to any particular kind of variable; it just organises the counting.
- **Basic set operations.** Events are sets of outcomes, so set language applies unchanged. The **union** $A \cup B$ contains the outcomes in $A$, in $B$, *or in both* — an inclusive "or". The **intersection** $A \cap B$ contains only outcomes lying in **both**. The **complement** $A^c$ contains everything in the universal set that is not in $A$. With $A = \{1,2,3\}$ and $B = \{3,4\}$: $A \cup B = \{1,2,3,4\}$ and $A \cap B = \{3\}$. With universal set $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ and $A = \{2,5\}$, the complement is $A^c = \{1,3,4\}$ — which is also why $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$ is free.
- **Addition rule.** $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$. The subtraction is not a correction factor; it is bookkeeping. Adding $P(A)$ and $P(B)$ counts every outcome lying in both events **twice**, so one copy must come back out. With $P(A) = 0.4$, $P(B) = 0.5$ and $P(A \cap B) = 0.2$: $0.4 + 0.5 - 0.2 = 0.7$. When $A$ and $B$ are **mutually exclusive** (disjoint) they share no outcome at all, so $P(A \cap B) = 0$ and the rule collapses to plain addition: disjoint events with $P(A) = 0.3$, $P(B) = 0.2$ give $P(A \cup B) = 0.5$.
- **Conditional probability and independence.** $P(A \mid B)$ is the probability that $A$ occurs *given that $B$ has already occurred* — not the reverse, and not "both". The mechanism is a change of denominator: you throw away every outcome outside $B$ and rescale what is left, $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$, defined only when $P(B) > 0$. With $P(A \cap B) = 0.2$ and $P(B) = 0.5$, $P(A \mid B) = 0.2/0.5 = 0.4$. **Independence** is then the statement that this rescaling changed nothing: $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$, equivalently $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$. Check it by multiplying — $P(A) = 0.3$, $P(B) = 0.4$, $P(A \cap B) = 0.12$ and $0.3 \times 0.4 = 0.12$, so those events are **independent**. If learning that $B$ occurred moves $P(A)$ at all, the events are **dependent**.
- **Multiplication rule for independent events.** Rearranging the definition gives the general rule $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)$, and when the events are independent $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$, so it simplifies to a plain product: $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$. Roll a fair die and flip a fair coin — physically separate mechanisms, so $P(6 \text{ and heads}) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{12}$. Toss a fair coin twice: $P(\text{both heads}) = \frac12 \times \frac12 = \frac14$. Independent means *one event does not change the probability of the other*; it does not mean the two probabilities are equal, and it certainly does not mean the events cannot both happen.
- **Multiplication rule for dependent events.** When the first event changes the setup for the second, you must use the conditional factor: $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)$. Sampling **without replacement** is the canonical case, because the first draw changes what remains for the second. A bag holds 3 red and 2 blue marbles; drawing two without replacement, $P(\text{both red}) = \frac{3}{5} \times \frac{2}{4} = \frac{6}{20} = \frac{3}{10}$. The second factor is $2/4$, not $3/5$: one red is gone and the bag now holds 4 marbles. Draw *with* replacement and the draws are independent, giving $\frac35 \times \frac35 = \frac{9}{25}$ instead — the same bag, a different experiment. Two cards from a standard deck without replacement are dependent for exactly this reason.

**Rules to remember**

- $0 \le P(\text{event}) \le 1$; impossible is 0, certain is 1, and $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$.
- Theoretical = favourable ÷ total over **equally likely** outcomes. Experimental = successes ÷ trials. Neither is an estimate of the other's method.
- More trials stabilise an estimate. They never remove randomness or force the exact answer.
- The sample space is *every* outcome. $\{H,T\}$ for one coin, four outcomes for two.
- Union = in either **or both**; intersection = in **both**; complement = everything else in the universal set.
- $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$, always. Mutually exclusive just means that last term is 0.
- $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B) / P(B)$ — condition on $B$ by making $B$ the new denominator.
- Independent $\iff P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) \iff P(A \mid B) = P(A)$. Otherwise dependent.
- The general product $P(A)\,P(B \mid A)$ covers both cases; independence is the special case where $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Reporting a count where a probability was asked for.** 270 successes is a count; $0.27$ is the probability. Likewise 18 red spins is not the answer — $18/50$ is.
- **Confusing mutually exclusive with independent.** Disjoint events have $P(A \cap B) = 0$; independent events have $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. For two events of positive probability those cannot both hold, so disjoint events are in fact *strongly* dependent — one occurring rules the other out.
- **Adding probabilities of overlapping events.** Forgetting to subtract $P(A \cap B)$ double-counts the overlap and can push the total above 1.
- **Reversing the conditional.** $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ have different denominators and are different numbers; the wording "given that" tells you which set becomes the new universe.
- **Multiplying straight through when sampling without replacement.** $3/5 \times 3/5 = 9/25$ is the with-replacement answer and is the standard distractor for $3/10$.
- **Believing the long run guarantees the short run.** Randomness does not mean no pattern ever emerges, nor that a small number of trials must come out balanced.
- **Treating a simulated estimate as exact.** It is an estimate, and re-running the simulation moves it.
- **Assuming randomness rules out causation.** They are unrelated ideas; a random process can have perfectly good causes.
- **Miscounting the sample space.** Two coins have four outcomes, not three — $HT$ and $TH$ must be organised as separate entries or every probability built on that list is wrong.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Decide first whether the question is theoretical (a model of equally likely outcomes), experimental (observed counts), or simulated (trials imitating a model). The three have different denominators and the distractors trade on mixing them.
2. If the sample space is small, write it out. Counting a listed set beats reasoning about a ratio you have not seen.
3. Sanity-check every answer against $0 \le P \le 1$ and against the complement. An answer above 1 or an answer that is $1 - $ the right one is usually a lost or extra term.
4. For "or", reach for the addition rule and ask explicitly whether the events overlap. For "and", reach for the multiplication rule and ask explicitly whether the first event changes the second.
5. To test independence, multiply $P(A)$ by $P(B)$ and compare with $P(A \cap B)$ — a one-line check that settles the question without any story about the events.
6. On any "without replacement" wording, change both the numerator and the denominator of the second factor before multiplying.
