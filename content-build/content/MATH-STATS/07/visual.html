1. Two-Way Table Bridge

Two-way tables and Distributions in two-way tables already taught you to divide a cell by the grand total, by a margin, or by its own row total. Nothing about the arithmetic changes today — only the names. Drag the athletes who passed and watch the same cell answer three different questions, one of which is P(A and B), one P(A), and one P(A given B). Like asking "how many of everyone?" and "how many of us?" about one identical group of people.

The move that carries the whole lesson is the one you just made with a slider: the cell never changed meaning, the denominator did. Probability keeps exactly that habit and adds one thing — the ratios now describe a run of the process that has not happened yet, which is why they must obey rules (they sit between 0 and 1, complements sum to 1) that a descriptive percentage never had to.

2. Sample Space Counter

Theoretical probability is not a feeling and not a measurement — it is a count over the sample space, the set of every outcome the experiment can produce. Pick an experiment, pick an event, and watch favourable ÷ total do all the work. Like a raffle drum: you win by counting your tickets and then counting the drum.

Every chip here is equally likely, which is the assumption that lets counting stand in for probability at all. Load the die and the chips stay the same while the arithmetic stops being valid — which is precisely the gap that experimental probability, in the next visual, exists to measure.

3. Trials Settle Down

A simulation runs the experiment many times with a random number generator and reports successes ÷ trials. Eight independent simulations are drawn here at once; watch the band they span narrow as the trials pile up, and press reseed to prove nothing was ever removed. Like listening to a crowd: one voice is unpredictable, ten thousand have a definite volume.

This is why a simulation is worth building at all: when a process is easy to imitate but awkward to calculate — a shuffled deck, a retry policy, a queue — you can generate the answer instead of deriving it. The price is honest and permanent: what you get back is an estimate, and the way to buy precision is more trials, never a cleverer reading of a short run.

4. Union Minus Overlap

Events are sets of outcomes, so union, intersection and complement are the whole toolkit. Tap an outcome to move it between A, B, both and neither, and watch the addition rule keep its books. Like counting the people at a party who brought food or drink: add the two lists and everyone who brought both gets counted twice.

The complement is the quiet workhorse here: because A and its complement are disjoint and together cover everything, P(A) + P(not A) = 1. Any time an event is described with "at least one", the complement — "none" — is usually a single, much smaller count, and subtracting from 1 beats enumerating.

5. Shrink The Universe

Conditioning on B does one physical thing: it throws away every outcome outside B and rescales what is left. Build a 100-outcome world, then switch to the conditioned view and watch the denominator change under your hands. Like being told the card is a face card — the deck did not change, your deck did.

Two habits worth building. First, P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different questions with different denominators, and swapping them is the single most expensive error in applied probability. Second, independence is checked by arithmetic, never by whether the two events "feel related" — the multiplication test settles it in one line.

6. With Or Without Replacement

One bag, two draws, and a single switch that decides whether the events are independent. Every branch below is an exact fraction, so you can read the second draw changing its mind. Like dealing cards versus rolling a die: the deck remembers what left it, the die never does.

The general rule is a single formula, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B given A), and independence is only the special case where the second factor forgets the first. That is worth organising your memory around: there is one multiplication rule, and "multiply the two probabilities" is what it degenerates to when the first event leaves the second one untouched.
