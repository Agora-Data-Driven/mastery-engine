**The big idea**: **Probability using sample spaces** and **Basic theoretical probability** gave you the definition this lesson exists to serve — when outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes — and both computed those two numbers by *listing* the sample space. **Multiplication rule for independent events** and **Multiplication rule for dependent events** already carry the engine in probability clothing: a multi-stage outcome is assembled by multiplying stage by stage, with the pool shrinking when the stages are dependent. Listing dies fast, though. A 5-card hand from 52 cards has 2,598,960 members, and no enumeration you would write by hand reaches it. This lesson replaces the list with arithmetic, in four steps — **Counting principle and factorial**, **Permutations**, **Combinations**, and **Combinatorics and probability** (khanacademy.org). Build an outcome as a sequence of choices and multiply the choice counts; when the choices are the items themselves and none may repeat, that product is a factorial; when you stop after $r$ slots, divide the unused tail off; and when order was never part of the outcome, divide out the $r!$ ways you counted each selection. Then hand both numbers back to favourable-over-total, under one discipline: **count the numerator and the denominator the same way.**

**Key concepts**

- **The fundamental counting principle: if one task can be done in $m$ ways and a second in $n$ ways, the two together can be done in $m \cdot n$ ways.** The mechanism is a tree. Each of the $m$ first-stage branches carries its own full copy of the $n$ second-stage branches, so the leaves are $m$ copies of $n$ — a product, not a sum. With 3 shirts and 4 pants there are $3 \times 4 = 12$ outfits: shirt 1 pairs with 4 pants, and so does shirt 2, and so does shirt 3. The distractors are real operations doing other jobs — $m + n = 7$ counts *single garments* you could pick, one item in total, while $m - n$ and $m / n$ answer nothing here. The rule chains over any number of stages: add 2 hats and it is $3 \times 4 \times 2 = 24$.
- **Repetition allowed or forbidden changes only what the next factor is.** If every one of $r$ slots draws from the same $n$ items with repeats permitted, each slot has $n$ choices and the count is $n^r$: a 4-digit PIN is $10^4 = 10{,}000$. Forbid repeats and the pool shrinks by one each time: $10 \times 9 \times 8 \times 7 = 5{,}040$. Same experiment, different rule — which is exactly why $n^r$ is *not* the permutation formula for arbitrary $n$ and $r$. It is the with-repetition count.
- **A factorial is the counting principle run to exhaustion: $n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots 1$.** Arrange 4 distinct books in a row on a shelf: 4 books can take the first position, 3 remain for the second, 2 for the third, 1 for the last, so $4! = 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 24$. Likewise $5! = 120$ — not $5^2 = 25$, not $5 \times 4 = 20$. The growth is violent: $10! = 3{,}628{,}800$, so a program printing one arrangement per millisecond needs an hour to finish the list you were tempted to write out.
- **$0! = 1$ is forced by the recurrence, not decreed.** Every factorial obeys $n! = n \times (n-1)!$. Put $n = 1$: $1! = 1 \times 0!$, and since $1! = 1$, it follows that $0! = 1$. Combinatorially there is exactly one arrangement of nothing — the empty one — so the answer is 1, never 0 and never undefined. The formulas below need it too: arranging all $n$ items should give $n!$, and $\frac{n!}{(n-n)!} = \frac{n!}{0!}$ only delivers that when $0! = 1$.
- **Permutations count ordered arrangements: $_nP_r = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$.** Derive it instead of memorising it. Filling $r$ labelled slots from $n$ distinct items gives $n$ choices, then $n-1$, down to $n-r+1$ — exactly $r$ factors. Multiplying that falling product by $\frac{(n-r)!}{(n-r)!}$ completes the top into $n!$, which is all the ratio ever was. Arranging 3 of 5 distinct students in a line: $5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60$, and $\frac{5!}{2!} = \frac{120}{2} = 60$. Two-letter arrangements from A, B, C with no letter repeated: $3 \times 2 = 6$, namely AB, AC, BA, BC, CA, CB. See why the near-misses fail: $\frac{r!}{(n-r)!}$ has the wrong quantity on top, and $\frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}$ has already divided the orderings away.
- **"Order matters" is the whole test, and role labels are the giveaway.** Choosing a president, a vice president and a secretary from 5 people is a permutation, because Ana-as-president is a different outcome from Ana-as-secretary: $5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60$. Apply the swap test — exchange two chosen items and ask whether the outcome changed. If it did, you need a permutation; if it did not, a combination.
- **Combinations count selections: $_nC_r = \frac{_nP_r}{r!} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}$.** The mechanism is an exact over-count. Every unordered set of $r$ items shows up among the ordered lists precisely $r!$ times, once per internal rearrangement, so dividing by $r!$ is exact rather than approximate. Three-person committees from 6 people: $_6P_3 = 6 \times 5 \times 4 = 120$ ordered slates, each committee appearing $3! = 6$ times, so $_6C_3 = \frac{120}{6} = 20$. Two students chosen from 5 when order does not matter: $\frac{5 \times 4}{2} = 10$. Four pizza toppings from 10 available: $_{10}C_4 = \frac{5040}{24} = 210$ — a selection, because a pizza does not remember which topping you named first. The symmetry $_nC_r = {}_nC_{n-r}$ comes free: choosing 4 toppings to put on is the same act as choosing 6 to leave off.
- **Permutations are never fewer than combinations, and the two are equal exactly when $r \le 1$.** The factor between them is $r!$, and $r! = 1$ only at $r = 0$ and $r = 1$. So the slogan "a permutation always gives a bigger number" is false in precisely those two cases — worth holding, because $_nC_1 = {}_nP_1 = n$ is a place a question can sit.
- **Combinatorics turns into probability the moment the outcomes are equally likely: $P(E) = \frac{\text{favourable}}{\text{total}}$.** Not total minus favourable, not favourable plus total, and nothing to do with an expected value or a variance. Counting supplies both numbers. Draw a 2-card hand at random from 4 distinct cards. Total hands: $_4C_2 = 6$. Hands containing one specified card: hold that card fixed and choose its partner from the remaining 3, giving $_3C_1 = 3$. So $P = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$.
- **The convention cancels — provided you use the same one above and below the bar.** Recount that same experiment as ordered deals: total $_4P_2 = 12$, and the specified card can occupy either position with 3 partners each, so 6 are favourable and $P = \frac{6}{12} = \frac{1}{2}$, identical. Mix the conventions — 3 favourable hands over 12 ordered deals — and $\frac{1}{4}$ answers no question at all. In general $\frac{_{n-1}C_{r-1}}{_nC_r} = \frac{r}{n}$, which sanity-checks at both ends: draw one card of $n$ and it is $\frac{1}{n}$; draw the whole deck and it is 1.
- **When the outcomes themselves are ordered, permutations are the appropriate tool.** A password of 2 different letters chosen from 5, order significant, has $_5P_2 = 5 \times 4 = 20$ possibilities, because the sample space *is* a set of ordered pairs. Choose one uniformly and $P(\text{it starts with A}) = \frac{4}{20} = \frac{1}{5}$. The tool follows the sample space you are counting, not your taste.

**Rules to remember**

- Stages multiply: $m$ ways then $n$ ways gives $m \cdot n$, and the rule chains over any number of stages.
- Repeats allowed: $n^r$. Repeats forbidden: the falling product $n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)$.
- $n!$ arranges all $n$ items; $4! = 24$; $5! = 120$; $0! = 1$.
- $_nP_r = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$ when order matters; $_nC_r = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}$ when it does not.
- $_nC_r = \frac{_nP_r}{r!}$, $_nC_r = {}_nC_{n-r}$, and $_nC_0 = {}_nC_n = 1$.
- Swap two chosen items: outcome changed means permutation, outcome unchanged means combination.
- Equally likely outcomes give $P = \frac{\text{favourable}}{\text{total}}$, and nothing else does.
- Count numerator and denominator with the same convention, and the convention cancels.
- Cancel factorials, never expand them: $\frac{10!}{7!} = 10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720$.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Adding when the stages compose.** 3 shirts with 4 pants is 12 outfits, not 7; addition counts single garments, multiplication counts pairs.
- **Answering $0! = 0$ or "undefined".** The recurrence forces 1, and every formula containing $(n-r)!$ breaks at $r = n$ otherwise.
- **Reading $5!$ as $5 \times 5$ or as $5 \times 4$.** The factorial runs all the way down to 1.
- **Using a permutation for a committee.** It over-counts by exactly $r!$: 120 ordered slates where the answer is 20 committees.
- **Using a combination for ranked roles.** President, VP and secretary from 5 is 60, not 10; the labels make the order real.
- **Quoting $n^r$ as the permutation formula.** It is the with-repetition count, correct only when repeats are allowed.
- **Expanding factorials before cancelling.** $\frac{52!}{47!}$ overflows a calculator long before the five surviving factors would.
- **Mixing ordered and unordered counts in one fraction.** The most damaging error in the topic, because each number is individually correct.
- **Reaching for favourable-over-total when outcomes are not equally likely.** That formula is a consequence of the equally-likely hypothesis, not a definition that survives without it.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Read for the word that fixes order. "Arranged", "in a row", "line", "password", "president and vice president" mean permutation; "committee", "hand", "toppings", "chosen" with no roles attached mean combination.
2. Run the swap test explicitly before writing a single formula. It settles nearly every classification item on its own.
3. Ask whether repeats are allowed. "Distinct", "different" and "no letter repeated" forbid them and send you to the falling product rather than to $n^r$.
4. Compute by cancelling: write $_nP_r$ as $r$ descending factors, and $_nC_r$ as that same product over $r!$.
5. Sanity-check magnitudes with $_nC_r \le {}_nP_r \le n^r$, remembering the equalities at $r \le 1$.
6. For a probability, count the total first, then the favourable outcomes with the same convention, then divide. If an answer exceeds 1, you mixed conventions.
7. For formula-identification items, look at the denominator: an $r!$ present means combinations, absent means permutations, and an $n!$ anywhere but the numerator is a distractor.
