1. Counting Without Listing

Probability using sample spaces found probabilities by writing the sample space out and counting it. Drag the two stages and watch that written-out tree grow past the point where anyone would write it — which is the entire reason the counting principle exists. Like a wardrobe: every shirt faces the whole rail of trousers, so outfits multiply rather than add.

The counting principle is the Multiplication rule for independent events with the probabilities stripped out: independent stages multiply their option counts exactly as independent events multiply their probabilities. And when the stages are dependent — no item may be reused — the pool shrinks by one each time instead of resetting. That single change is the next three visuals.

2. Factorial Ladder

Arrange everything, and the pool shrinks by one at every slot. Drag n to watch the falling product, the recurrence that forces 0! = 1, and how long it would take to print the list. Like seating guests at a table: each chair you fill leaves one fewer guest standing.

0! = 1 is not a special case bolted on afterwards. It is what the recurrence n! = n × (n−1)! already says at n = 1, and every later formula quietly leans on it: arranging all n items must come out as n!, and n! / (n−n)! only agrees with that when 0! = 1.

3. Slots And Leftovers

Stop after r slots instead of arranging everything, and the leftovers are what the factorial formula divides away. Switch repetition on and off to see why n^r is a different question, not a wrong answer. Like a podium: three places to award, and everyone else goes home uncounted.

n^r is a perfectly good counting formula — it is simply answering a different question. Read the wording of the item: "distinct", "different" and "no letter repeated" forbid reuse and send you to the falling product, while a PIN pad, a die rolled twice or a coin tossed r times allow it. Nothing in the arithmetic tells you which world you are in; only the sentence does.

4. Divide Out Order

Every selection you want was counted once for each of its r! internal rearrangements. Cycle through the selections and watch one of them explode into the ordered lists that all mean the same thing. Like a handful of coins: it does not matter which one you picked up first.

The r! you divide by is the exact size of every over-count group, never an approximation — which is why a quotient of factorials always lands on a whole number. It is also why using a permutation where a combination was wanted is wrong by a factor you can name and correct, rather than merely "too big".

5. Favourable Over Total

Deal a hand at random and ask for the chance it holds card A. Count it as unordered hands, then as ordered deals: both numbers change, the probability does not. Then mix the two and watch it break. Like weighing in kilograms or in pounds — fine, until you put one on each side of the scale.

Favourable-over-total is a consequence of the outcomes being equally likely, not a definition that survives without it — and it never says which convention to count in, only that you use one. When the outcome is itself ordered, permutations are the natural convention: a password of 2 different letters from 5 has 5P2 = 20 possibilities, and the chance a random one starts with A is 4 / 20 = 1/5.
