**The big idea**: **Probability using sample spaces** taught you to list outcomes; **Basic theoretical probability**, the **Addition rule**, the **Multiplication rule for independent events** and **Combinations** taught you to compute with them. A random variable is the hinge that turns all of that into numbers: a rule attaching one number to every outcome in the sample space, so a chance process acquires a *distribution* — with a centre and a spread, exactly the language you built in **Measuring center in quantitative data**, **Variance and standard deviation of a population** and **Density curves**. Four moves follow. Split random variables by the values they can take (**Discrete random variables**, **Continuous random variables**); define the centre of one (**More on expected value**); name the three families repeated independent trials generate — binomial, geometric and Poisson, each with its own mean and standard deviation formulas; and see what happens to that centre and spread when you rescale one variable or add two together (**Transforming random variables**, **Combining random variables**). All nine units come from the same course source (khanacademy.org), and all nine yield to one habit: write the distribution down first, then read the summary off it.

**Key concepts**

- **Discrete random variables take countable numerical values, and their probabilities must sum to exactly 1.** The mechanism is that $X$ is a rule, not a number: over three coin tosses, $X$ = "number of heads" sends HHT, HTH and THH all to the single value 2. Grouping outcomes by the value of $X$ partitions the whole sample space, which is *why* the probabilities sum to 1 — never to 0, to the mean or to the standard deviation. Countability is the test: the list 1, 2, 3, 4 is a legal complete set of values, and the number of heads in 5 coin tosses is discrete (0 through 5), while a chosen student's height, the noon temperature and the time taken to run a mile are not. For one roll of a fair six-sided die, $P(X = 4) = 1/6$ and $6 \times 1/6 = 1$.
- **Continuous random variables take any value inside real-number intervals, and probability is area, never height.** Because the values fill an interval — say the closed interval $[0, 5]$ — no single value can carry positive probability: hand each of infinitely many points the same $c > 0$ and the total blows past 1. So $P(X = c) = 0$ for any exact $c$, and only ranges are worth asking about: $P(2 \le X \le 3)$ is the *area under the density curve* between 2 and 3, the object you met in **Density curves**. Summing bar heights and counting equally likely cases are discrete tools and mislead here. The exact weight of a shipped package is continuous; the number of children in a family, of games a team wins, or of cars in a lot are not.
- **Expected value is the probability-weighted sum $E(X) = \sum x \cdot P(x)$ — the long-run average, not the most likely outcome and not the median.** The weights do the work: the largest $P(x)$, an unweighted sum of the $x$-values, and the product of the probabilities are three other quantities entirely. For a fair die, $E(X) = (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 3.5$, a value the die can never show — the "average" is the balance point of the distribution, not an attainable outcome. A degenerate check: if $X$ always takes the value 4, $E(X) = 4$. And because the sum is linear, shifting the variable shifts its mean: $E(X + 3) = E(X) + 3$.
- **Binomial random variables count the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials.** Four conditions, all required: a fixed number $n$ of trials, independence, two outcomes per trial, constant success probability $p$. Then $X$ counts *successes*, not trials, not an average and not a probability, and $P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$ — **Combinations** supplies $\binom{n}{k}$, the number of orderings, and the **Multiplication rule for independent events** supplies $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$, the probability of one ordering. Counting heads in 5 tosses of a fair coin qualifies. For $X \sim \text{Binomial}(4, 0.5)$, $P(X = 0) = \binom{4}{0}(0.5)^4 = 1/16$; for $X \sim \text{Binomial}(3, 0.2)$, all three succeeding is $0.2^3 = 0.008$, not $3 \times 0.2$ and not $0.8^3$. Rolling a die until the first 6 fixes no $n$; drawing cards without replacement changes $p$; recording heights is not a count.
- **The binomial mean and standard deviation formulas, $\mu = np$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)}$, fall straight out of decomposition.** Write $X = I_1 + \dots + I_n$, where each indicator is 1 on success and 0 on failure. One indicator has mean $p$ and variance $p(1-p)$; means always add, and variances add because the trials are independent, so $\mu = np$ and $\text{Var}(X) = np(1-p)$, whose square root is $\sigma$. For $X \sim \text{Binomial}(10, 0.3)$ the mean is 3. For $X \sim \text{Binomial}(10, 0.5)$, $\sigma = \sqrt{10(0.5)(0.5)} = \sqrt{2.5} \approx 1.58$ — the root matters, since $np(1-p) = 2.5$ is the variance and $\sqrt{np} = \sqrt{5}$ is nothing at all. Hold $n$ fixed and push $p$ toward 0 or 1: $p(1-p) \to 0$, so $\sigma$ shrinks toward 0 — the arithmetic saying a nearly certain outcome barely varies. Spread is greatest at $p = 0.5$.
- **Geometric random variables count the trial number of the first success.** Same independent trials with constant $p$, but $n$ is not fixed — you stop when you succeed, so the values are $1, 2, 3, \dots$ with no upper bound, and $P(X = k) = (1-p)^{k-1}p$: $k-1$ failures, then a success. With $p = 0.25$, $P(X = 1) = 0.25$ and $P(X = 2) = 0.75 \times 0.25$. The mean is $1/p$, so you wait 4 trials on average at $p = 0.25$ — not $p$, not $np$, not $(1-p)/p$. The toss on which the first heads appears is geometric; the number of heads in 5 tosses is binomial. One sentence separates them: binomial fixes the trials and counts successes, geometric fixes the successes at one and counts trials.
- **The Poisson distribution models counts of random events in a fixed interval, and its parameter $\lambda$ is the average count over that interval.** There is no $n$ and no per-trial probability: $\lambda$ is neither a number of trials nor a standard deviation, and $P(X = k) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$. Emails arriving in an hour is the standard example; student heights and the number of tails in 10 flips are not. Its signature property is that mean and variance coincide: for $\lambda = 3$ the mean is 3, and for $\lambda = 4$ the variance is 4. It is the limit of a binomial with $n \to \infty$, $p \to 0$ and $np \to \lambda$, which is why "many opportunities, each unlikely" is its signature.
- **Transforming random variables: $Y = aX + b$ shifts the mean the same way and scales the spread by $|a|$.** So $E(Y) = aE(X) + b$, $\text{SD}(Y) = |a|\,\text{SD}(X)$ and $\text{Var}(Y) = a^2\text{Var}(X)$. The mechanism is geometric: adding $b$ slides every value along the axis and changes no distance between values, so spread cannot move; multiplying by $a$ stretches every distance by $|a|$, and the absolute value is there because a standard deviation is never negative. This is **Effects of linear transformations** again, on a random variable instead of a data set. If $X$ has mean 10, $Y = X - 4$ has mean 6 and $Y = X + 3$ has a mean 3 higher. If $X$ has standard deviation 5, $Y = -3X$ has standard deviation 15, not $-15$; and $Y = 2X$ doubles it rather than quadrupling it — quadrupling happens to the variance.
- **Combining random variables: means always add, and for independent variables so do variances — even for a difference.** $E(X \pm Y) = \mu_X \pm \mu_Y$ needs no assumption whatever, so means 4 and 7 give 11 for the sum and $-3$ for the difference. $\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)$ requires independence, and so does $\text{Var}(X - Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)$: negating $Y$ flips its values but not its spread, so uncertainty accumulates whichever sign you use. With variances 2 and 5, $\text{Var}(X + Y) = 7$ and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{7} \approx 2.65$ — not $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{5} \approx 3.65$. Standard deviations never add; variances do.

**Rules to remember**

- Discrete means countable values; continuous means every value in an interval.
- Discrete probabilities sum to exactly 1; a density curve encloses total area 1.
- For a continuous variable $P(X = c) = 0$. Ask about ranges; read probability as area.
- $E(X) = \sum x \cdot P(x)$: the long-run average, which need not be attainable.
- Binomial needs fixed $n$, independence, two outcomes, constant $p$; $X$ counts successes.
- Binomial: $\mu = np$, $\sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)}$, peaking at $p = 0.5$ and shrinking to 0 as $p \to 0$ or $1$.
- Geometric: $P(X = k) = (1-p)^{k-1}p$, mean $1/p$, $X$ = the trial of the first success.
- Poisson: mean $=$ variance $= \lambda$.
- $Y = aX + b$: mean $a\mu + b$, standard deviation $|a|\sigma$, variance $a^2\sigma^2$.
- Independent $X$, $Y$: $\text{Var}(X+Y) = \text{Var}(X-Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)$. Never add standard deviations.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Calling a measurement discrete because it is recorded to one decimal place.** Weight, height and time stay continuous however coarsely they are written down.
- **Reading the height of a density curve as a probability.** Height is density; probability is area over a range, and a single point has none.
- **Confusing binomial with geometric.** "In $n$ trials" is binomial; "until the first success" is geometric.
- **Dropping the combination factor.** $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ is one ordering's probability; $\binom{n}{k}$ counts the orderings.
- **Quoting $np(1-p)$ as the standard deviation.** That is the variance — take the square root.
- **Treating $\lambda$ as a probability or a sample size.** It is a mean count, and the variance too.
- **Letting a standard deviation come out negative.** The multiplier enters as $|a|$, so $\text{SD}(-3X) = 15$.
- **Subtracting variances for $X - Y$.** Independent variances add on a difference exactly as on a sum.
- **Adding standard deviations.** Convert to variances, add, then take one square root.
- **Assuming independence.** Sampling without replacement is the usual offender, and it breaks the binomial conditions and the variance rule together.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Classify first: can you list the values, or do they fill an interval? Counts are discrete, measurements are continuous, and that one distinction decides a large share of the questions.
2. For "which scenario" items, run the four binomial conditions in order, then ask whether the setting fixes the trials or fixes the successes at one.
3. For expected value, reach for $\sum x \cdot P(x)$. The distractors are the largest probability, the median and an unweighted sum.
4. Keep $\mu$ and $\sigma$ apart: $np$ against $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$. An option reading $np(1-p)$, $\sqrt{np}$ or $n(1-p)$ is a corrupted formula.
5. For a transformation, apply the shift to the centre only and the scale to both, taking the absolute value of the multiplier before reporting a standard deviation.
6. For two variables, take the means in whatever sign is asked, add the variances regardless of sign, and square-root last.
7. If a question asks for the probability of one exact value of a continuous variable, the answer is 0 before any computation.

A sample mean is a sum of many random variables divided by $n$; a sample proportion is a binomial count divided by $n$.
