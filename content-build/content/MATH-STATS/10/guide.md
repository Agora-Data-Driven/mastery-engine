**The big idea**: **Variance and standard deviation of a population (Khan Academy)** gave you the fixed numbers $\mu$ and $\sigma$; **Variance and standard deviation of a sample (Khan Academy)** gave you $\bar{x}$ and $s$, computed from the handful of individuals you actually measured. **Discrete random variables** taught you to treat a chance-determined number as a random variable, **Transforming random variables** and **Combining random variables** told you what scaling and adding do to a mean and a variance, and **Binomial mean and standard deviation formulas** handed you $np$ and $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$. This lesson welds those halves together into the idea the rest of statistics stands on: **a statistic computed from a random sample is itself a random variable**, so it has a distribution of its own — its *sampling distribution* — whose center and spread you can write down exactly. The three units are **What is a sampling distribution?**, **Sampling distribution of a sample mean** and **Sampling distribution of a sample proportion** (khanacademy.org). Know the center and spread of $\bar{x}$ and of $\hat{p}$ and you can finally say how far one sample's answer is likely to sit from the truth.

**Key concepts**

- **A parameter summarises the whole population; a statistic summarises a sample.** The mechanism separating them is *who was measured*. Census everyone and you get a parameter — the population mean $\mu$, the population standard deviation $\sigma$, the population proportion $p$ — one fixed number, and normally unknown. Measure 40 of them and compute the same summary and you get a statistic: $\bar{x}$, $s$, $\hat{p}$. "The mean commute of all 62,000 employees is 34 minutes" is a parameter; "the mean commute of the 40 we surveyed is 31.8 minutes" is a statistic. Every question in this lesson depends on that split, because only one of the two changes when you sample again.
- **A statistic is a random variable — that is the entire subject.** Redraw 40 employees and $\bar{x}$ comes back 33.1, then 29.6, then 34.4, while $\mu$ sits at 34 the whole time. So $\bar{x}$ is a number produced by a chance process, exactly the object **Discrete random variables** defined, and it therefore owns a mean, a standard deviation and a shape of its own.
- **The sampling distribution of a statistic is its distribution over all possible samples of one fixed size $n$.** Mechanism: fix $n$, draw a sample, compute the statistic, write it down, and repeat over every sample that could have been drawn. The histogram of those recorded values is the sampling distribution. Keep three histograms strictly apart: the population (center $\mu$, spread $\sigma$), the 40 raw values inside one sample (a rough, noisy copy of the population), and the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ (centered at $\mu$, far narrower). It is not the raw data in a single sample, not the population, and not the joint distribution of two variables in a scatterplot.
- **Its use is that it turns "how much does my answer wobble?" into a number.** You only ever get one sample, so what you need is the spread of the values the statistic *could* have taken: that is what says how far a single $\bar{x}$ is likely to miss by. It does not remove the random error, it does not let you skip collecting data, and it proves nothing about whether the population is normal.
- **Center of the sample mean: $E[\bar{x}] = \mu$, for every $n$.** Derive it. Write $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}(X_1 + \cdots + X_n)$ with each $X_i$ drawn from the population, so $E[X_i] = \mu$. Expectation is linear, so $E[X_1 + \cdots + X_n] = n\mu$ and $E[\bar{x}] = n\mu / n = \mu$. If $\mu = 20$, the mean of the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ is 20 — not 40, not 10, and not something that depends on $n$. A statistic centered on the parameter it estimates is called **unbiased**.
- **Spread of the sample mean: $\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \sigma / \sqrt{n}$.** Derive that too. For independent draws variances add, so $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + \cdots + X_n) = n\sigma^2$; scaling a random variable by $1/n$ multiplies its variance by $1/n^2$, giving $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}) = n\sigma^2 / n^2 = \sigma^2 / n$. Take the square root: $\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \sigma / \sqrt{n}$. With $\sigma = 12$ and $n = 9$ that is $12 / \sqrt{9} = 12/3 = 4$. Note what it is not: not $\sigma$, not $\sigma^2$, and not $\mu / \sqrt{n}$ — the population mean plays no part in the spread.
- **The $\sqrt{n}$ in the denominator is a law of diminishing returns.** Spread falls as $n^{-1/2}$, so you must **quadruple** the sample to halve the standard deviation: with $\sigma = 12$, $\sigma_{\bar{x}}$ is 4 at $n = 9$, 2 at $n = 36$, and 1 at $n = 144$. A bigger sample always makes the sampling distribution less spread out around its center, never more, and never shifts that center.
- **Shape, briefly.** If the population is normal then $\bar{x}$ is exactly normal for any $n$; if it is not, the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ grows more bell-shaped as $n$ rises, which is what lets **Z-scores** and **Normal distribution calculations** transfer to $\bar{x}$ once you swap $\sigma$ for $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$. The theorem behind that promise is stated properly in a later unit; here it is only the reason the normal machinery applies. What never happens is the sampling distribution turning categorical, or uniform, or identical for every $n$.
- **Center of the sample proportion: $E[\hat{p}] = p$.** Count the successes: $X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$ and $\hat{p} = X/n$. From **Binomial mean and standard deviation formulas**, $E[X] = np$, so $E[\hat{p}] = np/n = p$. With $p = 0.5$ and $n = 100$ the mean of $\hat{p}$ is $0.5$ — not 0.05, not 5, not 50. It equals $p$ whatever $n$ is; it is not the sample size, not the variance of $\hat{p}$, and it is only 0.5 when $p$ happens to be 0.5.
- **Spread of the sample proportion: $\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$.** Same route: $\text{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}$, and dividing a random variable by $n$ divides its standard deviation by $n$, so $\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{np(1-p)} / n = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$. With $p = 0.5$ and $n = 100$: $\sqrt{0.25/100} = \sqrt{0.0025} = 0.05$. Hold $p$ fixed and raising $n$ always decreases it. The three standing distractors are $p(1-p)/n$ (that is the variance, the square root is missing), $p/n$, and $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$ — which is the standard deviation of the *count* $X$, not of the proportion.
- **$\hat{p}$ is $\bar{x}$ wearing different clothes.** Code each success 1 and each failure 0. That 0/1 population has mean $p$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{p(1-p)}$, so the general rule $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$ reads $\sqrt{p(1-p)} / \sqrt{n} = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$. One rule, two names — which is also why $p = 0.5$ is the hardest case to pin down: $p(1-p)$ peaks at 0.25 there, so for a given $n$ the sampling distribution is at its widest.
- **Both spread formulas assume independent draws, and a finite population only approximates that.** Sampling without replacement makes each draw depend on the ones before it. The standard guard is the **10% condition**, $n \le 0.10N$: the exact correction factor is $\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}$, which stays above about 0.95 while that holds, so it is ignored. For the normal shape of $\hat{p}$, check the **large counts** condition $np \ge 10$ and $n(1-p) \ge 10$ — at $p = 0.5$, $n = 100$ both equal 50, comfortably clear.

**Rules to remember**

- Parameter = population, fixed, usually unknown ($\mu$, $\sigma$, $p$). Statistic = sample, random ($\bar{x}$, $s$, $\hat{p}$).
- A sampling distribution is the distribution of a *statistic* across repeated samples of size $n$ — never the data inside one sample, never the population.
- $E[\bar{x}] = \mu$ and $E[\hat{p}] = p$: both are unbiased, at every $n$.
- $\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$; $\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$.
- Raising $n$ shrinks the spread and leaves the center exactly where it was.
- Quadruple $n$ to halve the standard deviation.
- $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$ is the SD of the count $X$; divide by $n$ to reach the proportion.
- Conditions: $n \le 0.10N$ for independence, $np \ge 10$ and $n(1-p) \ge 10$ for normality of $\hat{p}$.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Calling one sample's histogram a sampling distribution.** The raw values in a sample describe individuals; a sampling distribution describes a statistic across many samples. Distractors offer the first, the population, or a scatterplot's joint distribution.
- **Letting the center depend on $n$.** "It depends on $n$" is always wrong for the mean of $\bar{x}$ and for the mean of $\hat{p}$. Sample size is a spread question only.
- **Dropping the square root, or taking it of the wrong thing.** $\sigma^2/n$ is a variance; $\sigma/n$ over-shrinks. With $\sigma = 12$, $n = 9$, the root belongs to $n$: $12/3 = 4$, not $12/9$ and not $4/3$.
- **Confusing $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$ with $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$.** The first grows with $n$ because counts grow; the second shrinks. Ask which quantity you are describing before choosing.
- **Reading "unbiased" as "accurate".** Unbiased says the sampling distribution is centered correctly, nothing about how wide it is. A single $\bar{x}$ from a tiny sample is unbiased and still badly off.
- **Assuming the mean of $\hat{p}$ is 0.5.** It is $p$. The 0.5 in the worked example came from the stated $p$, not from any rule.
- **Believing a bigger $n$ repairs a bad sample.** Every formula here assumes a random sample; a biased selection method just concentrates the sampling distribution around the wrong value.
- **Treating $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ as exact for a small population.** Once $n$ passes about 10% of $N$, the finite-population correction is doing real work and the simple formula overstates the spread.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Ask "population or sample?" first. Whole population wants $\mu$, $\sigma$ or $p$ and the word *parameter*; anything computed from a sample is a *statistic*.
2. For "what does a sampling distribution describe", the correct option names a **statistic** and **repeated samples**. Reject any option about values within one sample or about individuals in the population.
3. Do centers before spreads: mean of $\bar{x}$ is $\mu$, mean of $\hat{p}$ is $p$. Any option involving $n$ in the center is a distractor.
4. Write the spread formula symbolically before touching numbers, then substitute once.
5. For $\bar{x}$, take the square root of $n$, not of $\sigma$: $\sigma = 12$, $n = 9$ gives $12/3 = 4$.
6. For $\hat{p}$, compute $p(1-p)$ first, divide by $n$, then take the square root: $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$, $0.25/100 = 0.0025$, $\sqrt{0.0025} = 0.05$.
7. On "as $n$ increases" questions, answer *less spread out around the same center* — never more spread, never skewed, never unchanged.

**Where this leads**: the next lesson, **11 Confidence intervals (khanacademy.org)**, runs this lesson backwards. Here you knew $\mu$ or $p$ and derived how $\bar{x}$ and $\hat{p}$ scatter around it; there you have exactly one $\bar{x}$ or $\hat{p}$ and must reach back to the parameter — and the width of the interval you report is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ or $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ multiplied by a critical value, with the estimated standard deviation renamed the *standard error*. Every property established here is what makes that step legal: unbiased centering is why the interval is built around the estimate, and the $\sqrt{n}$ law is why a bigger sample buys a narrower one.
