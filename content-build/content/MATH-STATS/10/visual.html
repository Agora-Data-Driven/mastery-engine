1. Three Histograms

You already read a population off a density curve: center mu, spread sigma (Density curves, Normal distributions and the empirical rule, Variance and standard deviation of a population). New here is a third picture — the histogram of x-bar itself, one value per sample, over 800 repeats. Change the population's shape and the sample size, and watch which of the three moves. Like weighing shoppers: one shopper, one trolley's average, and the spread of a thousand trolley averages are three different pictures.

A  ·  the POPULATION — every individual
B  ·  ONE SAMPLE — the n values you actually measured
C  ·  the SAMPLING DISTRIBUTION — 800 values of x-bar

All three share one horizontal axis, 0 on the left and 19 on the right, so the widths are directly comparable. A and B are pictures of individuals; only C is a picture of a statistic. Confusing B with C is the single most common error in this lesson, and the two look least alike exactly when n is large.

2. Fixed Or Random

Six numbers about the same 62,000 employees. Draw a fresh sample of 40 and three of them never budge while three jump every time. The three that jump are the statistics — and the histogram of one of those columns is its sampling distribution. Like re-weighing a sack of flour: the sack's true weight is fixed, your bathroom scale's reading is not.

This is why the sampling distribution is useful, and the claim is narrow: it does not remove the random error, it does not spare you from collecting data, and it says nothing about whether the population is normal. It tells you how far a single sample's answer tends to land from the parameter — which is the only thing that lets one sample speak about 62,000 people.

3. The Root-n Law

Two curves on one axis: the population, and the sampling distribution of x-bar. Drag mu, sigma and n and watch which one moves sideways and which one only ever changes width. Like a camera: more light does not move the subject, it just sharpens the picture.

▬ population (mu, sigma) ▬ x-bar (mu, sigma/sqrt(n))
population ± 1 sigma
x-bar ± 1 SD

The judgment this panel exists for: sample size is a precision dial, never an accuracy one. It cannot move a centre that is in the wrong place, so a badly drawn sample just gives you a tight, confident, wrong answer. And because the cost of precision runs as the square of the gain, "collect four times the data" is the honest price of halving the wobble.

4. Proportion Machine

A proportion is a mean of ones and zeros, so it obeys the same rule — but the algebra is worth watching happen. Count the successes with a binomial, then divide the whole random variable by n. Like converting a tally into a batting average: the same information, divided by how many times you went in to bat.

p-hat = 00.51

Three distractors live in that chain, and each is a real quantity sitting one step away: p(1−p)/n is the variance (no square root), sqrt(n·p(1−p)) is the SD of the count and grows with n, and p/n is nothing at all. Say out loud which object you are describing — the count or the proportion — before you pick a formula.

5. When Independence Breaks

Both spread formulas were derived by adding variances, which needs independent draws — and drawing people out of a finite population without replacement is not independent. Slide n up against N and watch the exact answer part company with sigma/sqrt(n). Like taking cards from a deck: the first card tells you nothing, the fiftieth tells you almost everything.

Neither condition touches the centre. E[x-bar] = mu and E[p-hat] = p survive dependence and small counts untouched — they only need the sample to be drawn at random. What breaks is the spread formula and the bell shape, which is exactly why every later procedure states its conditions before it quotes a standard error.
