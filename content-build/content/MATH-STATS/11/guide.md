**The big idea**: **What is a sampling distribution? (khanacademy.org)**, **Sampling distribution of a sample mean** and **Sampling distribution of a sample proportion** answered a question you could only ask because someone had already handed you the parameter: *given* $\mu$ or $p$, where do the statistics land? This lesson runs that arrow backwards. You have one sample, you will never see $\mu$, and you still have to report something honest. The trick is that the sampling-distribution statement is symmetric: if $\bar{x}$ lands within $1.96$ standard errors of $\mu$ in about 95% of samples, then $\mu$ sits within $1.96$ standard errors of $\bar{x}$ in exactly those same samples — one event, read from the other end. So **Introduction to confidence intervals** builds an interval around the statistic you *can* see, **Estimating a population mean** and **Estimating a population proportion** supply the two standard errors that go in it, and **More confidence interval videos** drills the arithmetic and the interpretation until the tradeoffs are reflexes. **Z-scores**, **Normal distributions and the empirical rule** and **Variance and standard deviation of a sample** are already yours; there is no new probability here, only a new direction of reading.

**Key concepts**

- **A confidence interval is a plausible range of values for a population parameter — not a statement about your sample.** The parameter is a fixed unknown number; the interval is the random thing, because it is built from a random sample. Mechanism: take the point estimate, reach out the same distance either side, and report the pair. Without it you report $\bar{x} = 503$ hours and imply a precision the data cannot support; with it you report $(493.2, 512.8)$ and the reader can see how much the sample actually pinned down. It is never a range *guaranteed* to contain the value for every sample — some samples miss, by construction.
- **The confidence level is the long-run capture rate of the procedure.** 95% means that if you repeated the whole exercise — new sample, same formula — about 95% of such intervals would capture the parameter. Derivation, with numbers: battery life with $\sigma = 40$ hours and $n = 64$ gives a standard error of $40/\sqrt{64} = 5$; 95% of samples put $\bar{x}$ inside $\mu \pm 1.96(5) = \mu \pm 9.8$; and $|\bar{x} - \mu| < 9.8$ is the same inequality as $\mu \in (\bar{x} - 9.8,\ \bar{x} + 9.8)$. The 95% attaches to the *method*, and once you compute $(493.2, 512.8)$ nothing random is left: that interval either contains $\mu$ or it does not.
- **Three readings of "95%" that are wrong, and worth naming.** It is not that 95% of the population falls inside the interval — an interval for a mean is far narrower than the spread of individuals. It is not that 95% of sample values fall inside it — same confusion, individuals versus a parameter. And it is not the exact probability that $\mu$ lies in *this* computed range; $\mu$ is not random. The parameter does not later change, either: it was fixed before you sampled.
- **The margin of error is the distance from the statistic to each endpoint, and it sets the width.** Interval $=$ estimate $\pm$ margin of error, so the width is $2 \times$ the margin. If $\bar{x} = 72$ with a margin of error of 3, the interval is $(69, 75)$; if $\bar{x} = 50$ with a margin of 2, it is $(48, 52)$. Always write the smaller endpoint first — $(75, 69)$ is not an interval. Structurally, the margin of error is $\text{critical value} \times \text{standard error}$: it measures random sampling noise only, never bias, never the sample size, never the size of a causal effect. Two intervals with the same centre and different widths therefore differ in exactly one thing — the wider one has the larger margin of error.
- **Estimating a population mean: the interval is $\bar{x} \pm t^{*}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$, centred at $\bar{x}$.** The sample mean $\bar{x}$ is the point estimate of the unknown population mean $\mu$ — not $\hat{p}$, not $z$, not $\sigma$. The critical value is $t^{*}$ rather than $z^{*}$ because you had to estimate the spread with $s$; that extra uncertainty fattens the tails, and $t$ with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom is the correction. Worked: $n = 36$, $\bar{x} = 68$, $s = 12$ gives a standard error of $12/6 = 2$, and $t^{*} \approx 2.03$ at 95% with 35 degrees of freedom, so the margin is $4.06$ and the interval is $(63.94, 72.06)$. With $\sigma$ genuinely known you would use $z^{*} = 1.96$ instead, and as $n$ grows $t^{*}$ converges to $z^{*}$ anyway.
- **Estimating a population proportion: $\hat{p} = x/n$, and the interval is $\hat{p} \pm z^{*}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}$.** The sample proportion $\hat{p}$ is the point estimate of the unknown population proportion $p$, and it is a count over a sample size: 48 of 80 people preferring option A gives $\hat{p} = 48/80 = 0.60$ — not 0.48, which is the count misread as a rate, and not 0.80, which is the sample size. Here $z^{*}$ is correct rather than $t^{*}$, because a yes/no variable has no separate spread parameter to estimate: its variance $p(1-p)$ is determined by its mean. Worked: 87 successes in $n = 145$ gives $\hat{p} = 0.60$, standard error $\sqrt{0.6(0.4)/145} = 0.0407$, margin $1.96(0.0407) = 0.080$, interval $(0.520, 0.680)$.
- **Three levers move the width, and only one of them is free.** Sample size enters as $\sqrt{n}$: quadruple $n$ and the margin halves, so going from $n = 100$ to $n = 400$ turns a margin of 8 into 4. Confidence level enters through the critical value: $1.645$ at 90%, $1.960$ at 95%, $2.576$ at 99%, so buying more capture buys a wider, vaguer interval from the same data. Variability enters through $s$ or $\sigma$: noisier data, wider interval. So for a fixed sample the tradeoff is stark — **higher confidence yields a wider interval**, and the only way to have both high confidence and precision is more data.
- **Shifting and stretching are different movements.** If $\bar{x}$ rises from 72 to 76 while the margin stays 3, the interval moves from $(69, 75)$ to $(73, 79)$: it shifts upward, same width. If instead the margin grows from 3 to 5 with $\bar{x}$ fixed, it becomes $(67, 77)$: same centre, wider. Questions test these one at a time, and mixing them up is how "shifts upward" gets answered with "gets wider".
- **The conditions are what make any of this true.** The sample must be random — a biased sampling method shifts the centre, and no margin of error accounts for that. Sampling without replacement needs $n$ below about 10% of the population for independence. For a mean, the sampling distribution must be near-normal: a normal population, or $n \gtrsim 30$ by the central limit theorem. For a proportion, the working rule is at least 10 successes and 10 failures. Watch it fail: 3 of 20 gives $\hat{p} = 0.15$, a margin of $1.96\sqrt{0.15(0.85)/20} = 0.157$, and the interval $(-0.007, 0.307)$ — a negative proportion, which is the arithmetic telling you the normal approximation has broken down.

**Rules to remember**

- The parameter is fixed; the interval is random. Confidence describes the procedure, not the number you computed.
- 95% confidence $=$ about 95% of such intervals capture the parameter. Nothing about 95% of the population or of the sample values.
- Interval $=$ point estimate $\pm$ margin of error. Width $= 2 \times$ margin. Lower endpoint first.
- Margin of error $=$ critical value $\times$ standard error.
- Means centre at $\bar{x}$ and use $s/\sqrt{n}$; proportions centre at $\hat{p} = x/n$ and use $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$.
- Holding confidence fixed, larger $n$ $\Rightarrow$ narrower. Holding the sample fixed, higher confidence $\Rightarrow$ wider. More variability $\Rightarrow$ wider.
- Precision costs data quadratically: halving the margin needs four times the sample.
- Same centre, one wider $\Rightarrow$ the wider one has the larger margin of error. It cannot be anything else.
- Move the centre and the interval shifts; change the margin and it stretches.
- A margin of error covers random sampling error only — never bias from a bad sample.

**Common pitfalls**

- **"There is a 95% probability $\mu$ is in $(493.2, 512.8)$."** Both endpoints and $\mu$ are now fixed numbers; the probability is 0 or 1 and you cannot tell which.
- **Reading the interval as a range for individuals.** An interval for a mean shrinks as $n$ grows; the spread of the population does not.
- **Confusing the count with the proportion.** 48 of 80 is $\hat{p} = 0.6$; reporting 0.48 or 0.80 is the classic slip.
- **Reporting endpoints in the wrong order,** or reporting $(\text{estimate}, \text{margin})$ — $(72, 3)$ is the ingredients, not the interval.
- **Believing a bigger sample makes the interval "more confident".** It makes it narrower at the *same* confidence; the level is yours to choose, not something $n$ improves.
- **Raising the confidence level to make a result look stronger.** It only makes the claim vaguer — at 99% you might report $(0.30, 0.90)$, which excludes nothing interesting.
- **Skipping the conditions.** Non-random sampling, dependent draws, or fewer than 10 successes and 10 failures make the arithmetic run and the answer mean nothing.
- **Treating the margin of error as a bound on all error.** It is a bound on sampling variability, at the stated capture rate, and nothing else.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For any interpretation question, ask what is random. If an option treats the parameter as random or the interval as fixed, it is the distractor.
2. For "what happens to the width", identify which single lever moved. $n$ up $\Rightarrow$ narrower; confidence up $\Rightarrow$ wider; variability up $\Rightarrow$ wider. Only one changes per question.
3. For "what is it centred at", answer with the point estimate — $\bar{x}$ for a mean, $\hat{p}$ for a proportion. Never the margin, the standard deviation, or the confidence level.
4. For assembly questions, do estimate $-$ margin, then estimate $+$ margin, and write them in that order: $72 \pm 3 \to (69, 75)$; $0.6 \pm 0.08 \to (0.52, 0.68)$.
5. For proportion arithmetic, divide the successes by the sample size before anything else, and sanity-check that the answer lies between 0 and 1.
6. Distinguish a shift from a stretch by comparing centres and widths separately.
7. Distrust any option containing "exactly", "guaranteed", or "every sample" — capture is a long-run rate with exceptions built in.

**Where this leads**: the next lesson, **12 Significance tests (hypothesis testing) (khanacademy.org)**, keeps this machinery and changes the question. Instead of asking which parameter values are plausible, it names one value in advance and asks how surprising your data would be if it were true — adding null and alternative hypotheses, $P$-values, significance levels, and the two ways a decision can be wrong. The link is tight: a value outside a 95% interval is exactly a value a two-sided test would reject at $\alpha = 0.05$, so everything you have just built is the same object seen from the other side.
