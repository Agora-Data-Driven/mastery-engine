1. Flip The Curve

You already own the curve on top: the sampling distribution of a sample mean, centred at μ with standard error σ/sqrt(n). It says where x-bar lands when you know μ. Drag your sample's x-bar and watch the same inequality read backwards, as an interval that either reaches μ or does not. Like a tethered dog: if the dog is within 10 m of the post, the post is within 10 m of the dog — one fact, two ways of saying it.

In real work μ is never on the screen — that is the whole reason the interval exists. The dashed line is drawn here only so you can audit the machinery once. Notice what is not random: the width. Every sample of this size produces the same margin of error; only the centre moves. So the procedure's capture rate is decided before you collect a single observation.

2. Three Dials, One Width

Every margin of error is critical value × standard error, so exactly three things can move it: how much data you have, how noisy it is, and how much confidence you demand. Two of them cost money. Like a camera: more light and a steadier hand sharpen the picture, but you can always cheat a blurry shot by claiming less about what is in it.

Only one of these three dials buys real information. More data genuinely shrinks the uncertainty; raising the confidence level shrinks nothing — it widens the claim until it is safe, which is why a 99% interval can be so wide that it rules nothing out. And σ is not a dial at all in most projects: it is a property of the thing you are measuring, which you can only change by measuring something cleaner.

3. Shift Or Stretch

An interval has exactly two degrees of freedom: where its centre is, and how far the margin of error reaches from that centre. Move one at a time and watch which of the two things happens — they are tested separately and confused constantly. Like a spotlight on a stage: you can walk it left and right, or open the iris wider. Never mistake one for the other.

The centre is the point estimate — x-bar for a mean, p-hat for a proportion. It is never the margin of error, never the sample standard deviation, never the confidence level. And the pair you report is the two endpoints, smallest first: (72, 3) is the estimate sitting next to its margin, which is the ingredients list rather than the interval.

4. Counts To A Proportion

A proportion starts life as two counts. Divide, then let the point estimate supply its own standard error — a yes/no variable has no separate spread to estimate, because p decides the variance by itself. Push the counts to the edges and watch the approximation break in public. Like a poll: it is only ever "so many said yes out of so many asked", and everything else is arithmetic on those two numbers.

Everything from visual 2 carries over unchanged — larger n narrows, higher confidence widens — but the noise term is no longer free to vary. It is sqrt(p-hat(1 − p-hat)), which peaks at p-hat = 0.5 and shrinks toward the edges. A near-unanimous result is estimated more precisely than a split one from exactly the same number of people, right up until there are too few of the rare outcome for any of this to apply.

5. Fifty Intervals, One Truth

Fifty independent samples, one fixed μ, one procedure. Each sample gets its own interval; some reach μ and some do not, and no interval knows which kind it is. Redraw the batch and watch the capture count wobble around the level you asked for. Like a factory that stamps rings for a peg: the machine's 95% is about the machine, and tells you nothing about the ring in your hand.

This is the judgment the whole lesson turns on. Confidence is a property of the procedure, measured over repetitions you will never actually run — and you get exactly one sample, with no way to tell a hit from a miss. That is not a weakness to apologise for; it is the honest shape of inference from a sample, and it is what makes the next lesson's error probabilities meaningful.
