**The big idea**: **Introduction to confidence intervals**, **Estimating a population mean** and **Estimating a population proportion** taught you to turn one sample into a range of plausible parameter values. A significance test runs that machinery in the opposite direction: instead of asking *which parameter values are plausible given my sample*, it pins one specific claim about the parameter and asks *how surprising my sample would be if that claim were true*. Every part is already yours — **Sampling distribution of a sample mean** and **Sampling distribution of a sample proportion** supply the reference distribution, **Z-scores** convert a distance into standard errors, and **Normal distributions and the empirical rule** turn that reading into a probability. The five topics organised under this lesson in your progress tree, each tagged `khanacademy.org` there — **The idea of significance tests**, **Error probabilities and power**, **Tests about a population proportion**, **Tests about a population mean**, and **More significance testing videos** — are that one question asked about different parameters, with growing care about what the answer does and does not license you to say.

**Key concepts**

- **The null hypothesis is a claim of no effect or no difference, and it is the only hypothesis you can compute with.** $H_0$ names a *single* value — $p = 0.5$, $\mu = 100$ — which pins the sampling distribution completely, so probabilities become calculable. The alternative $H_a$ ("the coin is biased") names a whole range of values and determines nothing, which is why it cannot be the thing you test against. The researcher's own claim is normally the alternative, never the null: "the drug helps somewhat" gives you no distribution to measure surprise against.
- **The purpose of the test is to weigh evidence against $H_0$ — not to estimate, and never to prove.** Estimation was the previous lesson's job and it returns an interval; a test returns a verdict on one claim. The logic is proof by contradiction with probability in place of certainty: assume $H_0$, and reject only if your data are too odd to live with in that world. Since "too odd" is never "impossible", the conclusion is always about evidence, never about truth.
- **The p-value is the probability of a result at least as extreme as the one observed, computed assuming $H_0$ is true.** It measures how surprising the data are *if the null holds*. Worked example: flip a coin $n = 100$ times under $H_0: p = 0.5$ and see 60 heads. The standard error is $\sqrt{0.5 \times 0.5 / 100} = 0.05$, so $\hat{p} = 0.60$ sits $z = (0.60 - 0.50)/0.05 = 2.0$ standard errors out, and the two-sided p-value is $2 \times P(Z > 2.0) \approx 0.046$. Read it as: about 4.6% of fair-coin samples are this far from 0.5 or further.
- **What the p-value is not.** Not the probability that $H_0$ is true — that needs a distribution over hypotheses, which this framework has not got. Not the probability that the data prove a causal link; causation comes from the *design*, not from any statistic. And not the confidence level, which you fixed in advance and which never depends on the data.
- **Small p-value ⇒ strong evidence against $H_0$; large p-value ⇒ weak evidence against it.** The direction never inverts: a smaller p means the null world produces data like yours more rarely. A p-value of 0.80 says 80% of samples from a true null are at least this extreme — unremarkable, and therefore *weak evidence against $H_0$*. It is not evidence *for* $H_0$: an underpowered study of a real effect gives large p-values routinely.
- **The significance level $\alpha$ is the threshold, and it must be chosen before the data are examined.** The rule is mechanical: if $p < \alpha$, reject $H_0$; otherwise fail to reject. At $\alpha = 0.05$, a p-value of 0.02 rejects and one of 0.12 does not. $\alpha$ is the one quantity fixed in advance — the p-value, the test statistic and the sample mean are all outputs. Pick $\alpha$ after seeing $p$ and the error rate it exists to control becomes meaningless.
- **The wording of both verdicts is load-bearing.** Rejecting $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ means the data give enough evidence against $H_0$ at that level — not that $H_0$ is false for certain, nor that the alternative now holds in every population. Failing to reject means the sample lacks enough evidence against $H_0$ — not that $H_0$ is true, not that the alternative is disproven, and not that $p = 1$. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
- **A Type I error is rejecting a null that is true, and its long-run rate is exactly $\alpha$.** The rejection region *is* the $\alpha$ most extreme outcomes of the null distribution, so a true null lands there with probability $\alpha$ by construction. Run 200 tests at $\alpha = 0.05$ on nulls that are all true and expect about 10 rejections — every one a false alarm.
- **A Type II error is failing to reject a null that is false; its rate is $\beta$, and power $= 1 - \beta$ is the probability of rejecting a false null.** Worked example: $H_0: \mu = 100$, truth $\mu = 104$, $\sigma = 12$, $n = 36$, so $SE = 12/\sqrt{36} = 2$. A two-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$ rejects when $\bar{x} > 103.92$ or $\bar{x} < 96.08$. Under the truth, $P(\bar{x} > 103.92) = P(Z > -0.04) \approx 0.52$: power is only about 52%, so $\beta \approx 0.48$. A real 4-point effect would be missed roughly half the time.
- **Sample size is the main lever on power, because it shrinks the standard error.** Take the same effect to $n = 144$: $SE = 1$, the cutoff falls to 101.96, and power becomes $P(Z > -2.04) \approx 0.98$. Increasing $n$ increases power, always, holding everything else fixed. Three other levers exist — a larger true effect, less variability, and a larger $\alpha$ — but that last one buys power by accepting more Type I errors, which is why the two error probabilities are studied together.
- **Tests about a population proportion: the parameter named in $H_0$ is the population proportion $p$.** The quantity computed directly from the data is the sample proportion $\hat{p}$; $p_0$ and $\alpha$ are stated by you, not measured. The statistic is $z = (\hat{p} - p_0)\big/\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}$ — the standard error uses $p_0$, because every probability here is computed *in the null world*. Conditions: random sample, $n$ under 10% of the population, at least 10 expected successes and failures (50 of each above).
- **That z statistic is literally a count of standard errors, which is why distance drives the p-value.** With $p_0 = 0.5$, $n = 100$: $\hat{p} = 0.55$ gives $z = 1$, $p \approx 0.32$; $\hat{p} = 0.60$ gives $z = 2$, $p \approx 0.046$; $\hat{p} = 0.70$ gives $z = 4$, $p \approx 0.00006$. When the observed $\hat{p}$ is many standard errors from $p_0$, the p-value is small — monotonically, no exceptions.
- **Tests about a population mean: the parameter is $\mu$, the point estimate is the sample mean $\bar{x}$, and the statistic is $t = (\bar{x} - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt{n})$ with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom.** It is a t-test rather than a z-test because $\sigma$ is unknown and $s$ estimates it, adding uncertainty that the heavier tails of the t distribution absorb. Worked example: $\mu_0 = 100$, $\bar{x} = 104$, $s = 12$, $n = 36$ gives $SE = 2$ and $t = 2.00$ on 35 df, two-sided $p \approx 0.053$ — just above 0.05, so you fail to reject. A large *positive* statistic means the sample mean lies far above the null value; a large negative one, far below.
- **Holding the effect size fixed, a larger sample pushes the p-value down.** Same 4-point gap, same $s = 12$, but $n = 144$: $SE = 1$, $t = 4.00$, $p \approx 0.0001$. The standard error falls like $1/\sqrt{n}$, so the identical effect is measured against a finer ruler. The corollary matters in practice: a tiny, useless effect becomes "significant" at large enough $n$, so statistical significance is not practical importance.
- **A two-sided alternative is sensitive to differences in either direction; a one-sided one is not.** $H_a: \mu \neq \mu_0$ counts extreme results in both tails, so at $z = 2.0$ the p-value is $0.046$; $H_a: \mu > \mu_0$ counts one tail, $p = 0.023$; and $H_a: \mu < \mu_0$ gives $p = 0.977$ on that same sample — a one-sided test can never reject on evidence pointing the wrong way. Like $\alpha$, the direction is chosen before the data.
- **The four-step ritual behind every worked example in More significance testing videos.** State $H_0$, $H_a$ and $\alpha$; check the conditions; compute the statistic and its p-value; conclude *in context*, in the language of evidence. Skipping step four is where most marks, and most real-world claims, are lost.

**Rules to remember**

- $H_0$ is always a statement of no effect or no difference, at one specific parameter value; the researcher's claim is the alternative.
- p-value $= P(\text{data at least this extreme} \mid H_0 \text{ true})$. Never $P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$.
- Small p ⇒ strong evidence against $H_0$. Large p ⇒ weak evidence against it, and no evidence for it.
- $p < \alpha$ ⇒ reject $H_0$; $p \geq \alpha$ ⇒ fail to reject. Never "accept" or "prove".
- $\alpha$ and the direction of $H_a$ are chosen before looking at the data; everything else is computed from them.
- Type I = reject a true null (rate $\alpha$). Type II = fail to reject a false null (rate $\beta$). Power $= 1 - \beta$.
- Increasing $n$ increases power. Increasing $\alpha$ increases power *and* Type I error.
- Proportion test: parameter $p$, estimate $\hat{p}$, $z$ standard errors from $p_0$. Mean test: parameter $\mu$, estimate $\bar{x}$, $t$ on $n-1$ df.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Reading the p-value as the probability that $H_0$ is true.** It is computed *assuming* $H_0$; it cannot also be a verdict on it.
- **Writing "accept $H_0$".** Failing to reject is a statement about your sample's evidence, not about the world.
- **Treating a large p-value as proof of no effect.** With $n = 36$ and a real 4-point effect, the test misses it about half the time.
- **Choosing $\alpha$, or the one-sided direction, after seeing the data.** Both must be fixed in advance or the stated error rate is fiction.
- **Confusing $\hat{p}$ with $p_0$, or $\bar{x}$ with $\mu$.** Hatted and barred symbols come from the sample; plain Greek letters are the population claims.
- **Using $\hat{p}$ in the standard error of a proportion test.** The test's null distribution is built at $p_0$; the confidence interval is the one that uses $\hat{p}$.
- **Reporting significance as importance.** At $n = 100{,}000$ a 0.1% difference clears any $\alpha$ you like and may be worth nothing.
- **Forgetting the conditions.** Random sampling, the 10% rule and large enough counts are what make the normal or t reference distribution legitimate.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Identify the parameter first. "Proportion", "percent", "share" ⇒ $p$ and $\hat{p}$, z-test; "mean", "average" ⇒ $\mu$ and $\bar{x}$, t-test. Options naming $r$ or $\sigma$ are distractors.
2. For a decision question, compare $p$ to $\alpha$; the answer is reject, or fail to reject. Any option containing "accept", "proven" or "disproven" is wrong on wording alone.
3. For error questions, recite the three short sentences — Type I *rejects a true null*, Type II *fails to reject a false null*, power *rejects a false null* — and match one to the option.
4. When a question changes one input, trace it through the standard error: bigger $n$ ⇒ smaller $SE$ ⇒ bigger statistic ⇒ smaller p-value ⇒ more power.
5. Watch for the "chosen before the data" family. Only $\alpha$, the hypotheses and the direction qualify; the statistic, the p-value and the sample mean are outputs.
6. Distrust any option upgrading a result to certainty or to causation. The honest sentences are always about evidence *at a level*.
