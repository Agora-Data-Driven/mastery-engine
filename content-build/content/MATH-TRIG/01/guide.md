**The big idea**: This lesson has no prior lesson to build on, so it builds on you — specifically on **similar triangles** and the **ratio-and-proportion** reasoning from **college algebra**, on the **Pythagorean theorem**, on **inverse functions** from precalculus, and on the angle-from-a-dot-product habit you already have from **linear algebra** (cosine similarity) and from `atan2` in **Python**. Here is the observation that makes everything else inevitable. Fix one acute angle of a right triangle and you have fixed its *shape*: any other right triangle with that angle is similar to it, so all three sides scale by the same factor $k$ and the *ratio* of any two sides is unchanged. A quantity that depends on the angle and on nothing else is a function of the angle — and sine, cosine and tangent are exactly those functions. That one fact buys three abilities: from an angle plus one side, get any other side; from two sides, get the angle back by inverting; and from a sketch of something real, get a number. Everything downstream — the unit circle, rotation matrices, Fourier — is this definition wearing different clothes.

**Key concepts**

- **Introduction to the trigonometric ratios: each ratio is defined by *which two sides* it compares.** For an acute angle $\theta$ in a right triangle, $\sin\theta = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$, $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$, $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$ — **SOH-CAH-TOA**, whose entire usefulness is that it pairs each ratio with the correct two sides. "Opposite" and "adjacent" are named *relative to* $\theta$ and swap when you look from the other acute angle; the hypotenuse faces the right angle and never changes role. Worked: opposite 3, hypotenuse 5, so $\sin\theta = 3/5 = 0.6$; Pythagoras gives the third side as 4, so $\cos\theta = 4/5$ and $\tan\theta = 3/4$.
- **Scale invariance is the mechanism — the ratios are functions of the angle, not of the triangle.** Double that triangle to 6-8-10 and $\sin\theta = 6/10 = 0.6$, unchanged; treble it to 9-12-15 and it is still $0.6$. Without this there would be no such thing as "the sine of 37°", only "the sine of *this* triangle", and no table, no calculator key and no closed-form answer would exist.
- **Solving for a side in a right triangle: name the two sides in play, then pick the one ratio that relates exactly those two.** You know $\theta$ and the adjacent side and you want the hypotenuse: that pair is adjacent-and-hypotenuse, so the ratio is $\cos\theta$, and $\text{hyp} = \text{adj}/\cos\theta$. One equation, one unknown, then algebra. Worked: $\tan\theta = 3/4$ with adjacent 8 gives opposite $= 8 \times 3/4 = 6$; $\sin\theta = 3/5$ with hypotenuse 10 gives opposite $= 10 \times 0.6 = 6$. The failure mode is not arithmetic — it is assigning the sides to the wrong parts of the ratio, which produces a clean, plausible, wrong number.
- **Solving for an angle in a right triangle: build the ratio from the two known sides, then apply an inverse trigonometric function.** $\sin$, $\cos$ and $\tan$ map angle $\to$ ratio; restricted to acute angles each is one-to-one, so each has an inverse mapping ratio $\to$ angle. Worked: opposite 7, adjacent 24, so $\tan\theta = 7/24$ and $\theta = \tan^{-1}(7/24) = 16.26°$. Worked: opposite 3, hypotenuse 5, so $\theta = \sin^{-1}(0.6) = 36.87° \approx 37°$. The signature error is pressing the forward key: $\tan(7/24)$ in degree mode is $\tan(0.2917°) = 0.00509$, a small decimal that is a *ratio*, not an angle. Same for $\cos\theta = 3/7 \Rightarrow \theta = \cos^{-1}(3/7) = 64.62°$, and for $\sin A = 0.7071 \Rightarrow A = \sin^{-1}(0.7071) = 45°$.
- **Sine and cosine of complementary angles: the "co-" is literal.** The angles of a triangle sum to $180°$, so in a right triangle the two acute angles are **complementary** — they sum to $90°$. Now look at the same triangle from the other acute angle: the side *opposite* $\theta$ is the side *adjacent* to $90° - \theta$. Identical sides, different naming, therefore $\sin\theta = \cos(90° - \theta)$ and $\cos\theta = \sin(90° - \theta)$. Worked: $\sin\theta = 4/5 \Rightarrow \cos(90° - \theta) = 4/5$; $\sin 25° = \cos 65° = 0.4226$; $\cos 60° = \sin 30° = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Note carefully what this does **not** say: $\sin\theta = \cos\theta$ holds at $\theta = 45°$ only, where the angle *is* its own complement.
- **The reciprocal trigonometric ratios (Cosecant, Secant, Cotangent) flip the fraction.** $\csc\theta = 1/\sin\theta = \text{hyp}/\text{opp}$, $\sec\theta = 1/\cos\theta = \text{hyp}/\text{adj}$, $\cot\theta = 1/\tan\theta = \text{adj}/\text{opp}$. The pairing is deliberately counter-intuitive: **se**cant goes with **co**sine and **co**secant with **si**ne. Worked: $\sin\theta = 3/5 \Rightarrow \csc\theta = 5/3$; $\cos\theta = 12/13 \Rightarrow \sec\theta = 13/12$; $\tan\theta = 7 \Rightarrow \cot\theta = 1/7$. They add no new geometry — they exist so that a side you want can sit in the numerator, which keeps an equation linear instead of buried in a denominator. Each is undefined wherever its partner is zero.
- **Modeling with right triangles: sketch and label what is known, *then* choose a ratio.** A right-triangle model fits any situation where a $90°$ angle links sides and angles — a ladder against a wall (the wall and the ground are the two perpendicular legs, the ladder is the hypotenuse), a ramp (a 3-4-5 ramp has hypotenuse exactly 5), a sight line to the top of a tower. The classic: from an **angle of elevation** and the horizontal distance to the base you know the adjacent side and want the opposite one, which is the tangent. Worked: $d = 40$ m at $\alpha = 32°$ gives height $= 40\tan 32° = 25.0$ m. Sketching first is what stops you solving a correctly-set-up equation for the wrong side.

**Rules to remember**

- $\sin = \text{opp}/\text{hyp}$, $\cos = \text{adj}/\text{hyp}$, $\tan = \text{opp}/\text{adj}$. Opposite and adjacent are relative to the angle; the hypotenuse is absolute.
- The ratio depends only on the angle. Scaling a triangle changes every length and no ratio.
- Two sides known $\Rightarrow$ inverse function. One side and one angle known $\Rightarrow$ forward function.
- $\sin\theta = \cos(90° - \theta)$, and the acute angles of a right triangle always sum to $90°$.
- $\csc = 1/\sin$, $\sec = 1/\cos$, $\cot = 1/\tan$ — and $\sec$ partners $\cos$, not $\sin$.
- $\tan^{-1}(x)$ is an inverse function, never $1/\tan(x)$; that is $\cot(x)$.
- Check degree versus radian mode before you trust any number a calculator hands back.
- Model first, solve second: sketch, label the given sides relative to the angle, then choose the ratio.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Assigning the sides to the wrong parts of the ratio.** The commonest wrong answer in the whole lesson, and the arithmetic never complains.
- **Pressing the forward key when you wanted the inverse.** $\theta = \tan(7/10)$ returns $0.0122$; a "very small angle" that appears from nowhere is the tell.
- **Reading a ratio as an angle.** $\sin A = 0.4$ is not $A = 0.4°$, and dividing both sides by $0.4$ does nothing useful — only $\sin^{-1}$ isolates $A$.
- **Believing $\sin\theta = \cos\theta$ generally.** It is true at $45°$ and nowhere else in the acute range; the real identity involves the *complement*.
- **Confusing $\cot$ with $\tan^{-1}$, or $\csc$ with $\sin^{-1}$.** Reciprocal and inverse are unrelated operations that share sloppy notation.
- **Forgetting the hypotenuse is the longest side.** $\cos\theta = 12/13$ is consistent as (adjacent 12, hypotenuse 13); (adjacent 13, hypotenuse 12) describes no triangle.
- **Rounding intermediate results.** Round once, at the end; rounding a mid-problem side quietly moves the final angle.
- **Reaching for the law of cosines, the Pythagorean theorem or logarithms** when the setup already reduces to one trigonometric equation in one unknown.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Ask what is given. Two sides means an inverse function; a side and an angle means a forward one. That single fork answers a large share of the bank.
2. Name the two sides relative to the stated angle *before* choosing sin, cos or tan. The named pair selects the ratio uniquely.
3. When an answer choice applies a forward function to a ratio, such as $\theta = \tan(5/12)$, it is wrong by construction — the argument of a trig function is an angle.
4. On complementary-angle items, look for the pair whose measures sum to $90°$ and whose function names differ: $\sin 25°$ and $\cos 65°$.
5. On reciprocal items, invert the fraction and stop — $\cos\theta = 12/13$ gives $\sec\theta = 13/12$, not $13/5$.
6. On word problems, sketch it. Decide which leg is the wall, which is the ground, and where the angle sits; the ratio then follows without any further thought.
