1. Ratios Not Lengths

You already own similar triangles: fix one acute angle and the shape is fixed, so every side scales by the same factor. Drag the legs to change the shape — then drag the scale and watch six lengths move while three ratios refuse to. Like a photo enlargement: everything gets bigger, and nobody's face gets wider.

This is why a calculator can have a sin key at all. If the ratio depended on the triangle rather than only on the angle, there would be no such number as "the sine of 37°" — only "the sine of this triangle", and no table, no key and no closed form could exist. Everything in the next three visuals is bookkeeping on top of this one fact.

2. Pick The Ratio

Solving for a side is one decision followed by algebra. Name the side you have and the side you want; exactly one ratio relates that pair. Pick a pair, then watch all three ratios answer and only one be right. Like choosing a spanner by the bolt: three will turn, one actually fits.

Notice what never happens: the arithmetic does not complain. Feed a well-formed equation the wrong pair of sides and it returns a clean, plausible, wrong number with no warning attached. That is why the discipline is name the two sides first, choose the ratio second — and why "assigning the sides to the wrong parts of the ratio" is the most common wrong answer in this whole lesson.

3. Inverse Undoes It

Two sides known and the angle missing reverses the machine: build the ratio, then apply an inverse trigonometric function. Drag the legs, switch which pair you were handed, and compare the inverse key against the forward key pressed by mistake. Like a postcode lookup: the address gives you the code, and you need the reverse directory to go back.

Two notations get confused here and they are unrelated operations. sin-1 is the inverse function — feed it a ratio, it returns an angle. csc is the reciprocal ratio, 1/sin, and returns a ratio. Visual 4 is the reciprocals; the superscript −1 next to a trig name never means "one over" in this course.

4. Flip Or Complement

Six ratios, three sides, one triangle. Two different moves generate the extra names: flipping a fraction gives cosecant, secant and cotangent, and walking to the other acute corner gives the complementary-angle identities. Switch between them on the same triangle. Like a photograph and its negative versus the same photograph seen from the other side of the room.

Neither move adds geometry. The complement identities are a relabelling — the same physical side is "opposite" one acute angle and "adjacent" to the other — and the reciprocals are a rearrangement, so the side you want sits on top of the fraction and the equation stays linear. Every one of the six values is still opposite, adjacent or hypotenuse over one of the other two.

5. Ladder Ramp Elevation

Every right-triangle word problem is the same four moves: sketch it, label what is known relative to the angle, choose the one ratio, solve. Pick a scenario and drag its givens — the sketch, the labels and the chosen ratio all follow. Like reading a recipe before turning on the hob: the sketch is where the problem is actually solved.

A right-triangle model is appropriate whenever a genuine 90° angle links the sides and the angles — a wall meeting the ground, a rise over a run, a plumb line against a sight line. It is the wrong tool for anything with no right angle in it (that is lesson 03's law of sines and law of cosines) and for anything cyclical rather than static. Sketching first is what stops you solving a perfectly correct equation for the wrong side.
