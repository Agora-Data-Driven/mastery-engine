**The big idea**: **Introduction to the trigonometric ratios** defined sine, cosine and tangent as ratios of sides, and **Solving for a side in a right triangle**, **Solving for an angle in a right triangle**, **Sine and cosine of complementary angles** and **The reciprocal trigonometric ratios (Cosecant, Secant, Cotangent)** made those ratios into a working toolkit — one that stops dead at $90^\circ$. **Modeling with right triangles** was the ceiling of that idea: one static shape, one answer. This lesson removes the ceiling. Put the angle at the centre of a circle of radius $1$ and the ratios become *coordinates*. Coordinates exist for every angle, so sine and cosine stop being ratios attached to a triangle and become **functions of a real number**. Functions can be graphed, stretched, shifted and fitted, which is how a right-triangle tool ends up describing tides, daylight and a Ferris wheel.

**Key concepts**

- **Radians & Degrees: measure an angle by the arc it cuts, not by an arbitrary 360.** A radian is the angle whose arc length equals the radius: $\theta = s/r$. One full turn has arc $2\pi r$, so it is $2\pi r / r = 2\pi$ radians — and also $360^\circ$. Halving both gives the only conversion worth memorising, $\pi \text{ rad} = 180^\circ$. Everything else is that fraction: degrees to radians multiply by $\pi/180$, radians to degrees multiply by $180/\pi$. Worked: $210^\circ \times \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{210\pi}{180} = \frac{7\pi}{6}$; $60^\circ \to \frac{60\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{3}$; and $\frac{\pi}{4} \times \frac{180}{\pi} = 45^\circ$. Why bother: a radian is a pure number, a length over a length, which is what lets $s = r\theta$ hold with no stray constant.
- **Unit circle introduction: the definition that survives past $90^\circ$.** Draw the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ — radius exactly $1$, centred at the origin — and measure $\theta$ counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis. The point where the terminal ray meets the circle is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$: **cosine is the $x$-coordinate, sine is the $y$-coordinate**, in that order. For an acute $\theta$ this is not a new definition at all; drop a perpendicular to the $x$-axis and you have your old right triangle with hypotenuse $1$, so $\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{1} = y$ and $\cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{1} = x$. Worked: $\theta = 0$ gives $(1, 0)$, so $\cos 0 = 1$ and $\sin 0 = 0$; $\theta = \pi/2$ gives $(0, 1)$; and a point at $\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac12\right)$ has $\sin\theta = \frac12$ — read the $y$, not the larger-looking number.
- **Trig values of special angles ($\pi/6, \pi/4, \pi/3$) come from two triangles you already own.** Scale the $45$–$45$–$90$ triangle (legs $1,1$, hypotenuse $\sqrt2$) to hypotenuse $1$: both legs become $\frac{1}{\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$, so $\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$. Scale the $30$–$60$–$90$ triangle (sides $1, \sqrt3, 2$) to hypotenuse $1$ and the legs become $\frac12$ and $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$. The short leg faces the small angle, so $\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$, $\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$, $\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$, $\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac12$. Tangent is the quotient: $\tan\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3/2}{1/2} = \sqrt3$. The swap between $\pi/6$ and $\pi/3$ is exactly **Sine and cosine of complementary angles** again, $\sin\theta = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)$ — so asking which first-quadrant angle has cosine $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ means asking which one has sine $\frac12$, and the answer is $\pi/6$.
- **The Pythagorean identity is the circle's equation wearing trigonometric clothes.** The point $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ lies on $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, so $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ for **every** angle — not just acute ones, and it restates the unit-circle equation rather than a fact about one triangle. Its working use is completing a pair: given one function and a quadrant, the other follows. Worked: if $\cos x = \frac{5}{13}$ and $x$ is acute, then $\sin^2 x = 1 - \frac{25}{169} = \frac{144}{169}$, so $\sin x = \frac{12}{13}$, positive because acute angles sit in the first quadrant. Given $\sin x = \frac35$ acute, $\cos x = \sqrt{1 - \frac{9}{25}} = \frac45$. Rearranged it is also a rewriting rule: $1 - \sin^2 x = \cos^2 x$.
- **Graphs of $\sin(x)$, $\cos(x)$, and $\tan(x)$: unroll the circle along a time axis.** Walk around the unit circle and plot the height $y$ against the angle and you get $\sin x$ — starting at $\sin 0 = 0$, peaking at $1$, back through zero, down to $-1$, closing after $2\pi$. Plot the horizontal coordinate instead and you get $\cos x$, the identical wave started at its peak, $\cos 0 = 1$. Both are trapped in $[-1, 1]$, so both have amplitude $1$, and both repeat every $2\pi$. Tangent is different in kind: $\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \frac{y}{x}$ is the *slope* of the terminal ray, so it is undefined wherever $\cos x = 0$ and its graph has **vertical asymptotes** at $x = \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi$ — it is the only one of the three that does. A line through the origin also looks the same after half a turn, so the period of $y = \tan(x)$ is $\pi$, not $2\pi$.
- **Amplitude, midline, and period of sinusoidal functions are three independent dials.** In $y = A\sin(Bx) + D$: the **midline** is $y = D$, the line the wave oscillates about; the **amplitude** is $|A|$, the distance from the midline up to a peak (half the peak-to-trough height, never negative); and the **period** is $\frac{2\pi}{|B|}$, because $Bx$ has to travel $2\pi$ for the wave to close, so $x$ only travels $2\pi/B$. Worked: $y = -2\sin(4x) + 1$ has midline $y = 1$, amplitude $|-2| = 2$, period $\frac{2\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}$, and therefore range $[-1, 3]$. $y = 3\cos(x) - 2$ has amplitude $3$ — the $-2$ moves the wave, it does not shrink it. $y = \sin(2x)$ has period $\frac{2\pi}{2} = \pi$: doubling $B$ halves the period.
- **Transforming and graphing sinusoidal functions: read $y = A\sin\big(B(x - C)\big) + D$ outside-in.** $A$ stretches vertically and, if negative, reflects across the $x$-axis; $B$ compresses horizontally; $D$ slides the whole wave up; $C$ slides it right. Worked: $y = -\sin(x)$ is $y = \sin(x)$ reflected over the $x$-axis. $y = \sin(x) - 4$ is shifted down $4$. $y = \sin\left(x - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ is shifted **right** by $\frac{\pi}{2}$ — subtracting inside the function moves it in the positive direction, because $x$ must now be $\frac{\pi}{2}$ larger to reproduce the same output. Extremes follow from the midline: $y = 2\cos(x) + 1$ has midline $1$ and amplitude $2$, so its maximum is $1 + 2 = 3$ and its minimum $-1$. Graphing by hand is then mechanical: draw the midline, mark $\pm$ amplitude, quarter one period, place the shape.
- **Modeling with sinusoidal functions (including phase shift): read the four constants off the data.** A sinusoidal model is right when a data set shows **repeating cyclical oscillation** over time — not linear growth, not a one-off exponential decay, not scatter. Fit it in four steps: $D = \frac{\max + \min}{2}$, $A = \frac{\max - \min}{2}$, $B = \frac{2\pi}{\text{period}}$, and $C$ = the horizontal offset that puts the model's peak where the data's peak actually is. In $y = A\sin(B(x - C)) + D$, that $C$ is the **phase shift** — the only constant that answers "why is the maximum late?". Worked: a wheel of period $12$ has $B = \frac{2\pi}{12} = \frac{\pi}{6}$; if it peaks at $t = 2$ rather than $t = 0$, the phase shift carries that offset while amplitude, midline and period stay untouched. And $y = 5\sin(x) + 10$ has midline $10$ and amplitude $5$, so its range is $[5, 15]$.

**Rules to remember**

- $\pi$ radians $= 180^\circ$. Degrees $\to$ radians: multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180}$. Radians $\to$ degrees: multiply by $\frac{180}{\pi}$.
- The unit circle has radius $1$, and its point at angle $\theta$ is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ — cosine first.
- $\theta = 0 \Rightarrow (1,0)$; $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} \Rightarrow (0,1)$; $\theta = \pi \Rightarrow (-1,0)$; $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{2} \Rightarrow (0,-1)$.
- $\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac12$, $\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}$, $\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$; cosine runs the same list backwards; $\tan\frac{\pi}{3} = \sqrt3$.
- $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$, always, because $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ on the unit circle.
- $\sin$ and $\cos$: amplitude $1$, period $2\pi$, no asymptotes. $\tan$: period $\pi$, vertical asymptotes where $\cos x = 0$.
- For $y = A\sin(B(x - C)) + D$: amplitude $|A|$, midline $y = D$, period $\frac{2\pi}{|B|}$, phase shift $C$, range $[D - |A|, D + |A|]$.
- Inside the parentheses acts horizontally and backwards; outside acts vertically and forwards.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Converting the wrong way.** $\frac{\pi}{180}$ shrinks a degree count into radians; $\frac{180}{\pi}$ grows a radian count into degrees. Sanity-check the size: $210^\circ$ exceeds half a turn, so its radian measure must exceed $\pi$ — and $\frac{7\pi}{6}$ does.
- **Reading the unit circle point as $(\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$.** It is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. At $\theta = \pi/2$ the point is $(0,1)$, so the swapped version would claim $\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 0$.
- **Mixing up $\pi/6$ and $\pi/3$.** The bigger angle gets the bigger sine: $\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 0.87$ against $\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = 0.5$.
- **Quoting a mangled identity** such as $\sin^2 x - \cos^2 x = 1$ or $\sec^2 x + \csc^2 x = 1$. Only the *sum* of the two squares is $1$; test a candidate at $x = 0$.
- **Taking the square root without choosing a sign.** $\sin^2 x = \frac{144}{169}$ allows $\pm\frac{12}{13}$; the quadrant, stated in the problem as "acute", picks one.
- **Calling $|A|$ the peak-to-trough distance.** Amplitude is measured from the midline, so it is *half* of that: $y = -2\sin(4x)+1$ swings through $4$ units but has amplitude $2$.
- **Reading $B$ as the period.** $y = \sin(2x)$ has period $\pi$; a bigger $B$ means a faster, shorter wave.
- **Shifting the wrong way horizontally.** $\sin(x - \frac{\pi}{2})$ goes right; $\sin(x + \frac{\pi}{2})$ goes left. Test one point rather than trusting the sign.
- **Expecting $\tan(x)$ to behave like the other two.** It has no amplitude, no maximum, and its period is half theirs.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For a conversion, write the factor first — degrees carry $\frac{\pi}{180}$, radians carry $\frac{180}{\pi}$ — then simplify; the choices are usually fractions of $\pi$.
2. For anything about a point on the circle, say "cosine is $x$, sine is $y$" out loud before answering.
3. For a special-angle value, rebuild the triangle rather than recalling a table: which is the short leg, and is it facing the angle?
4. For an identity question, decide whether you must *state* it, *justify* it (unit circle), or *use* it to complete a pair. The distractors are mangled versions of the true one.
5. For amplitude, midline and period, match each constant to its slot in $y = A\sin(B(x-C)) + D$ first. Amplitude takes an absolute value; period is $\frac{2\pi}{|B|}$; midline is $y = D$.
6. For a transformation, translate the algebra into one plain sentence — "reflected over the $x$-axis", "shifted right $\frac{\pi}{2}$" — and check it against a single easy input such as $x = 0$.
7. For a modeling question, ask what the described feature can only be: a delayed peak is a phase shift, a raised centre is the midline, a swing size is the amplitude, a cycle length is the period.

**Where this leads**: every angle here lived at the centre of a circle or inside a right triangle. The next lesson, **03 Non-right triangles & trigonometry**, drops both restrictions — the Law of sines and the Law of cosines extend these same functions to triangles with no right angle at all, which is what lets you solve general triangles and the word problems built on them. The Law of cosines is the Pythagorean theorem plus a correction term made of $\cos$, so the values you can now produce for any angle are what make it computable.
