1. From Triangle To Circle

Everything you already do — opposite over hypotenuse, adjacent over hypotenuse, and the reciprocal ratios on top of them — needs a right triangle, and a right triangle has no room for an angle of 125°. Set the hypotenuse to 1, put the angle at the centre of a circle, and the two ratios become the two coordinates of a point. Drag past 90° and watch which description survives. Like a clock hand: the little triangle only describes the first quarter, the hand keeps going all the way round.

The reciprocal ratios did not go anywhere: cosecant, secant and cotangent are still 1/sin, 1/cos and 1/tan, and the circle is what finally explains their gaps. Secant is undefined at exactly the angles where the point sits on the vertical axis and its x is 0; cosecant and cotangent fail on the horizontal axis, where y is 0. A division by zero, not a special rule to memorise.

2. Radians Or Degrees

A degree is a convention: someone chose 360. A radian is a measurement — how many radii fit along the arc the angle cuts. Change the radius and watch the radian count refuse to move. Like measuring a running track in laps instead of metres: the lap count is the same whether the track is big or small.

Why anyone bothers: a radian is a length divided by a length, so it is a pure number with no unit attached. That is what lets you write the arc as s = rθ with no conversion constant hiding in it — and it is why every formula in the calculus of these functions assumes radians. Feed those formulas degrees and they are wrong by a factor of 180/pi.

3. Special Angle Triangles

The exact values are not a table to memorise — they are two triangles you have used for years, shrunk until the hypotenuse is 1. Pick an angle and watch which leg is short. Like two familiar set squares from a geometry kit: the same two shapes answer every one of these questions.

Read the pattern down the sine column: 0, 1/2, sqrt(2)/2, sqrt(3)/2, 1 — written as sqrt(0)/2, sqrt(1)/2, sqrt(2)/2, sqrt(3)/2, sqrt(4)/2 it is one sequence, and cosine is the same sequence backwards. That is not a coincidence to memorise separately; it is sin(theta) = cos(90° − theta), the complementary-angle rule you already met, seen from the front.

4. The Identity Holds

sin² + cos² = 1 is not a fact about triangles that someone discovered. It is the equation of the circle, x² + y² = 1, with the coordinates renamed. Drag the angle anywhere — any quadrant, any sign — and watch the two squares trade size while their total refuses to budge. Like a fixed budget split between two envelopes: move money across and the total is still the same.

The identity gives you a magnitude, never a sign. Taking the square root of 144/169 allows ±12/13, and the question has to tell you which — usually with the word "acute", which pins the angle to the first quadrant where both coordinates are positive. Drag into quadrant III above and you will see the identity still holding perfectly while both values are negative.

5. Unwrapping The Curves

A graph appears the moment you stop plotting the point and start plotting one of its coordinates against the angle. Walk the point round the circle on the left and the curve on the right draws itself. Like a paint-dipped bicycle valve rolling along a wall: the wheel goes round, the mark it leaves is a wave.

Sine and cosine are the same curve — cosine is sine started a quarter-turn early, which is the first phase shift you will meet and the reason either one can model the same data. Tangent is a different animal: it is a ratio of the two coordinates, so it inherits neither their ceiling nor their period. Asking for the amplitude of tan(x) is a question with no answer.

6. Fit The Wave

Four constants, four independent jobs: how tall, how fast, how late, how high. Move one at a time and read what changed in the summary — then use the data buttons underneath to run it backwards, from a description of something real to the equation that models it. Like the four knobs on an old radio: volume, tuning, timing and balance, each doing exactly one thing.

The order of work when modeling is always the same, and it never starts with the phase shift: midline from the average of max and min, amplitude from half their gap, B from the period, and only then C — because C is measured as a horizontal distance in the units the period already established. A delayed peak is the one thing amplitude, midline and period cannot explain, which is exactly what makes phase shift the answer to "why is the maximum late?".
