**The big idea**: **Solving for a side in a right triangle** and **Solving for an angle in a right triangle** gave you a complete method for exactly one shape, and **Modeling with right triangles** made you bend every application into that shape — dropping a perpendicular somewhere to manufacture the $90^\circ$ corner the ratios demanded. This lesson removes the corner. Two laws extend trigonometry to *any* triangle: the **Law of sines**, which locks each side to the sine of the angle opposite it, and the **Law of cosines**, which is the Pythagorean theorem plus one correction term measuring how far the included angle sits from $90^\circ$. Neither is new machinery — the first falls out of dropping a single altitude and applying **Introduction to the trigonometric ratios** twice, the second out of placing the triangle on the plane of **Unit circle introduction** and collapsing the algebra with **The Pythagorean identity**. What is genuinely new is bookkeeping: with no right angle to anchor you, *the data you were given* decides which law can start, and one configuration fits two different triangles at once.

**Key concepts**

- **The Law of sines is one altitude, read twice.** In triangle $ABC$ drop the altitude $h$ from $C$ onto side $AB$. It splits the figure into two right triangles that share $h$, so the opposite-over-hypotenuse ratio you already own gives $h = b\sin A$ on the left and $h = a\sin B$ on the right. Equate them and divide by $\sin A\sin B$: $\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B}$, and the altitude from $A$ adds $\frac{c}{\sin C}$. **Each side pairs with the sine of the angle across from it, never an adjacent one** — that pairing is the entire content of the law. With $A = 30^\circ$, $a = 5$, $B = 60^\circ$: $b = a\,\frac{\sin B}{\sin A} = 5\cdot\frac{\sqrt3/2}{1/2} = 5\sqrt3 \approx 8.66$.
- **It survives an obtuse angle because of the unit circle.** If $A > 90^\circ$ the foot of the altitude lands outside segment $AB$, and the right triangle you actually get contains $180^\circ - A$. From **Unit circle introduction**, $\sin(180^\circ - A) = \sin A$ — the reflection symmetry you can read straight off **Graphs of sin(x),cos(x), and tan(x)** — so $h = b\sin A$ regardless. The statement needs no case split, which is exactly why it is stated for *any* triangle.
- **Equal angles force equal opposite sides, with no arithmetic.** If $A = B = 45^\circ$ and $a = 8$ then $b = 8\cdot\frac{\sin 45^\circ}{\sin 45^\circ} = 8$: the two ratios are literally the same number, so the sides they carry must match. Read the law as a proportion and a whole class of questions dissolves — scale one side and all three scale with it.
- **AAS and ASA are the Law of sines' home ground.** Two angles hand you the third for free, because the angles of a triangle sum to $180^\circ$: given $50^\circ$ and $60^\circ$, the third is $70^\circ$. Now every angle is known and any single known side fixes the scale of the whole figure through one ratio. Without a side-with-its-opposite-angle pair there is no equation linking a known length to an unknown one at all.
- **The common ratio is the circumdiameter.** $\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R$, where $R$ is the radius of the circle through all three vertices. It explains why the ratio is *constant* rather than merely equal in pairs: all three sides are chords of one circle.
- **SSA is ambiguous, and the mechanism is a compass arc.** Given angle $A$, its adjacent side $b$ and the opposite side $a$, the altitude from $C$ has length $h = b\sin A$. Swing an arc of radius $a$ centred at $C$: if $a < h$ it never reaches the base line and **no** triangle exists; if $a = h$ it touches once, giving a single right triangle; if $h < a < b$ it cuts the base **twice**, so two genuinely different triangles carry the same three measurements; and if $a \ge b$ the second intersection falls on the wrong side of $A$ and only one triangle survives. With $A = 30^\circ$ and $b = 10$ (so $h = 5$): $a = 4$ gives none, $a = 5$ gives one, $a = 7$ gives two ($B \approx 45.6^\circ$ or $134.4^\circ$), $a = 12$ gives one.
- **The ambiguity belongs to the sine function, not to the drawing.** Since $\sin B = \sin(180^\circ - B)$, the equation $\sin B = 0.714$ has two solutions below $180^\circ$, and both may complete a legal triangle. An inverse-sine key returns only the acute one and cannot warn you; you must test the obtuse partner yourself by asking whether $A + (180^\circ - B)$ is still under $180^\circ$.
- **The Law of cosines is Pythagoras plus a correction term.** Put $C$ at the origin with side $a$ along the axis, so $B = (a, 0)$ and $A = (b\cos C,\; b\sin C)$. Then $c^2 = (b\cos C - a)^2 + (b\sin C)^2 = b^2\cos^2 C + b^2\sin^2 C + a^2 - 2ab\cos C$, and **The Pythagorean identity** collapses the first two terms to $b^2$, leaving $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$. Everything hinges on the angle *between* the two known sides.
- **That correction term is a signed dial on the included angle.** Take $a = 3$, $b = 4$. At $C = 90^\circ$, $\cos C = 0$, the term vanishes and $c^2 = 25$, $c = 5$: the Pythagorean theorem is the special case of this law, not a separate rule. At $C = 60^\circ$, $c^2 = 25 - 12 = 13$, $c \approx 3.61$ — a sharper corner pulls the far ends together. At $C = 120^\circ$, $\cos C = -\tfrac12$ flips the term positive and $c^2 = 37$, $c \approx 6.08$. And $a = b = 5$ with $C = 60^\circ$ gives $c^2 = 25 + 25 - 25 = 25$, so $c = 5$ — the equilateral triangle, as it must.
- **Rearranged, it recovers an angle from three sides.** $\cos C = \frac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{2ab}$. With $a = 7$, $b = 8$, $c = 13$: $\cos C = \frac{49+64-169}{112} = -0.5$, so $C = 120^\circ$. Cosine is strictly decreasing across $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$ — one-to-one over precisely the range a triangle angle can occupy — so the sign of the result *tells* you the angle is obtuse and there is no second candidate to check. **That is why you prefer the cosine law whenever you are solving for an angle.**
- **SAS and SSS are the Law of cosines' cases**, because in both there is no side paired with its opposite angle for the sine law to start from. A surveyor who measures two sides of a plot and the angle between them and wants the third side is in the SAS case, and has exactly one natural first move.
- **Solving general triangles and word problems means producing all six measurements.** A general (oblique) triangle is solved when all three sides and all three angles are known, from three given — at least one of which must be a side. Two boundaries of a plot run $120$ m and $150$ m from a corner of $110^\circ$: the third boundary is $\sqrt{120^2+150^2-2(120)(150)\cos 110^\circ} \approx 221.8$ m, the remaining angles follow from either law, and the area is $\tfrac12 bc\sin A = \tfrac12(120)(150)\sin 110^\circ \approx 8457$ m$^2$ from the same three numbers. The work in a word problem is the labelling; once the figure carries letters, the configuration names itself.
- **The longest side faces the largest angle, and it is provable right here.** $\cos A = \frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}$ decreases as $a$ grows, and cosine itself decreases across $0^\circ$–$180^\circ$, so a longer side forces a larger opposite angle. Sides $4, 6, 9$: the angle opposite $9$ is $\cos^{-1}(-29/48) = 127.2^\circ$, opposite $6$ is $32.1^\circ$, opposite $4$ is $20.7^\circ$ — summing to $180^\circ$, order preserved. It costs nothing and catches mis-paired ratios instantly.

**Rules to remember**

- $\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R$ and $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$.
- The angles sum to $180^\circ$ — usually the cheapest first step, and free in AAS and ASA.
- Sine law for AAS and ASA (and for SSA, with care); cosine law for SAS and SSS.
- $\cos 90^\circ = 0$, so the Law of cosines *contains* the Pythagorean theorem.
- $\cos C < 0$ exactly when $C > 90^\circ$, which makes $c$ longer than $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.
- Inverse sine cannot separate $B$ from $180^\circ - B$; inverse cosine has no such gap.
- The longest side lies opposite the largest angle — check it before writing the answer down.
- Three angles determine shape only. Some length must be given or nothing has a size.

**Common pitfalls**

- **Pairing a side with the wrong angle.** $\frac{a}{\sin B}$ belongs to no law. Sides pair with the angle *opposite*, and this is the single most common wrong turn.
- **Accepting inverse sine's answer as the only one.** In an SSA setup always test the obtuse partner $180^\circ - B$; if $A + (180^\circ - B) < 180^\circ$ a second triangle exists, and the question usually wants both.
- **Reaching for the sine law in SAS or SSS.** Neither hands you a side and its opposite angle, so the ratio has no known member to start from. The configuration itself tells you which law is even possible.
- **Finishing with the sine law on the largest angle.** After the cosine law produces a side, use the sine law only for the angle opposite the *shorter* remaining side — that one is guaranteed acute, so the inverse-sine reading is safe.
- **Believing AAA determines a triangle.** Three angles fix the shape and nothing else; every scaled copy fits the data equally well.
- **Rounding intermediates.** Carry full precision and round once at the end. An angle recovered from an already-rounded side drifts by tenths of a degree, and the $180^\circ$ check then fails for no real reason.
- **Skipping the sanity checks.** Angles that miss $180^\circ$, or a longest side not facing the largest angle, mean an error upstream.
- **Assuming the triangle in a word problem has a right angle.** Bearings, plots of land and truss diagrams routinely do not, and these two laws exist precisely so you need not invent one.

**How to approach the questions**

1. Classify the given data first — AAS, ASA, SSA, SAS, SSS, AAA — because the law then chooses itself. A large share of these questions test only that classification.
2. Ask whether any side is paired with its opposite angle. If one is, the sine law can start; if none is, it cannot, and the cosine law must.
3. Watch for **Trig values of special angles (π/6,π/4,π/3)**: at $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ the ratios are exact, and the answer options are usually exact radicals such as $5\sqrt3$ rather than decimals.
4. When two angles are given, subtract from $180^\circ$ before doing anything else.
5. On any SSA phrasing, the expected answer is almost always that two triangles may fit the data — the ambiguity *is* the question.
6. Sanity-check with orderings: larger angle, longer opposite side, and the third side of a SAS triangle must land strictly between $|a-b|$ and $a+b$.
