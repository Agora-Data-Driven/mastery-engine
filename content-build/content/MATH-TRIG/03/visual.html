1. Drop The Altitude

Every ratio you own — opposite over hypotenuse, solving for a side, solving for an angle — needs a 90° corner, and an oblique triangle has none. So build one: the altitude from C cuts the triangle into two right triangles that share that one segment. Drag the angles and watch the two ways of measuring h refuse to disagree. Like measuring a ceiling's height from two different walls: one ceiling, two calculations that must land on the same number.

The altitude is scaffolding: it is erased the moment the law is written down. What survives is the pairing — a side with the sine of the angle across from it — and the fact that the same segment can be measured from either side of the figure. Every question about AAS or ASA is really a question about this one equality.

2. Sides Over Sines

Three separate divisions that always land on the same number. Choose whether your one known side is opposite a known angle (AAS) or wedged between the two (ASA), then drag anything: the three ratios move together and never separate. Like a photograph and its enlargement — every length changes, every proportion survives.

Two angles and any one side is always enough, and the reason is worth saying out loud: the angle sum hands you the third angle, which fixes the shape, and the one length fixes the scale. Three angles alone would fix the shape and leave the scale free — which is exactly why AAA can never be solved, and why every solvable configuration on the next visuals contains a side.

3. The Ambiguous Case

You are handed an angle A, the side b beside it, and the side a across from it — and that is not always enough to name one triangle. Swing the arc of radius a from C and count where it lands on the base: zero, one, or two places. Like a compass on paper: the pencil can reach the line twice, graze it once, or fall short of it entirely.

Nothing here is a quirk of the drawing. sin B = sin(180° − B), so the equation sin B = b·sin A / a has two answers below 180° whenever it has any, and geometry decides whether the obtuse one still leaves room for A. An inverse-sine key hands back only the acute answer and cannot warn you — which is why SSA is the one configuration you check by hand, and why the cosine law, whose inverse has no such twin, is the safer tool for angles.

4. The Cosine Correction

c² = a² + b² is a statement about right triangles. Bend the corner and one term appears to fix it up — and that term is zero at exactly 90°. Drag the included angle and watch the Pythagorean theorem pass by as a single point on the dial. Like a door: the wall-to-handle distance depends entirely on how far it is swung open.

Read the term, do not memorise the formula: −2ab·cos C is a signed correction to the Pythagorean sum. Positive cosine (a sharp corner) pulls the far ends together; zero cosine (a right angle) leaves the theorem you already had; negative cosine (a blunt corner) pushes them apart. Rearranged for the angle it is the one tool that recovers an angle with no ambiguity at all, because cosine is one-to-one across the whole 0°–180° range a triangle angle can occupy.

5. Which Law First

One triangle, six measurements, and six ways a problem might hand you three of them. Pick a configuration and the solve plan rewrites itself with these numbers — including the one setup that cannot be solved at all. Like a locksmith reading which teeth are cut: the key you were given decides which door opens first.

The classification is the whole skill, and it comes down to one question: is any side paired with the angle opposite it? If yes, the Law of sines can start. If no, it cannot, and the Law of cosines must — which is why SAS and SSS belong to it. A word problem is this same decision wearing units: label the figure, name the configuration, then apply the law that fits.
