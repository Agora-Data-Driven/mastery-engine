**The big idea**: **Unit circle introduction** told you where every trigonometric value comes from, **Graphs of sin(x),cos(x), and tan(x)** with **Amplitude, midline, and period of sinusoidal functions** told you those values repeat forever, and **Solving for an angle in a right triangle** let you run the machine backwards from a ratio to an angle. This is where those facts collide. Periodicity is exactly what stops a trigonometric function being invertible: the line $y = 1/2$ crosses the sine curve infinitely often, so "the angle whose sine is $1/2$" names infinitely many angles and is not yet a function. Restrict the domain to a stretch where the curve is one-to-one and you get the inverse trigonometric functions, each returning a single principal value. Everything else falls out of that decision. Because the inverse hands back one angle, solving an equation means putting the missing solutions back by hand, from the curve's symmetry and period. Because the table from **Trig values of special angles (π/6,π/4,π/3)** is short, the angle addition identities extend it to sums and differences. Underneath both sits **The Pythagorean identity**, which turns any one known ratio into all six.

**Key concepts**

- **Inverse trigonometric functions exist only after a domain restriction, and that restriction is the whole idea.** A function can be inverted only when it is one-to-one — every output produced by exactly one input. Sine fails badly: $\sin(\pi/6) = \sin(5\pi/6) = \sin(\pi/6 + 2\pi) = 1/2$. The fix is a convention: keep one branch on which the function moves monotonically through its entire range, discard the rest. $\sin^{-1}$ maps $[-1, 1]$ onto $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$; $\cos^{-1}$ maps $[-1, 1]$ onto $[0, \pi]$; $\tan^{-1}$ maps every real onto $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$. What breaks without it is not accuracy but well-definedness — a rule returning infinitely many outputs is a relation, not a function.
- **So $\sin^{-1}(x)$ means "the angle whose sine is $x$": an angle comes out, never a ratio and never a reciprocal.** Worked: $\sin^{-1}(1/2) = \pi/6$, because $\pi/6 = 30°$ is the one angle in $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ with sine $1/2$; $5\pi/6$ has the same sine and is rejected for sitting outside the principal range. $\cos^{-1}(0) = \pi/2$ — cosine is the $x$-coordinate on the unit circle, zero at the top, and $\pi/2$ lies in $[0, \pi]$ while $3\pi/2$ does not. $\tan^{-1}(1) = \pi/4$, since $\tan = \sin/\cos$ equals $1$ where sine and cosine agree. The notation catches two errors: $\sin^{-1}(x)$ is not $1/\sin(x)$ — that is $\csc(x)$, from **The reciprocal trigonometric ratios (Cosecant, Secant, Cotangent)** — and it is not $(\sin(x))^2$. In code, `numpy.arcsin` returns this principal value in radians.
- **Trigonometric angle addition identities give the ratios of a sum from the ratios of its parts.** $\sin(a+b) = \sin a \cos b + \cos a \sin b$ — sine *mixes* the two functions and keeps the sign. $\cos(a+b) = \cos a \cos b - \sin a \sin b$ — cosine keeps the functions *apart* and flips the sign. $\tan(a+b) = \frac{\tan a + \tan b}{1 - \tan a \tan b}$. The difference versions are free: replace $b$ with $-b$, using $\cos(-b) = \cos b$ and $\sin(-b) = -\sin b$, which flips exactly one sign in each. Why they are needed at all: these functions are not linear, so $\sin(a+b) \neq \sin a + \sin b$ — at $a = b = \pi/2$ the left side is $\sin(\pi) = 0$ and the right side is $2$.
- **What the addition identities are for: exact values at angles off the special list.** Worked: $\cos(15°) = \cos(45° - 30°) = \cos 45° \cos 30° + \sin 45° \sin 30° = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4} \approx 0.9659$. Worked: $\sin(75°) = \sin(45° + 30°) = \sin 45° \cos 30° + \cos 45° \sin 30° = \frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4}$ — the identical number, which is **Sine and cosine of complementary angles** reappearing, since $\sin(75°) = \cos(15°)$. Its sibling $\frac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4} \approx 0.2588$ is $\cos(75°)$, and swapping the two is the standard distractor. A calculator returns a decimal; the identity returns the exact surd.
- **Using trigonometric identities to solve problems begins with knowing what an identity is.** An identity is an equation true for *every* value of the variable in its domain — not a numerical approximation, and not an equation holding at one angle. That universality licenses substitution: if two expressions agree everywhere, either may replace the other anywhere. So the opening move on any simplification is **rewrite in sine and cosine, or recognise a known identity**. Worked: $\frac{1 - \sin^2(x)}{\cos(x)}$. **The Pythagorean identity** rearranges to $1 - \sin^2 x = \cos^2 x$, so the expression is $\frac{\cos^2 x}{\cos x} = \cos x$ wherever $\cos x \neq 0$. Worked: $\frac{\sin(x)}{\cos(x)} = \tan(x)$ by definition; inverted, $\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}$ is $\cot(x)$ — the distractor placed beside it.
- **The Pythagorean family turns one given ratio into all six.** Divide $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$ by $\cos^2 x$ for $1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x$, or by $\sin^2 x$ for $1 + \cot^2 x = \csc^2 x$. Worked: if $\sec(x) = 5/4$ and $x$ is acute, then $\cos x = 4/5$, since secant is the reciprocal of cosine. So $\sin^2 x = 1 - \frac{16}{25} = \frac{9}{25}$, and acuteness makes $\sin x = 3/5$, hence $\tan x = \frac{3/5}{4/5} = \frac{3}{4}$. The right-triangle reading is the same arithmetic: adjacent 4, hypotenuse 5, opposite 3. Every wrong option is a genuine ratio of that triangle in the wrong slot — $5/3$ is $\csc x$, $4/3$ is $\cot x$, $3/5$ is $\sin x$ — so name the ratio you want before computing.
- **Solving sinusoidal equations: the inverse gives one solution, symmetry and period give the rest.** The fundamental period of $\sin(x)$ and $\cos(x)$ is $2\pi$, so if $x$ solves the equation so does $x + 2\pi k$ for every integer $k$ (tangent repeats after $\pi$). Inside one period, a horizontal line strictly between the maximum and minimum cuts the curve **twice** — the curve hits the same $y$-value at two $x$-values per cycle. Procedure: invert once, then reflect. Sine is symmetric about $x = \pi/2$, so its partner is $\pi - x_1$; cosine is symmetric about $x = 0$, so its partner is $2\pi - x_1$. Worked: $\sin(x) = 1/2$ on $0 \le x < 2\pi$ gives $\pi/6$ and $\pi - \pi/6 = 5\pi/6$. The tempting $11\pi/6$ is where $\sin x = -1/2$.
- **Count solutions from the picture, because "two per cycle" fails exactly where questions are set.** Worked: $\sin(x) = 0$ on $0 \le x < 2\pi$ gives $x = 0$ and $x = \pi$ — not $2\pi$, which the half-open interval excludes even though $\sin(2\pi) = 0$. Worked: $\cos(x) = 1$ gives $x = 0$ alone, because at the peak the line is tangent and the two crossings have merged. And a compressed argument multiplies the count: in $\sin(2x) = 1/2$ the argument sweeps $0$ to $4\pi$, giving four solutions. Solve for the whole argument first, then unwind to $x$ — exactly what **Transforming and graphing sinusoidal functions** and **Modeling with sinusoidal functions (including phase shift)** trained you to do.

**Rules to remember**

- The inverses return **angles**, with principal ranges $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$, $[0, \pi]$ and $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ for $\sin^{-1}$, $\cos^{-1}$, $\tan^{-1}$. Domains are restricted for one reason: to make each function one-to-one so an inverse exists.
- $\sin^{-1}(x) \neq 1/\sin(x)$. The reciprocals have their own names: $\csc$, $\sec$, $\cot$.
- $\sin(a \pm b) = \sin a \cos b \pm \cos a \sin b$ (sign follows); $\cos(a \pm b) = \cos a \cos b \mp \sin a \sin b$ (sign flips). Nothing here distributes: there is no rule $f(a+b) = f(a) + f(b)$.
- An identity holds for every legal value; one agreeing pair of numbers proves nothing.
- $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$, $1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x$, $1 + \cot^2 x = \csc^2 x$, $\tan x = \sin x / \cos x$, $\cot x = \cos x / \sin x$.
- First move when simplifying: rewrite in sine and cosine, or apply a known identity.
- Fundamental period of $\sin$ and $\cos$ is $2\pi$; of $\tan$, $\pi$. $\sin(Bx)$ runs $|B|$ cycles across $[0, 2\pi)$, so expect $|B|$ times as many solutions there.
- Sine's partner solution is $\pi - x_1$, cosine's is $2\pi - x_1$; add $2\pi k$ for the general solution.

**Common pitfalls**

- Reading $\sin^{-1}(x)$ as $1/\sin(x)$, or as $\sin(x)$ squared. It is an angle; the superscript is notation, not an exponent.
- Answering $\sin^{-1}(1/2)$ with $5\pi/6$: a true sine value, wrong branch. Or answering $\cos^{-1}(0)$ with $0$, reading the input back out instead of the angle.
- Believing the restriction exists to keep outputs positive. $\tan^{-1}$ returns negative angles constantly.
- Writing $\sin(a+b) = \sin a + \sin b$ or $\tan(a+b) = \tan a + \tan b$. Both fail everywhere except where one angle is $0$.
- Writing $\cos(a+b) = \cos a \cos b + \sin a \sin b$: right ingredients, wrong sign — that expression is $\cos(a-b)$, which is why it convinces. Or interchanging $\frac{\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}}{4}$ (about $0.97$) with $\frac{\sqrt{6}-\sqrt{2}}{4}$ (about $0.26$).
- Testing an identity at one convenient angle and declaring it proved. $\tan x$ and $\cot x$ agree at $x = \pi/4$ and are nowhere near equal.
- Cancelling $\cos x$ without noticing the simplification is invalid where $\cos x = 0$.
- Keeping only the calculator's answer and losing the second solution, or applying $2\pi - x_1$ to a sine equation and $\pi - x_1$ to a cosine one.
- Including $2\pi$ in a solution set on $0 \le x < 2\pi$, or claiming two solutions when the line sits at the peak.

**How to approach the questions**

1. For any inverse evaluation, say the sentence in full — "the angle in [principal range] whose [function] is [value]" — then check your candidate lies in that range; most distractors are the right value from the wrong branch. If asked *why* domains are restricted, the answer is always one-to-one-ness.
2. For an exact value, split the angle into two you know: $15 = 45 - 30$, $75 = 45 + 30$, $105 = 60 + 45$. Write the identity first, substitute the special values second.
3. Say the sign rule before substituting: sine keeps the sign, cosine flips it. Cosine's wrong-sign version is a real identity for the *other* operation, so it never looks wrong.
4. To test a claimed identity, use an awkward angle such as $x = \pi/3$, not a symmetric one. Agreement at one point is a coincidence; disagreement at one point is a disproof.
5. When simplifying, rewrite in sine and cosine and hunt for $1 - \sin^2$, $1 - \cos^2$ or $1 + \tan^2$: all three are the Pythagorean identity in disguise.
6. For an equation, reach the form (trig function of an argument) = (number), solve for the *argument* across its own sweep, unwind to $x$, keep only what lies in the stated interval. Sketch the curve and the line before answering "how many".

**Where this leads**: this is the final lesson of Trigonometry — the spec lists nothing after it, because these four moves are what the rest of your mathematics is built on. **Precalculus** revisits the ground in **02 Trigonometry**, then spends it in **03 Complex numbers**, where multiplying in polar form *adds the arguments* — that is the pair of angle addition identities above, read as one statement about rotation. **Calculus** already treats these identities as owned material in **01 Limits and continuity**: $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ with the addition identity for $\sin(x + h)$ is exactly what makes the derivative of $\sin x$ come out as $\cos x$. Closer to your own work, every seasonality feature you have built is a sinusoid with a fitted amplitude and phase, and reading that phase back out is this lesson's inverse question — principal value and all.
