1. Restrict Then Invert

Graphs of sin(x), cos(x), and tan(x) and Unit circle introduction told you these curves repeat forever. That is exactly what stops them having an inverse. Pick a function, drag the value, and watch how many angles produce it — then watch the shaded branch keep exactly one. Like a clock face: 3 o'clock today, 3 o'clock tomorrow and 15:00 are the same hand position, so any answer has to say in advance which one it means.

keep one branch only one value -> exactly one angle so the inverse exists

The three ranges are a convention, not a discovery — but every library obeys the same one, which is why a second function exists for angles of points rather than of ratios. The principal value of tan inverse can never tell you the third quadrant from the first, because both give the same ratio; that is what atan2, with two arguments and a full turn of output, is for.

2. Splitting An Angle

The special-angle table stops at 30°, 45° and 60°. The addition identities build every sum and difference of them exactly. Pick two angles, then read the fake rules underneath — each one is checked at your pair and swept across all 25 pairs, because that is what "identity" means. Like walking two blocks north then two blocks east: how far you end up from home is not two plus two.

These two are the source of nearly every other identity in the chapter, so they are worth owning rather than memorising. Put b = a and the sine rule becomes the double-angle formula sin(2a) = 2 sin a cos a; the tangent version is not a fourth fact either — divide the sine identity by the cosine identity, then divide top and bottom by cos a cos b, and tan(a + b) = (tan a + tan b) / (1 − tan a tan b) falls out.

3. True Everywhere Or Once

An identity is an equation true for every legal value — which is why one agreeing pair of numbers proves nothing. Pick a claim, drag x, and compare what happens at your point with the verdict from sweeping the whole interval. Like the claim that every swan is white: a thousand white swans prove nothing, and one black swan settles it.

Notice what the verdict is computed from: 720 sampled values of x, not the one you are standing on. That gap is the whole difference between testing a rule and believing it. And it is why the first move on any simplification is rewrite in sine and cosine — once both sides are made of the same two functions, the Pythagorean identity either collapses the expression or visibly fails to.

4. One Ratio Given

Hand over a single ratio for an acute angle and every other ratio is already decided — the Pythagorean identity does the deciding. Drag the adjacent side and watch all six move together, with the exact fractions appearing whenever the triangle is a whole-number one. Like a recipe scaled by one ingredient: fix the flour and every other quantity is settled.

The word acute in these questions is not decoration — it is what fixes the sign. The Pythagorean identity gives sin²x = 9/25, and that has two square roots; only the promise that x lies in the first quadrant picks +3/5 and therefore tan x = +3/4. Move the same cosine into the second quadrant and sine stays positive while tangent flips; into the fourth, and both flip back.

5. Two Hits Per Cycle

The inverse hands back one angle. The equation wants all of them in the interval. Drag the level line and watch the solution set rebuild itself — including at the peak, where the famous two solutions collapse to one. Like a Ferris wheel passing your eye level twice a turn: once on the way up, once on the way down.

Every modelling question in the course ends here. A tide, a temperature curve or a seasonality feature is a sinusoid with an amplitude, a midline and a phase shift, and "when does it next reach 3 m" is this panel with different labels: solve for the whole argument, unwind it, then pick the solutions that lie in the window the question actually asked about.
